Buying Property in Dubai as an American + Golden Visa: 2026 Guide

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated September 2026  ·  14 min read
Yes — Americans can fully own property in Dubai, and it's one of the simplest markets in the world to buy in. US citizens can hold freehold title in 60+ designated areas with no residency required. Dubai charges zero property, income, and capital-gains tax on individuals, and a purchase of AED 2 million (~US$545K) earns a 10-year Golden Visa. The catch for Americans: the US still taxes your worldwide income, usually with no offsetting credit.

While Europe tightens its residency-by-investment programs, Dubai has gone the other way — actively courting foreign buyers with full ownership, no tax, and one of the most generous property-to-residency deals available anywhere. For Americans wanting a high-yield, tax-light base in a fast-growing global hub, the UAE is now one of the strongest options on the board. Dubai's market also moves faster and with less procedural friction than most of the destinations in this site's guides — no notary-driven civil-law process, no residency prerequisite, and a title-registration system that can close in weeks rather than months. That speed is an advantage, but it also means the buyer, not a notary or a mandatory legal-review step, carries most of the burden of getting the freehold status, the escrow protections and the visa mechanics right before money moves. Here's the 2026 reality, including the fine print most guides aimed at Americans skip.

Can Americans own property in Dubai?

Yes — fully and outright. Citizens of the US and every other country can buy freehold property in Dubai's designated zones, with the same ownership rights a local would have: you own the property and the land it sits on, in perpetuity. You do not need to be a UAE resident; a valid US passport is enough to complete the purchase, even remotely.

Freehold areas & how ownership works

Foreign freehold ownership in Dubai rests on Regulation No. 3 of 2006, issued by the Ruler of Dubai, which originally named 23 specific areas — including Emirates Hills, Dubai Marina, Palm Jumeirah and Jebel Ali — where non-UAE nationals could hold full title. The Dubai Land Department (DLD) has repeatedly expanded that list with further resolutions since, and the freehold map today is commonly cited at 60+ communities, adding areas such as Downtown Dubai, Business Bay, Dubai Hills Estate and Jumeirah Village Circle (JVC) — though no single DLD page currently publishes one consolidated, dated count, so always confirm a specific plot's freehold status with the DLD or your lawyer rather than relying on a listing site.

Outside freehold zones, foreigners can typically only buy leasehold (a long-term lease, commonly up to 99 years) rather than full title — so confirming a property is genuinely freehold is the first due-diligence step. Ownership is registered with the DLD, which issues the title deed (and, for jointly-owned buildings, administers the owners'-association rules through its Mollak system).

Buying off-plan: Oqood & the escrow law

A large share of Dubai's market is off-plan — buying a unit before, or during, construction, usually at a discount to completed stock and on a staged payment plan. Two protections matter here, and both are worth checking before you sign:

  • Oqood registration. Every off-plan sale must be registered with the DLD's Oqood system, which creates an interim record of your interest in the unit ahead of the final title deed. An unregistered off-plan "reservation" is not a protected legal interest — insist on Oqood registration as part of the sale, not after.
  • The escrow law. Under Law No. 8 of 2007, every off-plan project must have its own dedicated escrow account, opened in the project's name with a DLD-registered escrow agent, and used only to fund that project's construction — a developer cannot commingle buyer deposits across projects or divert them to other uses. The Department maintains a public Register of Real Estate Developers and a Register of Escrow Agents; before paying a deposit, confirm the developer and the specific project both appear on these registers, and ask for the escrow agreement reference. Buyers also have a statutory right to request the project's accounting records.

These two checks — Oqood registration and a verified escrow account — are the practical answer to Dubai's well-known history of stalled off-plan projects; skipping them is where most off-plan disputes start.

The zero-tax advantage — and the US catch

This is Dubai's headline draw: zero property tax, zero income tax, and zero capital gains tax for individuals. There is no annual property tax bill. The UAE's federal 9% corporate tax, introduced in 2023, also does not reach an individual simply earning rental income or a capital gain from personally-held real estate investment — it targets licensed businesses, not personal property holdings, so buying and renting out a Dubai apartment in your own name does not itself create a corporate-tax obligation. The main ongoing cost instead is service charges (building and community fees), covered below.

But here is the nuance most guides aimed at Americans skip: UAE zero-tax does not free a US citizen from US tax. The United States taxes citizens on worldwide income regardless of where they live. So rental income from your Dubai apartment is reportable on your US return (Schedule E), and a future sale is a US capital gain. Because the UAE charges no comparable tax, there is generally no foreign tax credit to offset either one — meaning the income and gain can be fully US-taxable, with no double-tax relief to fall back on. A UAE bank account (often needed for service charges and rent collection) can also trigger FBAR and FATCA reporting. Plan this with a cross-border CPA before you buy, not after your first US return is due.

The Golden Visa through property

Unlike Portugal, which scrapped its property route, the UAE rewards property buyers with residency. The tiers in 2026:

Property valueResidency
AED 2,000,000+ (~US$545K)10-year Golden Visa
Any completed freehold unit (sole owner; reportedly no minimum since 2026)2-year investor/property visa; joint owners each need AED 400,000

The AED 2M threshold can be met with a single property or several combined, and there is no local sponsor or employer required — your family can be included, and the residency stays valid even if you spend more than six months a year outside the UAE, making it a true flexible base. Eligibility is certified via a property status certificate from the DLD, which assesses the purchase price on the title deed rather than a later market valuation.

A mortgaged property can still count toward the AED 2M threshold, though in practice the paid-up equity and lender's no-objection paperwork are part of what the DLD and immigration authority (ICP) review when certifying eligibility — several UAE law firms describe a 2026 change simplifying this from a fixed "50% paid" rule to a valuation-based check, but no official DLD or ICP circular stating the exact mechanism or date was located this session, so treat the details as provisional until your lawyer confirms the current requirement against your specific lender and property.

Mortgages for non-residents

Financing is available to non-UAE-residents, and the binding cap comes from the UAE Central Bank, not from residency status:

Property priceMax. loan-to-value (LTV), expatriate first purchaseMin. down payment
Under AED 5,000,00080%20%
AED 5,000,000 and above70%30%

Those caps, set by the Central Bank of the UAE's mortgage-loan regulations, apply to expatriates generally rather than splitting UAE residents from overseas non-residents. In practice, though, individual banks routinely apply tighter, commercially-set limits to buyers who live abroad and lack UAE income or credit history — a larger deposit (often 40–50%) and a narrower panel of lenders are common for genuinely overseas buyers, even though no regulation mandates that specific split. Rates are variable, priced off EIBOR plus a bank margin, and move with each bank's own sheet rather than a single published government rate. Many international buyers pay cash instead, both for speed and because the realistic non-resident deposit narrows the leverage advantage. If you finance, a mortgaged property still counts toward the Golden Visa threshold based on its total value (see above).

Purchase costs

Dubai's transaction costs are moderate and transparent. Budget for:

  • DLD transfer fee: 4% of the purchase price (the main cost), usually paid by the buyer.
  • Agency fee: typically 2% + VAT.
  • Registration and admin fees (trustee fee): AED 4,000 + 5% VAT for properties priced at AED 500,000 and above; AED 2,000 + 5% VAT below that threshold. Paid to the DLD-licensed trustee office that processes the transfer.
  • Mortgage registration (if financing): 0.25% of the loan.

Service charges, DEWA & running costs

Once you own, the main recurring cost is the building's service charge — the community/owners'-association fee that covers common-area maintenance, security, insurance and (in many towers) district cooling infrastructure. These are set per project and vary widely by building and amenities; market listings commonly quote a rough range of AED 10–50 per square foot per year, but that range is not itself published by RERA as a single figure. Instead, the DLD runs an official Service Charge Index (accessible via the DLD website, the Dubai REST app, or the Mollak system) where a buyer can look up the RERA-approved service charge for a specific project and year before committing — always check the actual building, not a market average.

A separate line item is DEWA (Dubai Electricity and Water Authority) — the utility connection for electricity and water — plus, in towers on a district cooling network, a chiller fee billed by the cooling provider (not DEWA) for air-conditioning supplied centrally to the building. Chiller charges can be a meaningful share of a Dubai household's utility bill in summer and are set by the district-cooling operator rather than a government tariff, so ask for the specific building's cooling provider and recent bills before buying, rather than relying on a citywide estimate.

Rental yields

Dubai is frequently cited as one of the higher-yielding major global property markets, with real-estate portals commonly reporting gross rental yields roughly in the 5%–8% range, varying by area, property type and whether a unit is let long-term or as a licensed short-let. Portal data commonly puts higher-turnover, smaller-unit communities such as JVC, Dubai Sports City and International City toward the upper end of that range, and larger, higher-priced flagship addresses such as Downtown Dubai and Palm Jumeirah toward the lower end — the trade-off being capital appreciation and liquidity on exit rather than running yield. These figures come from private real-estate data providers and portals rather than a single DLD-published yield series, and they move with the market, so treat any specific area's number as indicative rather than guaranteed, and verify current asking rents and prices directly — ideally against actual signed leases in the building, not just listing prices — before underwriting a purchase.

Inheritance: wills vs. the Sharia default

If a non-Muslim foreign owner dies without a will, UAE personal-status law can default the distribution of their UAE estate — including Dubai real estate — toward Sharia-based forced-heirship rules, which may not match what a US owner would want or what their US will already provides for. The standard fix for expatriates is to register a separate will with the DIFC Wills Service Centre (run through the DIFC Courts), which lets a non-Muslim foreign owner direct how their UAE assets, including real property, are distributed, largely on their own terms, and is designed to run alongside — not replace — a US will covering the rest of the estate. This is a step worth taking at the time of purchase, not left for later, given how it interacts with the property's title registration.

Step-by-step: buying in Dubai as an American

  1. Confirm the area is freehold and the property is eligible for foreign ownership.
  2. Engage an independent buyer's lawyer (separate from the agent and developer).
  3. Sign the MOU (Form F) and pay a deposit, typically 10%.
  4. Obtain a No Objection Certificate (NOC) from the developer.
  5. Complete the transfer at the DLD, pay the 4% fee, and receive the title deed (or, for off-plan, confirm Oqood registration and the project's escrow account).
  6. Apply for the Golden Visa (if AED 2M+) using the DLD property status certificate.
  7. Register a DIFC will covering the UAE asset, and set up US tax compliance — Schedule E, FBAR — before year-end.

Is buying property in Dubai worth it in 2026?

For Americans, the appeal is rare: full freehold ownership, strong rental yields, no local tax, and a flexible 10-year residency — in a hub between Europe, Asia and Africa. The discipline required is on the US side: the IRS still wants its share, and without a foreign tax credit the planning matters more, not less. Buy in a true freehold area, use independent legal counsel, verify escrow and service-charge details before you commit, register a DIFC will, and line up your US tax plan first — and Dubai is one of the most buyer-friendly markets an American can enter.

Can you short-let it? The holiday-home permit

Dubai's rental yields are real, but short-term letting is licensed, not automatic. To let a unit on a nightly basis you need a holiday-home permit from Dubai's Department of Economy and Tourism — issued per unit, renewed annually, and requiring the building or owners' association to permit short-term use. Tourism fees per occupied night apply, and operating an unpermitted holiday home carries fines. Many towers and communities simply prohibit it in their bylaws, so confirm the building's position, not just the city's. See our guide to short-term rental rules abroad.

Frequently asked questions

Can Americans buy property in Dubai?

Yes. US citizens can fully own freehold property in Dubai's designated freehold areas (originally listed in Regulation No. 3 of 2006 and repeatedly expanded since, commonly cited today at 60+ communities), with full ownership rights, and you do not need to be a UAE resident — a valid US passport is enough.

Does buying property get you a Golden Visa?

Yes. A purchase of AED 2 million or more (~US$545K) qualifies for a 10-year Golden Visa; a sole owner of any completed freehold property reportedly now qualifies for a 2-year investor visa with no minimum value, though no official DLD circular states the exact date this changed; joint owners each need AED 400,000.

Is there property tax in Dubai?

No. Dubai has zero property, income and capital-gains tax for individuals, and the UAE's 9% corporate tax does not apply to an individual's personal real-estate investment. Buyers pay a one-time 4% DLD transfer fee plus annual service charges that vary by building (checkable via the RERA Service Charge Index). US citizens still owe US tax on worldwide income.

Can a US citizen get a mortgage in Dubai?

Yes. UAE Central Bank rules cap expatriate first-purchase financing at 80% loan-to-value under AED 5 million and 70% above that, based on property price rather than UAE residency — though banks often ask overseas non-resident buyers for a larger deposit as internal policy. Rates are variable, priced off EIBOR plus a bank margin.

How is off-plan buyer money protected in Dubai?

By Law No. 8 of 2007, which requires each off-plan project to hold its own dedicated escrow account with a DLD-registered escrow agent, used only for that project's construction. Confirm the developer and project are on the DLD's public registers, and that your unit is Oqood-registered, before paying a deposit.

What happens to my Dubai property if I die without a will?

UAE personal-status law can default a non-Muslim foreigner's estate, including Dubai real estate, toward Sharia-based forced-heirship rules. Most expatriate owners instead register a will with the DIFC Wills Service Centre to direct their UAE assets on their own terms, alongside their US will.

Do I pay US tax on a Dubai property?

Potentially yes. Despite the UAE's zero tax, US citizens are taxed on worldwide income: rental income (Schedule E) and resale gains are US-reportable, generally with no foreign tax credit to offset them since the UAE charges no equivalent tax, and a UAE bank account can trigger FBAR/FATCA.

Can I short-let my Dubai property?

Only with a holiday-home permit from Dubai's Department of Economy and Tourism, issued per unit and requiring the building or owners' association to allow short-term use — many towers prohibit it in their bylaws regardless of the citywide rule.
