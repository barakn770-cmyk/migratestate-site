Can Americans Get a Mortgage in Mexico? (2026)

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated September 2026  ·  9 min read
Yes — Americans can finance Mexican property, even as non-residents. Your three real options are a Mexican bank mortgage (best for residents), a cross-border USD loan (built for US buyers), or developer financing. Expect 9%–14% on peso loans, 5%–9% on USD cross-border loans, and a 35%–50% down payment as a non-resident. The biggest decision isn't the rate — it's the currency.

The myth that you must pay all cash for a home in Mexico is outdated. Financing exists for Americans in 2026 — through Mexican banks, a growing class of cross-border lenders, and developers — but the terms, the paperwork and above all the currency look nothing like a US mortgage. Picking the wrong structure can quietly cost you more than a couple of points of interest. Here's how to do it right.

Can Americans actually borrow in Mexico?

Yes. Foreign nationals are legally allowed to take a mortgage in Mexico, and both residents and non-residents can qualify depending on the lender and your financial profile. Access scales with your immigration status: a permanent resident (Residente Permanente) gets the best terms from Mexican banks; a temporary resident qualifies with select lenders; and a non-resident typically turns to specialized cross-border lenders or developer financing. Lenders will want US tax returns, bank statements, proof of income and a strong credit history — and they'll ask for more of it than a US bank would.

Two administrative details trip up first-time American borrowers before the loan paperwork even starts. First, a Mexican bank mortgage almost always requires you to hold an RFC (Registro Federal de Contribuyentes, Mexico's tax ID) and a Mexican bank account, since the lender disburses and collects payments locally — get the RFC early, it is also needed to pay property taxes and utilities. Second, the sector is supervised by CONDUSEF (Comisión Nacional para la Protección y Defensa de los Usuarios de Servicios Financieros), Mexico's financial-consumer protection regulator. CONDUSEF publishes a free comparison tool (Comparador de Créditos Hipotecarios) covering every regulated bank's published rate, CAT (total annual cost, Mexico's all-in APR-equivalent) and fees, and it is where you file a complaint if a lender misrepresents terms. Cross-border USD lenders and developers are not always regulated the same way a Mexican bank is, so CONDUSEF's comparison is most useful as a benchmark against a Mexican bank quote, not as a complaint channel for a US-based cross-border lender.

For most American buyers without Mexican residency, the realistic choice is a cross-border USD loan (predictable for a dollar earner) or developer financing on a new build (often easier to qualify for, but shorter terms and higher effective cost).

Each route suits a different buyer. Mexican bank mortgages (BBVA México, Santander México, Scotiabank México and similar) offer the lowest peso rates but generally require a permanent or temporary resident visa, an RFC, a Mexican bank account and local credit history — realistically out of reach for a non-resident American on a first purchase. Cross-border lenders (US-licensed firms that underwrite against US income and lend in USD, secured by the Mexican property or, in the restricted zone, by the fideicomiso) are built specifically for this gap and are the most common route for a non-resident American. Developer financing is offered directly by the builder on new construction or pre-construction, usually over 3–7 years at a rate the developer sets (often close to or above cross-border USD rates), with the balance due as a balloon payment or a refinance into a bank/cross-border loan at completion — read the balloon terms closely. Seller financing is a private, negotiated arrangement on a resale, rare but sometimes available when a seller wants to spread out a sale for their own tax reasons.

A fifth option many Americans overlook isn't a Mexican loan at all: tapping home equity in the US — a HELOC or cash-out refinance on your US home — to pay cash in Mexico. This sidesteps Mexican underwriting, RFC requirements and Section 988 currency risk entirely (see below), at the cost of putting your US home up as collateral instead of the Mexican property. US home-equity lines are typically priced off the Federal Reserve's Bank Prime Loan rate (7.00% as of September 2026) plus a lender-set margin, commonly landing in the high-single digits — a rate quote from your US lender, not a Mexican one, so it isn't subject to Banxico's policy rate or peso currency risk.

Rates, LTV and down payments

Mexican financing is more expensive and more conservative than a US mortgage. In early 2026:

TermPeso (Mexican bank)USD (cross-border)
Interest rate~9%–14%~5%–9%
Max LTV70%–80% (residents)60%–70%
Typical non-resident down payment35%–50%

Some fintech lenders stretch to 90% LTV for the very strongest profiles, but as a non-resident you should budget a 35%–50% deposit and stricter income documentation. Add closing costs of roughly 5%–8% on top.

Peso rates track Mexico's own monetary policy, not the Fed's. Banxico's overnight target rate stood at 6.50% as of August 2026 — the reference point Mexican banks price their variable and fixed mortgage rates against, which is why peso mortgages carry a premium over USD loans priced off the (lower) US rate environment. When comparing quotes, ask for the CAT (Costo Anual Total) rather than the headline rate: it's Mexico's regulator-mandated all-in cost figure, bundling interest, mandatory insurance and fees into one comparable number, the way an APR does in the US. CONDUSEF's free online comparator lists the CAT for every regulated bank's mortgage product.

Financing in the restricted (coastal) zone

If your dream home is on the beach or near a border — Tulum, Cabo, Puerto Vallarta — it sits in Mexico's restricted zone, where a foreigner holds title through a bank trust (fideicomiso). This does not block financing: lenders routinely mortgage trust-held property, and the bank that holds your fideicomiso is often involved. Just budget the trust's setup fee and annual fee on top of your loan costs. Full mechanics are in our Mexico property guide.

Mechanically, the fideicomiso is what the lender actually takes security over in the restricted zone: instead of a direct mortgage lien on land a foreigner cannot hold title to outright, the trustee bank holds legal title for your benefit, and the lender's mortgage attaches to your beneficial rights under that trust. This is a well-established structure — Mexican banks and cross-border lenders both underwrite trust-held property routinely — but it adds a second institution (the trustee bank) to the closing, a trust setup fee, and an annual trustee fee that continues for the life of your ownership, financed or not. If your lender is a different bank than your trustee, expect an extra layer of paperwork (a trustee consent or subordination) before the mortgage can be registered.

Closing timeline

A cash purchase in Mexico typically closes in 4–8 weeks from signed offer to deed, driven mainly by the notario's title search, tax-clearance certificates and (in the restricted zone) the trust-formation paperwork. Adding financing extends that: a Mexican bank mortgage typically adds 4–8 more weeks for underwriting and the property appraisal (avalúo), and a cross-border USD loan runs on a broadly similar timeline since the appraisal, title work and trust formation (if applicable) still have to happen in Mexico regardless of which country the money comes from. Developer financing on a pre-construction unit is different in kind rather than degree — the "closing" is really the signing of a purchase-sale agreement against a construction schedule, so the relevant timeline is the build schedule, not a mortgage underwriting period. Budget more time, not less, if you're financing and buying in the restricted zone at the same time.

Can you pay off a Mexican mortgage early?

Usually yes, but confirm it in writing before you sign. Mexican consumer-finance law gives borrowers a general right to prepay many types of retail credit without penalty, but that protection is not automatic for mortgage-secured loans the way it is for some other consumer credit, so the prepayment terms on a specific Mexican bank mortgage or cross-border loan are set in the contract itself, not guaranteed by statute. In practice, most major Mexican banks advertise no-penalty prepayment on standard mortgage products, and CONDUSEF's contract-clause database lets you check a specific bank's published terms before signing. Developer-financing contracts vary the most here — some allow early payoff freely, others charge a fee or require the balance to be paid on the original schedule regardless. Read the prepayment clause in whichever contract you sign; don't assume it matches what the bank down the street offers.

Peso vs USD — the decision that matters

This is where most buyers focus on the wrong number. A peso mortgage at 12% versus a USD loan at 7% looks like a simple rate comparison — but the currency you borrow in is a second bet layered on the property. Borrow in pesos and you owe in pesos: if the peso weakens against your dollar income, your effective cost falls, but if it strengthens, your dollar cost rises unpredictably. Borrow in USD and your dollar income matches your dollar debt — no FX risk, usually a lower rate, but a smaller pool of lenders. For a US earner buying a home to use (not a peso-income rental), a USD cross-border loan is usually the cleaner structure.

The US tax angle (and the currency trap)

Two US tax points every American borrower should know. First, the good news: mortgage interest on a foreign home can be deductible on your US return, within the usual qualified-residence limits, just like a US second home (the fideicomiso fees are not). Concretely, IRS Publication 936 caps deductible home acquisition debt at $750,000 ($375,000 married filing separately) for debt taken on after 15 December 2017 — and that cap is shared across your combined main home and one second home, not doubled for a Mexican property on top of a US one. Publication 936 defines a qualified home by what it is (a house, apartment, condo, or similar dwelling with sleeping, cooking and toilet facilities, used as a main or second home), not by which country it's in, so a Mexican home you personally use can qualify on the same terms as a US one — but confirm the treatment with a cross-border CPA, since the IRS guidance is written with domestic property in mind and doesn't address the foreign case explicitly.

Second, the trap almost nobody flags: IRS foreign-currency rules (Section 988). If you take a peso mortgage and later pay it off (or refinance) when the peso has weakened, the IRS can treat the difference as a taxable foreign-currency gain — you "saved" dollars repaying a cheaper-peso debt, and that saving is taxable income, even though you merely repaid a loan. The reverse doesn't offset it the way you'd expect: if the peso strengthens instead and repaying costs you more dollars, Section 988 treats that as a currency loss on personal-purpose debt, and losses on personal transactions are generally not deductible at all — so the risk is asymmetric, a possible taxable gain against no offsetting loss, which is exactly why it catches peso borrowers off guard at payoff. It's the single strongest reason a dollar-earning American often prefers a USD loan: it sidesteps the Section 988 problem entirely, since there's no peso principal to have moved. Model it with a cross-border CPA before you sign.

Step-by-step to a Mexican mortgage

  1. Confirm your status — permanent resident, temporary resident, or non-resident — which decides your lender pool.
  2. Get pre-qualified with a cross-border lender or Mexican bank; gather US tax returns, pay/income proof and bank statements.
  3. Choose your currency — USD for a dollar earner avoiding FX risk; pesos only with eyes open.
  4. Budget the deposit and costs — 35%–50% down as a non-resident, plus 5%–8% closing and (coastal) fideicomiso fees.
  5. Engage an independent Mexican lawyer for title, the trust (if coastal), and the loan documents.
  6. Order the appraisal (avalúo) and finalize the loan; the notario (notary) formalizes the deed and mortgage.
  7. Set up US compliance — FBAR for any Mexican account, and confirm interest deductibility and currency treatment with your CPA.

If you're not American: what changes

The Mexican lending side is identical for any foreign buyer. The lender routes, rates, LTV, down payments and the fideicomiso all apply the same whatever passport you hold. What changes is only the home-country tax treatment of your mortgage interest and any currency gain.

If you're not American, the Section 988 currency-gain trap and the US mortgage-interest deduction above are US-specific — they won't apply to you. The currency risk of a peso vs USD loan is real for everyone, but the tax consequences differ. Check three things in your country of residence: (1) whether mortgage interest on a foreign property is deductible at home; (2) whether your country taxes foreign-currency gains on debt repayment; and (3) whether you should borrow in your home currency, USD, or pesos to match your income. A Canadian or European earner may prefer a different currency mix than a US dollar earner. Confirm with a tax adviser in your country of residence.

Is financing in Mexico worth it in 2026?

For the right buyer, yes — financing lets you keep capital working at home while owning in Mexico, and rates on USD cross-border loans are reasonable for a dollar earner. The keys are choosing the currency that matches your income, budgeting a real 35%–50% deposit as a non-resident, and pricing the US currency-gain risk before you commit. Get those three right, line up an independent lawyer, and a Mexican mortgage is a sound, well-trodden path — not the cash-only minefield the myths suggest.

Frequently asked questions

Can an American get a mortgage in Mexico?

Yes. Foreign nationals, including non-residents, can finance Mexican property. Permanent residents get the best bank access; temporary residents qualify with select lenders; non-residents use cross-border USD lenders or developer financing — with more documentation and a bigger deposit.

What are mortgage rates in Mexico for foreigners in 2026?

Peso mortgages run about 9%–14%; cross-border USD loans for qualified US/Canadian buyers are roughly 5%–9%. The currency you borrow in matters as much as the rate.

How much down payment do Americans need to buy in Mexico?

Mexican banks lend ~70–80% to qualified residents; cross-border products cap at 60–70% LTV; non-residents often face 35–50% down. Some fintechs reach 90% LTV for the strongest profiles.

Can you finance property in Mexico's restricted (coastal) zone?

Yes. In the restricted zone you hold title through a fideicomiso (bank trust), and lenders routinely finance trust-held property. Budget the trust's setup and annual fees.

Is mortgage interest on a Mexican property tax-deductible in the US?

Often yes — interest on a qualified first or second home can be deductible within the US limits, even abroad. But a peso loan exposes you to IRS foreign-currency rules at payoff. Confirm both with a cross-border CPA.

Do I need an RFC to get a mortgage in Mexico?

For a Mexican bank mortgage, almost always — plus a Mexican bank account, since the lender disburses and collects locally. Cross-border and developer financing don't always require one up front, but you'll need it to pay property taxes and utilities once you own.

Can you pay off a Mexican mortgage early without a penalty?

Usually, but it's not guaranteed by law for mortgage-secured loans the way it is for some other consumer credit. Most major banks advertise no-penalty prepayment; developer financing varies more. Check your specific contract and CONDUSEF's contract database before signing.

How does financing work in Mexico's restricted (coastal) zone?

A trustee bank holds legal title in a fideicomiso for your benefit, and the mortgage attaches to your beneficial rights under that trust rather than a direct lien on the land. Both Mexican banks and cross-border lenders finance trust-held property routinely; budget the trust's setup and annual fees on top of loan costs.
