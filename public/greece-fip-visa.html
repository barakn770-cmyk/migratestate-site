Greece Financially Independent Person (FIP) Visa for Americans (2026)

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated September 2026  ·  11 min read
The Financially Independent Person (FIP) permit is Greece's non-work residency route for people who can live on savings, a pension or other passive income. A single applicant needs €3,500 a month (about €42,000 a year), plus 20% for a spouse and 15% per child, under Law 5038/2023. The permit runs three years and renews in further three-year blocks, carries no minimum-stay condition of its own, and can be paired with Greece's 7% flat tax for foreign pensioners — but 183+ days in Greece still makes you a Greek tax resident, and the US keeps taxing your worldwide income regardless.

Greece's Golden Visa gets most of the headlines, but it isn't the only door in — and for an American who actually wants to live in Greece rather than just hold a European foothold, it usually isn't the right one either. The Financially Independent Person permit, created by the 2023 Immigration Code, is Greece's answer to Portugal's D7 and Spain's non-lucrative visa: no property purchase, no business plan, just proof you can support yourself. Here is how it actually works in 2026, including the details that get flattened in shorter write-ups.

What is the FIP visa?

The Financially Independent Person (FIP) residence permit is a national (not Schengen-wide) permit for non-EU nationals — Americans included — who intend to live in Greece without working, supported by pensions, investments, rental income or other stable passive resources. It is created by Article 163, paragraph 8 of Law 5038/2023 (the Code of Immigration and Social Integration), and is applied for at a Greek consulate abroad before a first entry visa, then converted into a residence permit after arrival through the local decentralized administration (or, from abroad, renewed through Greece's immigration portal).

Unlike the Golden Visa, the FIP route requires no investment or property purchase at all. Many holders do go on to buy a home for their own use — and can pair the two guides on this site — but eligibility rests entirely on demonstrated income, not on where or whether you own real estate.

The €3,500/month income requirement

The core test is financial. A June 2024 decision by the Ministry of Migration and Asylum, clarifying Article 163(8), fixed the current thresholds:

ApplicantMinimum stable monthly income
Single applicant€3,500 (~€42,000/year)
+ spouse / cohabiting partner+20% → €4,200 combined
+ each dependent child+15% → +€525 per child

Accepted evidence includes foreign pension statements, bank statements showing recurring passive income (dividends, interest, rental income), or documented liquid savings sufficient to project the required monthly amount forward. The income does not need to originate in Greece or be remitted through a Greek bank, though opening a Greek account is standard practice for paying local bills and any 7% flat-tax liability (below).

No work, and no minimum stay to keep it

Like Spain's non-lucrative visa and Portugal's D7, the FIP permit is a non-work category — it does not authorize employment or self-employment in Greece, and it is not designed for remote workers, who should look at a separate digital-nomad-style route instead. Where it differs from many comparable visas is what it does not require: there is no codified minimum number of days per year you must spend in Greece to keep the FIP permit itself valid, unlike, for example, Portugal's D7 (which expects a genuine center of life in Portugal). In practice, though, two things push FIP holders toward real Greek presence anyway: the permit is meant to reflect actual relocation rather than a passive investment, and — as the next section covers — spending 183 or more days in Greece in a calendar year makes you a Greek tax resident regardless of what the immigration rule requires.

Application process, fees and timeline

The standard path: apply for a national (type D) entry visa at the Greek consulate covering your US state of residence, submitting proof of income, health insurance valid in Greece, a clean criminal record and proof of accommodation (a lease or property deed). Once in Greece, you convert the entry visa into a residence permit through the competent decentralized administration or municipality, which issues the physical permit card. Processing times vary significantly by consulate and by region inside Greece — budget several months end-to-end, and expect to renew any documents (like a police-clearance certificate) that expire while the file is pending.

Duration, renewal and the path to citizenship

The FIP permit is issued for three years on first approval and renews in further three-year blocks, for as long as the income condition and other requirements continue to be met — not the two-year cycle that applies to some other Greek categories, so don't assume the numbers are interchangeable across visa types. Family members included on the permit generally share its validity and renewal cycle. As with other Greek residence categories, ordinary naturalization for a non-EU national typically requires seven years of continuous, genuine residence in Greece plus a language and civics examination — a timeline that only makes sense if you are actually living in Greece for most of each year, not just renewing the permit from abroad.

Greek tax residency and the 183-day rule

Spending 183 or more days in Greece within a calendar year (or having your center of vital interests there) makes you a Greek tax resident, taxed on worldwide income at Greece's ordinary progressive rates. That is a separate question from whether your FIP permit stays valid, and it is easy for a first-time applicant to focus on the immigration rule and miss the tax one. Greece also levies ENFIA, the annual real-estate tax, on any Greek property you own, regardless of your tax-residency status.

Pairing with the 7% flat tax for pensioners

Many FIP applicants are retirees living on a pension, which makes Greece's Article 5B special regime worth knowing before you move, not after. Under Article 5B of the Income Tax Code, an individual receiving a pension who transfers tax residence to Greece can elect a flat 7% tax on all foreign-source income — not just the pension itself — for up to 15 years, instead of ordinary progressive rates that run well into the 40s at the margin. To qualify, you generally must not have been a Greek tax resident in five of the prior six years, and must be transferring residence from a country with an administrative-cooperation (information-exchange) agreement with Greece — the United States qualifies. The 5B election is a separate application from the FIP residence permit itself, made to the Greek tax authority (AADE), and the two should be planned together rather than sequentially, since the tax election has its own conditions and paperwork.

If you intend to genuinely relocate and don't want to tie up €400,000+ in Greek property to do it, the FIP visa is the cheaper, more direct route.

US tax: what Americans still owe

Moving to Greece on a FIP permit changes nothing about US tax obligations: American citizens are taxed on worldwide income no matter where they live. The US–Greece income tax treaty and the foreign tax credit generally prevent the same income from being taxed twice in full, but the math changes sharply if you elect the 7% Article 5B regime — because so little Greek tax is actually paid, the available foreign tax credit is small, and most of the US tax liability on that income typically still comes due. Model this with a cross-border CPA before electing 5B, not after your first US return under it. Greek financial accounts used to receive pension or investment income can also trigger FBAR (accounts over US$10,000 in aggregate) and Form 8938 reporting.

Step-by-step: how Americans apply

  1. Confirm your income qualifies — €3,500/month single, plus 20% for a spouse and 15% per child — and gather pension statements, bank statements or investment records as evidence.
  2. Arrange Greece-valid health insurance and secure proof of accommodation (a lease or a property you own).
  3. File the national (type D) visa application at the Greek consulate covering your US state of residence, with a clean criminal-record certificate and the financial evidence above.
  4. Travel to Greece on the approved visa and apply to convert it into a FIP residence permit through the local decentralized administration.
  5. If eligible and desired, file separately for the Article 5B 7% pensioner regime with AADE, ideally before you become a Greek tax resident.
  6. Set up US tax compliance — model the foreign tax credit against any 5B election, and flag FBAR/Form 8938 for Greek accounts — before your first US filing deadline after the move.
  7. Renew every three years, keeping income documentation current, and track your actual days of residence toward the seven-year citizenship threshold if that is a goal.

Is the FIP visa worth it in 2026?

For an American who wants to actually live in Greece — not just hold a European option — the FIP visa is usually the more sensible route than the Golden Visa: it costs a fraction as much, requires no property purchase, and it's built for exactly this use case (retirees and people with passive income who don't need to work locally). The trade-off is that it rewards real presence rather than an arm's-length investment, and the tax consequences of that presence — Greek tax residency at 183 days, and the separate but complementary Article 5B pensioner election — need to be modeled with a cross-border advisor before you move, not discovered on your first Greek tax return. Get the income documentation, the 5B timing and the US tax plan right up front, and Greece is a straightforward, well-trodden path for Americans who want to genuinely relocate.

Frequently asked questions

How much income do you need for Greece's FIP visa in 2026?

€3,500 per month (€42,000/year) for a single applicant under Law 5038/2023 Article 163(8), plus 20% for a spouse and 15% per dependent child. Pensions, rental income, dividends and interest all qualify, and the funds don't need to be Greek-sourced.

Can I work in Greece on the FIP visa?

No. It is a non-work permit for people living off passive income, similar to Spain's non-lucrative visa — not designed for remote workers or employees.

How long does the Greek FIP permit last?

Three years on first issuance, renewable in further three-year blocks — not the two-year term of some other Greek categories. There's no codified minimum-stay condition to keep the permit itself, though 183+ days a year in Greece makes you a Greek tax resident regardless.

Does the FIP visa require buying property in Greece?

No. It's based entirely on income, unlike the Golden Visa's investment thresholds — though many holders buy a home afterward for their own use.

Can I combine the FIP visa with Greece's 7% flat tax for pensioners?

Often yes, if you receive a foreign pension, weren't a Greek tax resident in five of the prior six years, and are transferring residence from a treaty/information-exchange country (the US qualifies). Article 5B taxes all foreign-source income at 7% for up to 15 years, but it's a separate AADE application from the FIP permit.

FIP visa or Greek Golden Visa — which is better for Americans?

The FIP visa suits Americans who actually want to live in Greece — it's far cheaper and needs no property purchase. The Golden Visa suits buyers who want a European base or eventual EU access without relocating, since it has no stay requirement at all, at the cost of a €400,000–€800,000 investment.

Do I still owe US tax if I move to Greece on the FIP visa?

Yes. US citizens are taxed on worldwide income regardless of Greek residency. The US–Greece treaty and foreign tax credit help, but electing the 7% Article 5B regime leaves little Greek tax to credit, so most US tax on that income can still be due. FBAR and Form 8938 apply to Greek accounts over the relevant thresholds.

Does the FIP permit cover my family?

Yes — a spouse or partner and dependent children can be included, with the income requirement increased by 20% and 15% per child respectively, and family members generally share the permit's three-year validity and renewal cycle.
