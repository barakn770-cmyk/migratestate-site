Buying Property in Mexico as an American: The 2026 Legal & Tax Guide

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated September 2026  ·  14 min read
Yes — US citizens can legally buy property anywhere in Mexico in 2026. Outside the "restricted zone" (within 50 km of the coast or 100 km of a land border) Americans can hold title directly. Inside it, residential property is held through a fideicomiso, a renewable 50-year bank trust. Expect closing costs of 5–10% and annual property tax (predial) of just 0.05–0.3% of value.

Mexico is the number-one destination for Americans buying a second home abroad — and for good reason: proximity, affordability, established expat communities, and ownership costs a fraction of the US. But the questions that actually keep buyers up at night aren't about the view. They're legal and financial: Can I even own it? Will the IRS come after me? What's a fideicomiso?

This guide answers each one, with 2026 figures, written in the order a careful buyer should actually think.

Can Americans legally buy property in Mexico?

Yes. There is no prohibition on US citizens owning real estate in Mexico, and you do not need to be a resident or a citizen to buy. The only meaningful restriction is geographic, and it has a simple, well-established workaround.

Under the Mexican Constitution, foreigners cannot hold direct title to residential land inside the restricted zone — any land within 50 kilometers of the coastline or 100 kilometers of an international border. Because most destinations Americans want (Cancún, Playa del Carmen, Tulum, Puerto Vallarta, Los Cabos) sit on the coast, this affects the majority of buyers. The workaround is the fideicomiso.

What is a fideicomiso, and do you need one?

A fideicomiso is a bank trust: a Mexican bank holds legal title as trustee, while you — the beneficiary — keep every meaningful right of ownership (live in it, renovate, rent, sell, or leave it to your heirs). The bank cannot use, encumber, or sell the property; it only acts on your instructions. It runs for a 50-year term, indefinitely renewable, costs roughly $500–$700 a year in trustee fees, and applies only inside the restricted zone — outside it, you take direct title with no trust needed. Setting one up requires a permit from the Secretaría de Relaciones Exteriores (SRE): the official fee is $19,950 MXN and the SRE's own published turnaround is five business days, though your bank and notario add their own processing time around it. For commercial use inside the zone, a Mexican corporation is often used instead. We cover the mechanics, the bank-fee comparison and the US tax treatment in full in our dedicated fideicomiso guide.

Can Americans get a mortgage in Mexico?

Sometimes — but most Americans pay cash or finance from the US side, and the numbers explain why.

Because peso mortgages are expensive and approval is stricter for non-residents, many buyers use one of three alternatives instead: a US home-equity line (HELOC) or cash-out refinance against a US property, a dedicated cross-border USD lender that underwrites against US income and US credit history rather than Mexican residency, or developer financing on new-build projects, which is typically shorter-term and carries a materially higher effective rate than either bank route. What are closing costs and property taxes?

Budget 5%–10% of the purchase price in closing costs, paid by the buyer:

ScenarioTypical closing costs
Outside the restricted zone (direct title)5%–8%
Inside the zone (with fideicomiso)7%–10%

These cover the acquisition tax (ISAI), notary fees, the public registry, appraisal, and — inside the zone — the fideicomiso setup and the SRE permit described above. In Mexico, the notario público is a senior government-appointed lawyer who validates the transaction, calculates and withholds the taxes due, and registers the deed; using a strong one is non-negotiable, and notario fees themselves typically run about 1%–1.5% of the transaction value.

The single largest line in that 5%–10% is the ISAI (Impuesto sobre Adquisición de Inmuebles), a state-level acquisition tax that each of Mexico's 32 entities sets independently — so the rate genuinely depends on where you buy:

The CDMX figures come directly from the city's own published fiscal code; the other states' rates are the figures most consistently cited by Mexican notary and fiscal-advisory sources as of mid-2026, and — because a state legislature can revise its fiscal code annually — should be confirmed with your notario against that state's current law before you budget a closing.

The good news on holding costs: annual property tax — predial — runs just 0.05%–0.3% of value per year, far below US levels. A $400,000 home might cost only a few hundred dollars a year.

Ejido land, pre-construction escrow & title insurance

Three practical risks account for most of the horror stories Americans hear about buying in Mexico — and all three are manageable with the right checks before you sign.

Ejido land: the single biggest trap

Ejido land is communal agrarian land, originally granted to farming communities and administered under Mexico's agrarian law rather than ordinary civil property law. It cannot be sold to a private buyer, foreign or Mexican, until it has been formally converted out of the ejido regime into pleno dominio (full private title) through Mexico's Registro Agrario Nacional (RAN) — the federal agrarian registry. Sellers sometimes offer ejido parcels at an attractive price with an informal "certificate of possession" that is not a deed and cannot be insured or mortgaged. Before you sign anything: (1) get the property's registration checked directly against the Public Registry of Property for that municipality, not just the seller's paperwork; (2) ask your attorney to confirm the parcel's dominio pleno status directly with the RAN, which publishes a status-lookup tool for exactly this purpose; and (3) treat "regularization is in process" as a reason to walk away or heavily discount the price, not a technicality to close around. A title that hasn't cleared ejido status cannot be conveyed into a fideicomiso or direct title cleanly, whatever the seller promises.

Buying pre-construction (off-plan)

Pre-construction pricing is common in growth markets like the Riviera Maya, and it can be a genuine discount — but you are extending credit to a developer, not buying a finished asset. Two protections matter most. First, insist that your deposits go into a third-party escrow (commonly structured as a fideicomiso de garantía held by a bank, separate from the developer's operating accounts) rather than directly to the developer, released against construction milestones rather than on a fixed calendar. Second, check the developer's track record on the Public Registry — confirm the land itself is unencumbered and that the developer, not a related shell company, actually holds title before you sign a contrato de promesa. A reputable notario or real-estate attorney will insist on both; a developer who resists either is a red flag.

Régimen de condominio and title insurance

Any multi-unit building should be registered under a formal régimen de condominio (condominium regime) with the Public Registry — this is what creates individually titled units, a legally constituted HOA, and an enforceable reserve fund and bylaws, rather than an informal shared-ownership arrangement. Ask for the registered reglamento (bylaws) before you buy, not after, especially if short-term letting matters to you (see below). Separately, title insurance is available in Mexico — US-regulated insurers including Stewart Title and First American operate Mexico-specific policies aimed at foreign buyers, covering exactly the ejido and title-defect risk described above. It is optional and adds to closing costs, but for a first-time buyer, or any purchase where the ownership history is not perfectly clean, it is worth pricing alongside your notario and attorney fees.

The part Americans get wrong: US taxes

Owning property in Mexico does not free you from the US tax system. Here's the framework — worth reviewing with a cross-border CPA, and covered in full depth in our guides to US taxes on Mexican property and, when the day comes, selling Mexican property as a US citizen.

1. Simply owning the property

Buying and holding a personal-use home does not, by itself, trigger an IRS filing requirement.

2. The fideicomiso and Form 3520

For years, advisors feared the fideicomiso would be treated as a foreign trust, triggering onerous Forms 3520/3520-A. IRS Revenue Ruling 2013-14 holds that a standard real-estate fideicomiso that merely holds residential title is not a trust for US tax purposes — meaning Forms 3520/3520-A are generally not required for a clean residential fideicomiso. This is the ruling most generic "move to Mexico" blogs miss. Confirm your specific structure with a CPA, as a modified trust or one holding cash can change the analysis.

3. Rental income

If you rent the property, that income is taxable in the US and reported on Schedule E — even though you also pay tax in Mexico. The Foreign Tax Credit generally prevents true double taxation.

4. Selling at a gain

A sale is a capital gain reportable in the US. Mexico also taxes the gain (ISR), and a Foreign Tax Credit usually applies — the full mechanics, including the notario's ISR withholding at closing, are in our guide to selling in Mexico as a US citizen.

5. Foreign bank accounts

If you open a Mexican bank account (common, often required), you may cross the FBAR (FinCEN 114) threshold of $10,000 aggregate, and possibly FATCA Form 8938. These are about accounts, not the property itself.

Will buying property get you residency in Mexico?

Not automatically. Unlike a Golden Visa, buying real estate in Mexico does not grant residency. However, owning property and demonstrating sufficient income or savings can support a Temporary or Permanent Resident visa application through a Mexican consulate — see our Mexico temporary resident visa guide for the current income and savings thresholds. Separately, note the 183-day rule: spend more than 183 days in Mexico in a calendar year and you may become a Mexican tax resident on worldwide income.

Step-by-step: buying property in Mexico as an American

  1. Define the zone. Coastal/border = restricted zone = fideicomiso. Inland = direct title.
  2. Engage an independent attorney — not the seller's, and separate from the notario.
  3. Get your RFC and, if needed, a Mexican bank account.
  4. Make an offer and sign a promissory agreement (contrato de promesa), usually with a 5–10% deposit.
  5. Due diligence: title search, no-liens certificate, property-tax status, ejido (communal land) check.
  6. Set up the fideicomiso (if in the restricted zone) and obtain the SRE permit.
  7. Close before the notario público, pay closing costs, and register the deed.
  8. Plan your US tax compliance before year-end.

Is buying property in Mexico worth it in 2026?

For lifestyle buyers and retirees, the value equation remains compelling: low carrying costs, a favorable environment for dollar holders, and mature expat infrastructure. For investors, rental yields in tourist hubs can be strong — but model the higher financing costs and both countries' taxes honestly before assuming the headline yield. The buyers who do well treat it as a legal and financial transaction first and a dream second: they confirm the zone and the title status before falling in love with a property, they price the SRE permit and notario fees into the budget rather than discovering them at closing, and they set up their Schedule E and depreciation correctly in year one rather than reconstructing it later. None of the diligence described in this guide is exotic by international standards — Mexico's system is simply different from a US closing, not less rigorous, and a good independent attorney and a licensed notario make it a well-trodden path rather than a gamble.

Can you short-let it? Bylaws and the lodging tax

Mexico is one of the more permissive markets for short-term letting, which is why Riviera Maya yields look attractive — but three checks still bite. The condominium bylaws registered under the régimen de condominio (see above) can prohibit short-term letting even where the municipality allows it — read the reglamento before you buy, not after. Cities (Mexico City in particular) have moved to restrict how many nights a unit may be let per year. And every state levies its own lodging tax (impuesto sobre hospedaje) on nightly rentals — Quintana Roo's is a commonly cited example, typically a few percentage points of the nightly rate — which major booking platforms now generally withhold and remit automatically, alongside the income-tax and VAT withholding on your rental income more broadly. Confirm the bylaws and the exact local rate for that building and state before you underwrite a nightly-rate yield. See our guide to short-term rental rules abroad.

Frequently asked questions

Can a US citizen own property in Mexico outright?

Yes. Outside the restricted zone (50 km from coast / 100 km from border) Americans hold direct title. Inside it, residential property is held via a fideicomiso bank trust, which conveys full ownership rights.

Is a fideicomiso safe?

Yes. It is a regulated 50-year renewable bank trust used by hundreds of thousands of foreign owners. The bank holds title as trustee but cannot use, sell, or encumber the property.

How much are closing costs in Mexico?

Typically 5–10% of the purchase price: 5–8% outside the restricted zone, 7–10% inside it with a fideicomiso.

Do I pay US taxes on a Mexican property?

Owning a personal-use home alone usually triggers no US filing. Rental income (Schedule E) and capital gains on sale are US-reportable, with a Foreign Tax Credit to avoid double taxation. A Mexican bank account may trigger FBAR/FATCA reporting.

Does buying property give me Mexican residency?

No, not automatically — but ownership plus sufficient income can support a residency visa application. Staying 183+ days a year can make you a Mexican tax resident.

What is ejido land, and why should I avoid buying it directly?

Ejido land is communal agrarian land that cannot be legally sold to a private buyer until it is converted to full private title (dominio pleno) through Mexico's Registro Agrario Nacional. A seller's informal "certificate of possession" is not a deed, cannot be mortgaged or insured, and cannot go into a fideicomiso. Always verify dominio pleno status with the RAN and the local Public Registry before signing.

Is title insurance available in Mexico?

Yes. US-regulated insurers including Stewart Title and First American offer Mexico-specific title insurance policies aimed at foreign buyers, covering title-defect and ejido-history risk. It's optional and adds to closing costs, but worth pricing for a first-time buyer or any property with an unclear ownership history.

What changes if I'm buying pre-construction (off-plan)?

You're extending credit to a developer, not buying a finished asset. Insist your deposits go into third-party escrow (often a fideicomiso de garantía) released against construction milestones, not directly to the developer's operating account, and confirm on the Public Registry that the land is unencumbered and titled to the developer before signing a contrato de promesa.
