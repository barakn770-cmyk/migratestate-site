Form 8949 for a Foreign Property Sale, 2026

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated July 2026  ·  11 min read
Sold a home abroad? The IRS still wants it on paper. A US citizen reports a foreign property sale on Form 8949, with totals carried to Schedule D — proceeds and basis translated to US dollars at two different exchange rates. But if the property was ever rented, it goes on Form 4797 first, not 8949. Getting that fork wrong is the single most common error on a foreign sale.

Which form: 8949, 4797, or both

Almost every guide to selling property abroad tells Americans to "report the gain on Form 8949 and Schedule D." For a large share of readers that instruction is simply wrong, and the error is expensive because it hides the depreciation the IRS is entitled to recapture. The form you use is decided not by where the property sits but by how you used it.

Form 8949, "Sales and Other Dispositions of Capital Assets", is the master ledger for capital assets: your personal residence, a vacation home you never rented, a plot of land held for investment. Each disposal is one line; the columns total up and roll into Schedule D, which nets them against your other capital gains and losses for the year. This is the path most people picture.

Form 4797, "Sales of Business Property", is a different animal. The moment a property is placed in service as a rental, it becomes Section 1231 business property, and its sale is reported on Form 4797 — which exists precisely to claw back the depreciation you deducted (or were entitled to deduct) while it was let. Only the net figure Form 4797 produces then travels onward to Schedule D. The property never appears on Form 8949 at all.

The fourth row is the messy, common one: an American buys a flat in Lisbon or Athens, lives in it, is posted elsewhere, rents it for two years, then sells. That sale can genuinely touch three forms at once — Form 4797 for the recapture, Form 8949 to record the Section 121 exclusion on the residence portion, and Schedule 1 for the mortgage currency gain. The rest of this guide walks the 8949 mechanics and shows exactly where each piece lands.

The eight columns, translated to dollars

Form 8949 has eight columns per transaction. On a domestic sale a broker fills most of them in for you. On a foreign sale nobody does, and two of them behave in a way that trips people up.

ColumnWhat it holdsThe foreign wrinkle
(a) DescriptionThe assete.g. "Apartment, Lisbon, Portugal"
(b) Date acquiredPurchase dateSets the exchange rate for basis
(c) Date soldClosing dateSets the exchange rate for proceeds
(d) ProceedsSale price less selling costsTranslated at the sale-date rate
(e) Cost basisPurchase price + improvementsEach item at its own purchase-date rate
(f) CodeAdjustment code(s)H for Section 121, L for a personal loss
(g) AdjustmentDollar adjustmentWhere the exclusion is subtracted
(h) Gain or loss(d) − (e) + (g)The taxable figure, in USD

Columns (d) and (e) are the whole game. The IRS does not care what the property did in euros or pesos; every US tax figure is a dollar figure. Your basis is translated at the spot rate on the day each cost was paid, and your proceeds at the spot rate on the closing day. Those are two different dates, so they are two different rates — and the gap between them lands squarely in column (h).

This produces the effect that surprises first-time foreign sellers: currency movement is taxable even when the property itself did nothing. A flat bought for €400,000 and sold for €400,000 shows a flat result in euros, but if the euro strengthened from 1.05 to 1.18 between the two dates, column (d) is roughly $472,000 against a column (e) of about $420,000 — a $52,000 gain conjured from nothing but the exchange rate. There is no separate "currency" line; it is simply baked into the numbers you enter.

One discipline matters more abroad than at home: use the spot rate on each actual date, not a blended annual average. If you bought in 2016, re-roofed in 2019 and added a bathroom in 2022, each of those costs is translated at its own date's rate and summed into column (e). Averaging them is a shortcut the instructions do not permit and one that usually overstates or understates basis by real money.

Which box to check

Before the columns, Form 8949 asks you to check a box that groups the transaction. Short-term (held one year or less) sits in Part I; long-term (held more than one year) in Part II. Within each part you choose Box A/B/C or D/E/F depending on whether the sale was reported to you on a Form 1099-B.

A foreign closing — handled by a notary in Mexico, a solicitor in Ireland, a sun-yakushosho in Japan — never generates a US Form 1099-B. That puts virtually every foreign real-estate sale in the "transactions not reported on a 1099-B" category: Box C if short-term, Box F if long-term. Because most people hold a home abroad for more than a year, Box F is the usual answer. Get this wrong and the totals land in the wrong Schedule D lines, though the tax result is generally the same once corrected.

Entering the Section 121 exclusion (code H)

If the property was your principal residence, the Section 121 exclusion can wipe out up to $250,000 of gain ($500,000 filing jointly). But you do not claim it by leaving the sale off the form. You report the full transaction and then back the exclusion out.

Concretely: fill in columns (d), (e) and the resulting gain, put code H in column (f), and enter the excluded amount as a negative number in column (g). Column (h) then shows only the gain that remains taxable after the exclusion. If the whole gain is excluded, column (h) is zero — but the line is still there. Omitting a fully-excluded sale is a filing error, especially where a settlement statement exists or the gain exceeded the limit.

This is also where the phantom currency gain quietly gets absorbed. The $52,000 exchange-rate gain from the example above is ordinary column-(h) gain, and Section 121 will usually swallow it whole — which is exactly why the exclusion is worth more to an American abroad than to one at home. It is doing a job a domestic seller never needs done.

The non-deductible currency loss (code L)

The mirror image is a trap. If the dollar strengthened while you owned the place, column (h) can show a loss on a home that rose in local-currency terms. On a personal residence that loss is not deductible — losses on personal-use property never are, and a currency-manufactured loss is no exception.

You still report the sale. You enter code L in column (f) and a positive adjustment in column (g) equal to the loss, so column (h) nets to zero. You are taxed on phantom gains but denied phantom losses, which is asymmetric and unfair, but it is the rule. Note the contrast: a loss on investment land or on a rental property generally is deductible — another reason the personal-versus-business character of the property drives everything.

The part that never touches Schedule D

Here is the nuance that even careful filers miss. If you funded the purchase with a foreign-currency mortgage and repaid it out of the sale, the loan is a separate taxable transaction under IRC Section 988. If the dollar strengthened between drawdown and repayment, you repaid the bank in cheaper dollars and realised a currency gain on the debt itself.

That gain is ordinary income, taxed at rates up to 37%, and it does not go on Form 8949 or Schedule D. It is reported as other income on Schedule 1. Section 121 does not shelter it — the exclusion covers the residence, never the loan. So a single sale can produce a capital figure on 8949/Schedule D and a wholly separate ordinary figure on Schedule 1, taxed at a higher rate, from the same closing. We cover the mechanics in our guide to the foreign mortgage phantom gain.

The mental model: a leveraged foreign sale is up to three tax events, not one. The property gain (Form 8949 or 4797 → Schedule D), the depreciation recapture if it was ever rented (Form 4797), and the mortgage currency gain (Section 988 → Schedule 1). They use different forms, different rates and, potentially, three different tax brackets.

Filling it in, step by step

  1. Decide the form first. Was the property ever rented? If yes, it starts on Form 4797, not Form 8949. If it was purely a home or investment land, Form 8949 is correct.
  2. Translate the basis. Take the purchase price plus each capital improvement and the transfer tax, converting each at the spot rate on the date it was paid. Sum into column (e).
  3. Translate the proceeds. Convert gross sale price less agent and legal fees at the spot rate on the closing date. Enter in column (d).
  4. Set the holding period and box. More than a year is long-term, Part II, Box F. One year or less is short-term, Part I, Box C.
  5. Apply Section 121, if it is a home. Code H in column (f); the excluded amount as a negative in column (g).
  6. Handle a personal loss. If column (h) is negative on a residence, code L and a positive offsetting adjustment so it nets to zero.
  7. Carry to Schedule D. Totals from Form 8949 (and any net from Form 4797) roll into Schedule D and then to Form 1040.
  8. Report the mortgage separately. Run the Section 988 calculation on any foreign-currency loan and report the gain as ordinary income on Schedule 1.
  9. Claim the foreign tax credit last. Foreign tax paid on the sale is claimed on Form 1116 — but only against gain that actually stayed in your US taxable income.

If you're not American: what changes

The sale itself is identical for any foreign seller. The local capital gains tax, the notary process, withholding at closing and the country's own main-residence relief do not change with your passport. What is purely American is the paperwork described here: Form 8949, Form 4797, Schedule D, the dual-exchange-rate translation and the Section 988 mortgage rule.

If you are not a US person, drop the IRS forms entirely and substitute the reporting of your own country of tax residence. Check three things. First, whether your home country taxes gains on foreign property and what disclosure form it uses — the UK reports foreign gains through Self Assessment, Canada on Schedule 3, Australia in the CGT section of the return, and each has its own main-residence relief with its own tests. Second, whether you have foreign asset or account reporting obligations at home that the sale and its proceeds trigger. Third, whether a double-taxation treaty exists between your country and the country where the property sits — immovable property is almost always taxable first where it is located and relieved second at home. A British, Canadian or Australian seller computes everything in their own currency, under their own reliefs, on their own forms. Confirm the specifics with a tax adviser where you are resident.

Getting it right in 2026

Foreign property sales are where thin US reporting knowledge becomes costly. The gain itself is often modest; the mistakes are structural. Reporting a former rental on Form 8949 instead of Form 4797 buries the depreciation recapture the IRS will eventually find. Using an average exchange rate instead of dated spot rates distorts basis. Forgetting that a currency loss on a home is non-deductible — or that a currency gain on a mortgage is ordinary income on a different schedule — changes the bill by real percentages.

For 2026 the surrounding numbers are worth holding in view: long-term capital gains are taxed at 0% up to $49,450 of taxable income for singles ($98,900 married filing jointly), 15% up to $545,500 ($613,700 MFJ) and 20% above, with an extra 3.8% net investment income tax once modified AGI passes $200,000 single or $250,000 joint. Depreciation on a former rental is recaptured as unrecaptured Section 1250 gain at up to 25%, and the mortgage currency gain sits in ordinary brackets reaching 37%. The forms are how those rates actually attach to your sale — which is why the sellers who do well are the ones who decide the form, translate the currency and separate the mortgage before they file, not the April after.

Frequently asked questions

Do I report a foreign property sale on Form 8949 or Form 4797?

It depends on how the property was used. A pure personal residence or a piece of land held for investment goes on Form 8949, with the totals carried to Schedule D. But a property that was ever rented out is business property under Section 1231 and is reported first on Form 4797, which handles the depreciation recapture; only the net result flows to Schedule D. Putting a former rental straight onto Form 8949 is one of the most common filing errors on a foreign sale.

What exchange rate do I use on Form 8949 for a foreign property?

Two different rates. Cost basis in column (e) is translated at the spot exchange rate on the date each cost was actually paid — the purchase, and each improvement or transfer tax separately. Proceeds in column (d) are translated at the spot rate on the closing date of the sale. Because the two dates use two rates, currency movement between them becomes part of the taxable gain measured in US dollars.

How do I show the Section 121 exclusion on Form 8949?

You report the full sale as normal, then enter the excluded amount as a negative number in column (g) and put code H in column (f). The exclusion reduces the gain in column (h) but the sale itself still appears on the form. You never simply omit the transaction, even when the entire gain is excluded and you received a closing statement.

Which box do I check on Form 8949 for a foreign sale?

Almost always Box C for short-term or Box F for long-term — the boxes for transactions not reported to you on a Form 1099-B. Foreign closings do not generate a US 1099-B, so a foreign notary or solicitor sale falls into the not-reported category. Hold longer than one year and it is long-term (Box F); one year or less and it is short-term (Box C).

Is a currency loss on a foreign home deductible on Schedule D?

No. A loss on personal-use property is never deductible, even when the loss was created entirely by the dollar strengthening rather than by the property falling in value. You report the sale, enter code L in column (f) and a positive adjustment in column (g) to zero out the non-deductible loss. Investment land and rental property losses, by contrast, generally are deductible.
