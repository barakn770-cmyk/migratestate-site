Mexico Temporary Resident Visa for Americans: The 2026 Guide

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated September 2026  ·  11 min read
Mexico's temporary resident visa (residente temporal) is the most-used residency route for Americans, and it doesn't require buying anything. You qualify one of three ways — income of roughly 680 days of UMA per month (about $4,650), savings/investments averaging 11,400 days of UMA (about $78,000), or Mexican real estate worth 91,710 days of UMA (about $628,000). Approval abroad gets you a 180-day entry visa; inside Mexico you have 30 days to exchange it for your actual residence card, which is valid up to four years before you become eligible for permanent residency.

Mexico's temporary resident visa is the workhorse of American relocation south of the border — more Americans move to Mexico on this single permit than on any other route, and unlike a golden visa it does not force you to buy property or park half a million dollars in a government-approved fund. It is granted on economic solvency, and Mexico is unusually explicit about the math: every threshold is defined as a number of days of the UMA (Unidad de Medida y Actualización), a peso-denominated reference value that INEGI updates every year. That precision is also what trips people up, because the dollar figure quoted in a five-year-old blog post is not the dollar figure that applies today. This guide gives you the 2026 numbers, the exact process from consulate to residence card, and — because MigrateState's readers are American — the US filing consequences that most Mexican-side guides skip entirely.

What is the temporary resident visa?

The residente temporal is a national long-stay visa for foreigners who want to live in Mexico for more than 180 days but are not seeking Mexican citizenship immediately or investing at golden-visa scale. It is issued in two stages: first a visa stamped into your passport by a Mexican consulate abroad (for Americans, typically the consulate covering your US state of residence), then, after you enter Mexico, a physical residence card issued by the Instituto Nacional de Migración (INM). Unlike the tourist permit (FMM), the temporary resident visa lets you open Mexican bank accounts as a resident, obtain a Mexican tax ID (RFC) if you choose to work or invest locally, sponsor family members, and — after enough years — convert to permanent residency without leaving the country.

Three qualifying paths exist under the economic-solvency category, and consulates will accept any one of them: a monthly income test, a savings/investment balance test, and a real-estate ownership test. You only need to clear one.

The three financial routes

Every threshold below is set in days of UMA, not in a fixed peso or dollar amount, so it moves each year when INEGI recalculates the UMA. For 2026 the daily UMA is 117.31 pesos (monthly 3,566.22 pesos; annual 42,794.64 pesos), effective 1 February 2026 — a 3.69% increase over 2025. The dollar approximations below use a September 2026 exchange rate near 17.1 pesos per dollar; consulates convert the peso figure to your currency using the rate in effect on your actual appointment date, so use the UMA-day figure and the current peso amount as the reliable numbers, and the dollar figure as a rough guide only.

Route 1: Income

The income route is the most common for salaried retirees and pensioners. You must show stable monthly income — pension, Social Security, salary, dividends, or rental income — averaging at least 680 days of UMA over the preceding six months, documented with certified bank statements plus, depending on the income type, a pension award letter, a signed employer verification letter, or investment statements. Consulates generally want the income to look durable: a one-time bonus or an irregular consulting invoice is weaker evidence than a recurring deposit that matches a pension or Social Security schedule.

Route 2: Savings and investments

If your income is lower, seasonal, or comes mostly from asset sales rather than a paycheck, the savings/investment route lets you qualify on a lump balance instead: a twelve-month average balance across bank and/or investment accounts of at least 11,400 days of UMA, backed by certified statements covering the full twelve months (or a signed bank letter plus the statements). This is the natural fit for someone who recently sold a home or business and is living off proceeds rather than a monthly check.

Route 3: Real estate ownership

The least-discussed route lets you qualify through property you already own in Mexico: an original notarized deed (escritura) showing title to real estate worth at least 91,710 days of UMA — roughly $628,000 at 2026 values — satisfies the economic-solvency requirement on its own, with no income or savings test at all. Practically, this route matters most to buyers at the upper end of the market: someone who has already bought a Mexican property near or above that value (through a fideicomiso in the restricted coastal/border zone, or directly outside it) can use the deed itself as the qualifying asset, rather than separately proving liquid income or savings. It is capital-intensive and illiquid compared with the other two routes, so most applicants still use income or savings unless they are already buying at that price point for other reasons — see our guide to buying property in Mexico as an American for the purchase process itself.

The consulate-to-canje process

The process runs in two distinct legal stages, and missing the second one is the most common — and most avoidable — mistake Americans make.

1. Consular visaMexican consulate in the US covering your stateIn-person interview; submit proof of the route you're using (income, savings, or deed); pay the visa feeVisa stamp valid 180 days for a single entry
2. Entry to MexicoPort of entryPresent the visa; an immigration officer records your entryMust enter within the 180-day validity window

The consular visa fee is $54 (payable at the consulate; non-refundable if the application is denied). The 30-day canje deadline is set by INM regulation, not by the consulate, and it runs from your entry date — not from when you feel ready to deal with paperwork. Book your INM appointment as soon as you land; in busy INM offices (Mexico City, Guadalajara, the coastal resort cities) appointment slots fill up, and missing the window can jeopardize your status even though you entered legally.

Renewal and the path to permanent residency

Once issued, the temporary resident card is valid for a period you and INM agree on at the canje appointment, up to a statutory maximum of four years of temporary residency in total (renewable within that ceiling). Under Article 54(V) of the Ley de Migración, a foreigner is granted permanent residence status once four years have elapsed since first obtaining temporary residence — at that point residente permanente becomes available regardless of the route you originally qualified under, without having to re-prove income, savings, or property ownership. Permanent residency removes the renewal cycle entirely and, unlike the temporary card, does not require maintaining the original financial qualification.

Mexican tax residency: not a day count

This is the single most misunderstood point in every "Mexico residency" article aimed at Americans, because English-language guides tend to import the 183-day rule from other countries. Mexico doesn't use one. Under Article 9 of the Código Fiscal de la Federación, an individual is a Mexican tax resident once they establish their casa habitación — their home — in Mexico. If you maintain a home in more than one country (common for part-time residents who keep a US house), residency instead turns on your centro de intereses vitales (center of vital interests): Mexico is your tax home if more than 50% of your total annual income has a Mexican source, or if the principal center of your professional activities is in Mexico. Mexican nationals are separately presumed to be tax residents unless they prove otherwise.

The practical takeaway: holding the residente temporal card does not, by itself, make you a Mexican taxpayer, and there is no bright-line day count you can watch to know when you cross the line. A retiree who buys a vacation condo, gets the temporary resident card, and keeps their primary home and income in the US generally does not become a Mexican tax resident on that basis alone. Someone who relocates their household and the bulk of their income to Mexico likely does. If your situation is anywhere near the line, get a written residency analysis from a Mexican accountant — the consequence of getting it wrong is being taxed on worldwide income you did not expect to report in Mexico.

If you also buy property: the fideicomiso

Temporary residency and property ownership are legally independent, but many applicants pursue both at once. If the property you buy — or already own, for the real-estate qualifying route — sits within Mexico's restricted zone (roughly 50 km of the coast or 100 km of a border), you cannot hold direct title as a foreigner; you hold it through a fideicomiso, a bank trust. The IRS settled the US tax treatment of this structure in Revenue Ruling 2013-14: a standard Mexican land trust used solely to hold a personal residence is not treated as a foreign trust for US purposes, so it does not trigger Form 3520/3520-A foreign-trust reporting. It is a distinct topic from the visa itself — see our dedicated fideicomiso guide for setup costs, the annual trustee fee, and beneficiary rules.

US tax: what doesn't change

Nothing about Mexican residency reduces a US citizen's US tax obligations. The United States taxes citizens and green card holders on worldwide income no matter where they live, so your US federal return, and any US state filing obligation you still have, continues exactly as before. What does change is your foreign-reporting footprint:

  • FBAR (FinCEN Form 114): required once the aggregate value of your foreign financial accounts — including any Mexican bank account you opened to qualify for the visa or to fund a fideicomiso — exceeds $10,000 at any point in the year. Due April 15, with an automatic extension to October 15.
  • Form 8938 (FATCA): required for specified foreign financial assets above $200,000 (single, year-end) or $300,000 (single, any time in the year); $400,000 / $600,000 for joint filers, with higher thresholds still for bona fide residents abroad.
  • Foreign tax credit (Form 1116): if you do become a Mexican tax resident under the Article 9 test above and pay Mexican income tax, the US–Mexico tax treaty and the foreign tax credit are what prevent the same income from being taxed twice.

Step-by-step checklist

  1. Pick your route — income, savings/investments, or an existing property deed — based on which threshold you clear most comfortably.
  2. Assemble the evidence — six months of income statements, twelve months of savings statements, or a notarized deed, per the route.
  3. Book the consular appointment — at the Mexican consulate covering your US state of residence.
  4. Attend the interview and pay the $54 fee — leave with the visa stamped in your passport, valid 180 days.
  5. Enter Mexico within 180 days — a single entry on that visa.
  6. Book your INM appointment immediately — the canje clock (30 calendar days) starts on your entry date.
  7. Complete the canje — collect your physical tarjeta de residente temporal.
  8. Get a residency and tax read from a Mexican accountant — before you assume the card does or doesn't make you a Mexican taxpayer.
  9. Track the four-year clock — toward automatic eligibility for permanent residency.

Is it worth it in 2026?

For Americans who actually want to live in Mexico rather than just visit for months at a time, the temporary resident visa remains the most accessible legal route on the table — a few hundred dollars in fees against a golden-visa-style investment, and three separate ways to qualify depending on whether your money sits in a paycheck, an account, or a property deed. The parts worth double-checking before you file are the ones this guide focused on: use the current year's UMA, not an old dollar figure from a forum post; don't skip the 30-day canje after entry; and don't assume the residence card and Mexican tax residency are the same thing, because under Article 9 they are not. Get those three right, and the rest of the process is genuinely one of the more straightforward residency routes available to Americans anywhere in the world.

Frequently asked questions

How much income do I need for Mexico's temporary resident visa in 2026?

680 days of UMA per month, verified over the last six months — about 79,771 pesos, roughly $4,650 at a September 2026 exchange rate. Add 220 UMA-days (about $1,500) per month for a spouse or each dependent. Consulates convert to your currency at the rate on your appointment date, so confirm the current peso figure directly.

Can I qualify through savings or investments instead of income?

Yes — a twelve-month average balance of at least 11,400 days of UMA, about $78,000 at 2026 values, backed by certified statements for all twelve months. This suits applicants with irregular income or proceeds from an asset sale rather than a steady paycheck.

Can I get Mexican temporary residency by buying property?

Yes, through the real-estate route: a notarized deed to Mexican property worth at least 91,710 days of UMA, about $628,000 at 2026 values, satisfies the requirement with no separate income or savings test.

What do I need to do after I enter Mexico on the visa?

The consular stamp is only an entry visa, valid 180 days for one entry. Once inside Mexico you have 30 calendar days to complete the canje at an INM office and receive your actual residence card — miss that window and your status is at risk.

Does becoming a Mexican temporary resident make me a Mexican tax resident?

Not automatically. Under Article 9 of the Código Fiscal de la Federación, Mexican tax residency follows where you establish your casa habitación, or — if you keep homes in two countries — your centro de intereses vitales (over 50% of income Mexican-sourced, or your professional activity centered in Mexico). It is a separate question from your immigration status.

How does Mexican residency affect my US taxes?

It doesn't reduce them — the US taxes citizens on worldwide income regardless of residence. What changes is reporting: a Mexican account tied to your visa or a fideicomiso counts toward the $10,000 FBAR threshold, and may trigger Form 8938 above $200,000–$600,000 depending on filing status and whether you live abroad.
