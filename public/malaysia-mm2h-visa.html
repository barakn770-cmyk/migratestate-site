Malaysia MM2H 2026: Tiers, Fixed Deposits & Property Rules for Americans

What MM2H is — and what changed in the 2024 relaunch

Malaysia My Second Home (MM2H) is the country's flagship long-stay residency program, run by the Ministry of Tourism, Arts and Culture through the dedicated MM2H Centre. For two decades it was known as one of the world's most accessible long-term visas: a modest fixed deposit, minimal stay requirements, and a renewable 10-year pass. That version no longer exists.

MM2H was suspended and rebuilt twice in the 2020s. The most consequential change landed with the 2024 restructuring, which replaced the old single-tier program with four distinct tiers — Silver, Gold, Platinum, and a separate Special Economic Zone (SEZ) track centred on Forest City in Johor — each with its own fixed-deposit minimum, pass validity, and, for the first time, a compulsory property purchase. The old version let participants choose whether to buy real estate; the current version does not.

Do Americans qualify?

Yes. Unlike Panama's friendly-nations list or similar shortlists elsewhere, MM2H does not gate eligibility by a published country list — it is open to citizens of countries that maintain diplomatic relations with Malaysia, which covers the United States. Applicants must be at least 25 years old for the Silver, Gold and Platinum tiers, pass a security screening and medical check, and show a clean background. Nationality determines nothing beyond that baseline; the tier you qualify for is purely a function of the deposit and property price you can commit.

The four federal tiers: Silver, Gold, Platinum, SEZ

Every federal tier shares the same skeleton — a fixed deposit placed with a Malaysian bank, a minimum-value property purchase, a 90-day cumulative annual stay requirement (waivable in part for dependants aged 25–49), and a one-off participation fee — but the numbers scale sharply by tier:

All four require the deposit to sit with a Malaysian financial institution licensed under the Financial Services Act 2013, and all four carry the same 90-day cumulative annual stay rule, which for participants aged 25–49 can be met by the principal applicant, their dependants, or a mix of both. Dependants — a spouse, children under 21 (or unemployed and single up to 34), disabled children of any age, parents and parents-in-law, and, for Platinum, foreign domestic staff — are added to the same application rather than filing separately.

The property lock, the 50% withdrawal, and dependants

Three mechanics apply uniformly across Silver, Gold and Platinum, and are the details that trip up applicants who research only the headline deposit figure. First, the property purchase is not a suggestion — it is a condition of the pass, must be completed after MM2H approval, and the property cannot be sold for 10 years (upgrading to a higher-value property is permitted). Second, participants may withdraw up to 50% of the principal fixed-deposit value after approval, for residence purchases, education, or medical and tourism-related expenses — a meaningful liquidity release once you've cleared the approval stage, and it does not need to be topped back up. Third, renewal at the end of each pass period requires a current medical report and proof of private health insurance for the principal and dependants, on top of maintaining the deposit and property.

Sarawak's separate program: S-MM2H

Malaysia's federal structure gives the state of Sarawak, on Borneo, control over its own immigration — which is why it runs an entirely separate program, Sarawak-Malaysia My Second Home (S-MM2H), administered by the state's own Ministry of Tourism, Creative Industry and Performing Arts rather than the federal MM2H Centre in Kuala Lumpur. It is a genuinely different product, not a regional variant of the federal tiers:

For an American drawn to Borneo's lower cost of living rather than Kuala Lumpur or Penang, S-MM2H is often the cheaper and less demanding route — no mandatory property purchase and a much lighter stay requirement — but it is a Sarawak-specific pass, and anyone splitting time between Sarawak and Peninsular Malaysia should confirm with a licensed S-MM2H agent how that affects onward travel within the country.

Buying the qualifying property

The MM2H property purchase is layered on top of Malaysia's ordinary foreign-ownership rules, not a shortcut around them: you still need to clear the relevant state's minimum price for foreign buyers (commonly RM1,000,000 but ranging roughly RM500,000–RM3,000,000 depending on the state) and obtain the mandatory state-authority consent, and — from 1 January 2026 — pay the flat 8% foreign-buyer stamp duty on the transfer. Our full guide to buying property in Malaysia covers those thresholds, the consent timeline, and the stamp-duty change in detail; the short version for MM2H purposes is that your Silver-tier RM600,000 property, for example, will usually already clear the foreign-buyer minimum in most states, but you should verify the specific state's threshold before you commit to a unit, since it can exceed the MM2H property floor in some markets.

The US tax angle

Malaysia's own tax pitch is genuinely attractive on paper: it taxes residents primarily on Malaysia-sourced income, and foreign-sourced income received by individual tax residents currently qualifies for an exemption running from 1 January 2022 through 31 December 2036, subject to conditions. None of that changes anything for a US citizen. The United States taxes citizens on worldwide income regardless of residency, so nothing about MM2H — the pass, the property, or Malaysia's own exemption — reduces a single dollar of your US tax bill.

What MM2H does create is a significant reporting obligation. A fixed deposit of US$150,000 to US$1,000,000, held with a Malaysian bank in your name, is a foreign financial account well past the US$10,000 FBAR threshold (FinCEN Form 114) and, given the size, almost certainly past the Form 8938 thresholds as well. Both are information returns, not tax bills — but the penalties for missing them are severe, and MM2H's deposit requirement means every single tier triggers both filings from day one. If the qualifying property is purchased through a Malaysian company or structure rather than your personal name (uncommon for MM2H but not unheard of for larger Platinum-tier purchases), that adds its own layer of US corporate-reporting exposure — a structuring question worth raising with a cross-border CPA before, not after, you wire the deposit. Rental income from the property, if you ever let it, is taxable in the US the same as any other foreign rental, with a foreign tax credit available for Malaysian tax paid.

Step by step: how the application runs

  1. Choose a tier based on the deposit and property price you can commit — ideally after a US tax advisor has weighed in on the reporting side.
  2. Engage a licensed MM2H agent. Applications must be submitted through an agent approved by the MM2H Centre; there is no direct-to-government self-filing route.
  3. Assemble documents — passport, police clearance, medical report, proof of funds for the deposit, and dependant documentation where relevant.
  4. Receive conditional approval, then place the fixed deposit with a licensed Malaysian bank and complete the compulsory property purchase (for Silver, Gold and Platinum; a Forest City unit for SEZ).
  5. Collect the MM2H pass once the deposit and property conditions are verified, and register biometrics as required.
  6. Maintain the deposit, the property, and the 90-day annual stay (30 days for Sarawak S-MM2H) for the life of the pass, and renew with a current medical report and health insurance when the term expires.

If you're not American: what changes

The Malaysian side is identical for any eligible foreign applicant. The same four tiers, the same fixed-deposit and property thresholds, and the same 10-year property lock apply whatever passport you hold. Only your home-country tax treatment differs.

If you are not a US citizen, drop the FBAR and Form 8938 discussion above and check three things at home instead. First, whether your country taxes worldwide income or only domestic-source income — a resident of a territorial-tax country may find Malaysia's own foreign-income exemption genuinely useful, unlike a US citizen. Second, whether your country requires reporting of foreign bank accounts or assets — many do, under their own thresholds. Third, whether a double-taxation treaty exists between your country and Malaysia that would affect how any Malaysian-source income, such as rent from the qualifying property, is taxed. Confirm the specifics with a tax adviser in your country of residence before you commit funds.

Is MM2H worth it in 2026?

For the right applicant, yes — but the 2024 relaunch changed who that applicant is. The old MM2H suited someone who wanted a long-stay pass without necessarily owning property; the current version is, by design, a combined residency-and-real-estate commitment, with a deposit that starts at a serious US$150,000 and a compulsory purchase on top. It fits an American who genuinely wants a Southeast Asian base — Kuala Lumpur's cost of living, English-language common-law legal system, and freehold property market are real advantages — and who is comfortable locking up both a large deposit and a property for a decade.

It is a weaker fit if you were hoping for the old low-commitment version, if a compulsory property purchase doesn't suit your plans, or if you want a European base instead — compare it against our best golden visas for Americans. For a lower-commitment entry point specifically into Malaysia, Sarawak's S-MM2H or the Forest City SEZ tier are worth pricing out before committing to Silver, Gold or Platinum.

Frequently asked questions

Do Americans qualify for Malaysia's MM2H program?

Yes. MM2H is open to citizens of countries that maintain diplomatic relations with Malaysia, which includes the United States. There is no friendly-nations-style shortlist to check first, as there is for some other programs — you qualify by meeting one of the four tiers' financial and age requirements, not by nationality alone.

How much money do I need for MM2H in 2026?

It depends on the tier. Silver requires a US$150,000 fixed deposit and a RM600,000 property purchase; Gold requires US$500,000 and a RM1,000,000 property; Platinum requires US$1,000,000 and a RM2,000,000 property. The Forest City/Johor SEZ tier is cheaper: US$65,000 (under 50) or US$32,000 (50 and over), with a compulsory Forest City property purchase. All figures are minimums set by the Ministry of Tourism, Arts and Culture and can change.

Do I have to buy property for MM2H?

Yes, as of the current 2024-restructured program, a property purchase is compulsory at every tier, not optional as it was under the pre-2021 rules. Silver requires a RM600,000+ residence, Gold RM1,000,000+, Platinum RM2,000,000+, and the SEZ tier requires a property specifically in Forest City, Johor. The property cannot be sold for 10 years.

Is Sarawak's S-MM2H the same program as federal MM2H?

No. Sarawak runs its own separate residency program, S-MM2H, administered by the state government rather than the federal Ministry, because Sarawak controls its own immigration under Malaysia's federal structure. It is cheaper (RM500,000 fixed deposit, no compulsory property purchase) and requires only 30 days a year in Sarawak, but the resulting pass is valid for Sarawak, not automatically the rest of Malaysia.

Can I withdraw part of my MM2H fixed deposit?

Yes. Across the Silver, Gold and Platinum tiers, MM2H participants may withdraw up to 50% of the principal fixed-deposit value after approval, for purposes including purchasing a residence, education, and medical or tourism-related expenses in Malaysia. The withdrawn amount does not need to be restored.

Does MM2H create US tax obligations?

Yes, reporting obligations at minimum. A Malaysian fixed deposit of US$150,000 to US$1,000,000 is a foreign financial account that triggers FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) and almost certainly Form 8938. As a US citizen you remain taxed on worldwide income regardless of Malaysian residency; Malaysia's exemption on most foreign-sourced income received by individual tax residents (currently running through 2036) does nothing to reduce your US bill.
