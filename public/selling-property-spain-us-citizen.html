Selling Property in Spain as a US Citizen, 2026

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated November 2026  ·  13 min read
Spain taxes the gain at 19%, and the buyer keeps 3% of the price on the way to the notary. For a non-resident without a permanent establishment, the Agencia Tributaria applies 19% to the gain on Spanish real estate. The buyer withholds 3% of the agreed price and pays it on modelo 211 within one month. You settle the difference, or claim a refund, on modelo 210. Municipal plusvalía is a separate tax on the land, and when the seller is a non-resident individual the buyer is the substitute taxpayer. None of that switches off the US return.

The 19% tax on the gain

A person who sells Spanish real estate without a permanent establishment in Spain is in the Impuesto sobre la Renta de no Residentes. The Agencia Tributaria's page on gains from selling real estate states the rate: 19%. That rate is the one article 25.1.f of the non-resident income-tax law sets for gains that arise when assets are transferred. It is not the 24% general rate the same agency's modelo 210 instructions print for other income of people who do not live in the EU, Iceland, Norway or Liechtenstein. Rent and the annual imputed-income charge can sit at that 24% rate. The gain on the sale does not.

The gain is not the headline price. Modelo 210's instructions, section 210 H, define both sides:

  • Transmission value is the amount the property was transferred for, minus the expenses and taxes of the sale that the seller paid.
  • Acquisition value is the amount the seller paid to acquire it, plus the expenses and taxes of the purchase, excluding interest. Where depreciation was allowable, that acquisition value is reduced by the depreciation the regulations allow.

The difference is the gain. A loss is still reported. The 3% withholding does not disappear just because the arithmetic comes out negative. You file to get it back.

One relief that Americans often read about does not apply to them. The same instructions allow a reinvestment exemption when the property was the seller's habitual home in Spain, but only for residents of another EU state, or of an EEA state with tax-information exchange. From 11 July 2021 that EEA list includes Iceland, Norway and Liechtenstein. A seller whose tax residence is the United States is outside the list. Do not plan the sale around a Spanish rollover you cannot claim. The US Section 121 exclusion is a different statute, discussed below.

The 3% the buyer keeps

Article 25.2 of the non-resident income-tax law, as the Agencia Tributaria restates it, requires the buyer — resident or not — to withhold and pay 3% of the agreed consideration. The payment is on account of the seller's tax on the gain. It is not a second tax, and it is not plusvalía.

The buyer pays that 3% on modelo 211 within one month of the transfer date and gives the seller a copy. The seller then files modelo 210, code 28 in the income-type box for an ordinary real-estate gain, and subtracts the withholding from the 19% bill. If the withholding is larger, the excess is refunded. If the buyer never pays the 3% over, the property itself can be charged for the smaller of the missing withholding and the tax actually due.

The filing window is easy to miscount. The modelo 210 instructions say returns for gains on transmitting real estate are filed, whatever the result, in the three months that start once the one-month period from the transfer date has ended. The buyer's month and the seller's three months are sequential. They are not the same deadline.

Plusvalía, the municipal tax

Plusvalía — the Impuesto sobre el Incremento de Valor de los Terrenos de Naturaleza Urbana — is not the 19% and it is not the 3%. It taxes the increase in the value of urban land, over a maximum of twenty years, and the town collects it.

On a sale for money, article 106.1.b of the Texto Refundido de la Ley Reguladora de las Haciendas Locales makes the seller the taxpayer. Article 106.2 then names a substitute: when that seller is a non-resident individual, the buyer is the substitute taxpayer. The town looks to the buyer for the filing and the payment. A private clause that says the seller will pay does not change who the town can pursue. The buyer who pays can ask the seller to reimburse the amount. A non-resident company is not the individual this article describes. Read the seller's legal form before you assume the substitute rule applies.

Two exits from the tax are in the same law. Article 104.5 says there is no charge when the land value did not increase between acquisition and transfer, if the interested party proves that. Article 107.5 says that if the real increase is smaller than the objective base, the real increase is the base, at the taxpayer's request. A sale at a loss on the building can still produce plusvalía, because the tax looks at the cadastral land value and a coefficient, unless one of those two rules is invoked and proved.

The objective base is the cadastral land value at the date of the transfer, multiplied by the coefficient for how many full years the land was held. The town picks the coefficient in its ordinance. It cannot exceed the maximums in article 107.4. The consolidated text the Boletín Oficial del Estado dates as last modified on 7 October 2026 prints these maximums:

Article 108 caps the town's tax rate at 30% of that base. The quota is the base times the rate. A town may set one rate or a rate for each holding period, and it may allow reductions the statute describes. Article 110 gives 30 business days from an inter-vivos transfer to file. If the flat was on the Costa del Sol or in Valencia, the purchase-tax rules in the Costa del Sol and Valencia guides are the buyer's problem on the way in. They are not the seller's 19%. Keep the purchase invoices anyway. They are the acquisition value.

What the US still taxes

Citizenship does the work. The United States taxes the gain whether or not you live in Spain and whether or not Spain has already taken 19%. You report it in US dollars on Form 8949 and Schedule D. The dollar gain is not the euro gain typed into modelo 210. Basis is the dollar cost when you bought, and the amount realized is the dollar value when you sell. A move in the exchange rate changes the US number even when the euro gain is fixed.

Spanish income tax you actually pay on that gain is the credit on Form 1116. The credit stops at the US tax on the foreign-source gain. It does not refund Spanish tax that exceeds the US tax, except through the credit's own carryback and carryforward. Plusvalía is a foreign tax on the same disposition only to the extent it is an income tax or a tax in lieu of one under US rules. Do not assume the municipal bill is automatically creditable. That is a question for the return, not a line this page will invent.

If the property was your main home for at least two of the five years before the sale, Section 121 can exclude up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 on a joint return. Publication 523 states those amounts and states that the exclusion does not cover gain from depreciation allowed or allowable after 6 May 1997. A holiday home that was never your main home does not qualify. A home you rented uses the 30-year ADS schedule for a residential rental placed in service after 2017, and the depreciation you were allowed to take comes back at a maximum federal rate of 25% even if you never deducted it.

Spain's 19% and the US brackets are not a matched pair. A seller whose US rate on the gain is lower than 19% can have Spanish tax left over after the credit. A seller whose US rate, plus the 3.8% net investment income tax where it applies, is higher than the Spanish tax still pays the IRS the difference. Run both computations in dollars before you accept an offer. The mechanics of the credit are in the foreign tax credit guide, and the sale reporting is in the Form 8949 guide.

Two returns, one closing.

A Spanish conveyance lawyer and a US cross-border CPA are doing different jobs. The notary will not compute Form 1116, and the CPA will not file plusvalía with the town.

Selling step by step

  1. Build the acquisition file. Purchase price, ITP or VAT, notary, registry, and documented improvements. Interest on the mortgage stays out of the Spanish acquisition value.
  2. Ask whether depreciation was allowable. If the flat was rented, both Spain and the United States reduce basis. The US reduction happens even when you skipped the deduction.
  3. Get the cadastral land value and the town's plusvalía ordinance before you negotiate who wires what at completion. On a sale by a non-resident individual, the buyer is the substitute taxpayer.
  4. Price the 3% into the completion statement. The buyer pays modelo 211 within one month of the deed. You need the stamped copy for modelo 210.
  5. Sign the escritura. The notary, the registry, and cancellation of any mortgage happen here. The buying guide walks the same documents from the other side of the table.
  6. File modelo 210 in the three months that follow the buyer's one-month window. Pay the balance or claim the refund. Use code 28 unless a reinvestment code actually applies, which it does not for a US-resident seller.
  7. Report the dollar gain on the US return for the year of the sale. Claim Section 121 only if the use test is met. Claim Form 1116 for Spanish tax paid, in the year the US rules let you claim it.

If you're not American

The Spanish side does not ask for a US passport. The 19% rate, the 3% withholding, modelo 210, and the plusvalía substitute rule for a non-resident individual are Spanish rules. What is American is the dollar computation, Section 121, Form 1116, and the depreciation the Internal Revenue Code requires on a foreign rental.

If you are not a US person, drop that layer and use the rules where you are tax-resident. Three questions matter. Does your home country tax a gain on Spanish real estate? Does it give a credit for the Spanish 19% and, separately, for plusvalía? Does a treaty assign the right to tax immovable property to Spain and leave you a credit at home? The EU/EEA reinvestment exemption is a Spanish rule with a residence test. It is not a US rule, and it is not automatic for every non-Spanish seller.

What to settle before you list

The number that surprises Americans is not the 19%. It is the gap between the 3% kept at the notary and the 19% computed months later, plus a municipal land tax the buyer may have to pay in the seller's name, plus a US gain measured in dollars. Sellers who collect the purchase invoices, the cadastral land value, and a depreciation schedule before they sign an offer can see all three. Sellers who discover the 3% on the day of the escritura are negotiating with money that has already been assigned to modelo 211.

Residency is a separate file. A sale does not preserve a Spanish residence permit, and a purchase has not been a residence route since the investor visa closed. If you still live in Spain on a non-lucrative visa, the day-count that makes you a Spanish tax resident can pull the gain into the resident income tax instead of this non-resident regime. That is a different return. Confirm which one you are in before you choose the 19% form.

Frequently asked questions

What Spanish tax does a US citizen pay on the gain when selling property?

The Agencia Tributaria states that the tax on a capital gain from selling Spanish real estate, for a non-resident without a permanent establishment, is 19%. The gain is the transmission value minus the acquisition value. Transmission value is the amount received minus the seller's expenses and taxes on the sale. Acquisition value is the cost plus purchase expenses and taxes, excluding interest, and reduced by depreciation where that applies.

What is the 3% withholding when a non-resident sells?

The buyer must withhold 3% of the agreed price and pay it to the Treasury on modelo 211 within one month of the transfer. It is a payment on account of the seller's tax, not the final bill. The seller files modelo 210 and subtracts the 3%. If the withholding is larger than the 19% tax, the excess is refunded. If the sale produces a loss, the seller can ask for the whole withholding back.

When is modelo 210 due?

The Agencia Tributaria instructions say the return for a gain on transmitting real estate is filed in the three months that begin once the one-month period from the transfer date has passed. That filing is required whether the result is tax due or a refund.

Who pays municipal plusvalía if the seller does not live in Spain?

On a sale for consideration, the seller is the taxpayer. Article 106.2 of the local-finance law makes the buyer the substitute taxpayer when that seller is a non-resident individual. The buyer then files with the town. The tax is not due if the land value did not rise. The rate cannot exceed 30%, and the filing window for a lifetime transfer is 30 business days.

Can a US citizen use Spain's reinvestment exemption on a main home?

The Agencia Tributaria limits that exemption to residents of another EU state, or of an EEA state with tax-information exchange, which from 11 July 2021 includes Iceland, Norway and Liechtenstein. A seller who is tax-resident in the United States is outside that list. The US Section 121 exclusion is a separate rule and can still apply if the Spanish home was the seller's main home.

Does the United States tax the sale after Spain has taxed it?

Yes. A US citizen reports the gain in dollars on Form 8949 and Schedule D. Spanish tax actually paid on that gain is creditable on Form 1116, up to the US tax on the foreign-source gain. Section 121 can exclude gain on a main home, but it does not shelter gain from depreciation taken after 6 May 1997. A rental uses the foreign-property depreciation rules, and that depreciation is taxed at up to 25% on the way out.
