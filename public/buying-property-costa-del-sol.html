Buying Property on the Costa del Sol as an American (2026): Marbella to Estepona, Taxes and Rules

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated October 2026  ·  12 min read
Americans can buy anywhere on the Costa del Sol outright — Marbella's Golden Mile, Estepona's old town, or an off-plan tower in Fuengirola. The national mechanics from our Spain buying guide apply — NIE, a flat 7% ITP on resale homes, no residency requirement — but Andalucía adds its own layer: a single province-wide tax rate instead of a sliding scale, a genuinely strong legal guarantee on off-plan deposits, and one of Spain's most actively enforced short-term-rental licensing regimes.

The Costa del Sol is the strip of Andalucían coastline — roughly Sotogrande to Málaga city — that draws more American buyers than the rest of Spain combined, and for reasons the generic national guide doesn't cover: a single flat property-transfer tax across the whole province, a genuinely well-functioning legal guarantee on off-plan deposits that most European markets don't match, and a short-term-rental licensing system Andalucía enforces harder than most of its neighbors. This guide is the Costa del Sol-specific layer on top of our general guide to buying property in Spain; read that one for the national process — NIE, the ended Golden Visa, the still-unenacted "100% tax" proposal — this one for what's different on this stretch of coast.

What it actually costs: a €500,000 purchase, worked out

Andalucía did something most Spanish regions haven't: it collapsed its transfer tax into a single flat rate instead of a sliding scale by price band. Under Ley 5/2021, de 20 de octubre, de Tributos Cedidos de la Comunidad Autónoma de Andalucía, a resale home anywhere in the province — Marbella included — pays 7% ITP on the higher of the purchase price or the Catastro's reference value, full stop, with no tiers by price. A new-build bought directly from a developer instead pays 10% IVA plus 1.2% AJD stamp duty. Reduced rates exist but rarely apply to a US buyer: a 3.5% ITP / 0.3% AJD rate for habitual residence purchases under €150,000 by a buyer under 35, and a 3.5% ITP / 0.1% AJD rate for large families or buyers with a disability rating of 33% or more, on a habitual residence up to €250,000 — both require the home to be your actual primary residence, which rules out the vacation-home and investment purchases most Americans make here.

Buying off-plan: the Ley 20/2015 guarantee

The Costa del Sol runs on off-plan sales more than almost anywhere else in Spain — new towers in Estepona and Nueva Andalucía routinely sell out before the first concrete pour — which makes the one piece of Spanish consumer-protection law every off-plan buyer should actually understand worth a dedicated section. Since 1 January 2016, the Disposición Adicional Primera of Ley 20/2015 (the insurance-sector solvency law, LOSSEAR) replaced the older 1968 regime and requires any developer collecting money before the keys are handed over to secure every euro of it from the moment the building licence is granted.

The developer must choose one of two mechanisms, and both have to stay in force for the full construction period:

  • Seguro de caución — an individual insurance policy per buyer, with the developer as policyholder and premium-payer and you as the insured party.
  • Aval bancario — a bank guarantee covering the total amount advanced, issued and maintained for the same period.

If the project stalls, the developer breaches the delivery date, or the building licence is revoked, you formally demand your money back from the developer first; if nothing comes back within 30 days, you claim directly against the insurer or the bank, who must pay out within a further 30 days and only then pursue the developer. A developer who fails to put the guarantee in place at all risks a fine of up to 25% of the unsecured amount — a real deterrent that makes this one of the stronger buyer protections in European off-plan markets. Before you sign anything or wire a deposit on an off-plan Costa del Sol unit, have your lawyer confirm the specific guarantee is in place and in the correct amount; a reservation fee paid before the building licence issues typically falls outside this protection entirely, which is exactly why reservation-stage deposits should stay small.

Can you short-let it? VUT licensing in Andalucía

Andalucía licenses short-term rentals under Decreto 28/2016, de 2 de febrero, as amended by Decreto 31/2024, de 29 de enero (in force from 22 February 2024). Any vivienda de uso turística (VUT) — a whole-home rental offered repeatedly to tourists, typically for stays of under two months — must be registered with the Registro de Turismo de Andalucía before you can legally advertise it, which means filing a declaración responsable confirming the unit meets the regulation's physical standards: at least 14 m² per sleeping place and 25 m² of main floor area, adequate ventilation and blackout, cooling for the May–August stretch and heating for December–March, a second bathroom once you sleep more than five guests, and a capacity cap of 15 places for a whole home (no more than four per bedroom).

The part that catches Costa del Sol buyers off guard is municipal density caps on top of the regional rules: Marbella, Estepona and several neighboring towns can and do limit the number of VUT licences per building, street or zone using published, objective criteria, which means a building that is already saturated with licensed units may refuse a new registration even though your unit otherwise qualifies. Operating without the declaración responsable on file is treated as clandestine tourism activity — a serious infraction carrying fines from €2,001 to €18,000 — and Andalucía has been actively enforcing this, cancelling thousands of unregistered listings regionwide. Confirm the specific building's and municipality's current VUT capacity with your lawyer or the local town hall before you count on short-let income; our national guide to buying property in Spain covers the May 2026 Supreme Court ruling that struck down the separate national rental registry while leaving Andalucía's own regional licensing regime fully intact. Non-resident mortgages on the Costa del Sol

The national picture in our Spain buying guide applies here without modification: Spanish banks lend to non-resident Americans at roughly 60%–70% loan-to-value (a 30%–40% down payment), on Euribor-linked rates, after reviewing US tax returns and income documentation. The Costa del Sol's large international-buyer market means several Spanish banks and specialist brokers run dedicated non-resident desks in Marbella and along the coast that are comparatively quick with foreign paperwork — but the underwriting terms themselves don't differ from the rest of the country. Buying ≠ residency: the NLV and digital-nomad routes

As everywhere in Spain, owning Costa del Sol property does not, by itself, grant residency — Spain repealed its real-estate Golden Visa on 3 April 2025 (see our full assessment in the national guide). If your actual plan is to live on the coast rather than just own a vacation home, the realistic paths are the non-lucrative visa (NLV) — roughly €2,400/month in passive income or savings for a single applicant, plus about €600/month for each additional dependent, tied to the annually set IPREM — or the digital-nomad visa for remote workers. Neither visa is triggered by the purchase itself, and the NLV specifically bars local employment. See our dedicated guide to Spain's non-lucrative visa for the full mechanics, including the renewal and 183-day tax-residency trap.

Step-by-step: closing on a Costa del Sol property

The mechanics mirror the national process in our buying property in Spain guide, with the Costa del Sol-specific checks folded in:

  1. Get your NIE and open a Spanish bank account, expecting a FATCA/W-9 check — identical to the rest of Spain.
  2. Engage an independent lawyer who, for an off-plan purchase, should confirm the Ley 20/2015 guarantee is in place before any deposit beyond a small reservation fee moves, and for a resale unit, verify title and any outstanding community debts.
  3. If you want short-let income, check VUT capacity for the specific building and municipality before you make an offer — a saturated building can make rental projections meaningless regardless of how the unit itself qualifies.
  4. Sign the contrato de arras (deposit contract), typically 10%, binding on both sides under the standard double-or-forfeit terms.
  5. Pay the purchase tax — 7% ITP for resale, or 10% IVA plus 1.2% AJD for new build — around completion.
  6. Sign the escritura before a notary, in person or via power of attorney — see our guide to closing without flying out.
  7. Register the property at the Land Registry (Registro de la Propiedad) in your name.
  8. Set up US tax compliance — FBAR for the new Spanish bank account, and a plan for future rental or resale reporting.

Start to finish, a straightforward resale purchase typically takes two to three months, in line with the national average; an off-plan purchase runs on the developer's construction timeline instead, often a year or more.

The US tax rules that still apply

Nothing about buying specifically on the Costa del Sol changes the IRS side, covered in full in our national guide. In short: the US taxes citizens on worldwide income, so rental income — whether from an ordinary lease or a licensed VUT unit — is reportable on Schedule E, and a future sale is a US capital gain. The foreign tax credit generally offsets most of the double taxation because Spain also taxes the gain. One wrinkle specific to a vacation home you don't rent out: Spain's IRNR imputed-income tax deems you to earn phantom rental income from any property that isn't your main home — 24% tax on 1.1%–2% of the cadastral value for a non-resident American — and because that Spanish tax applies even though the property earned nothing, there is often no corresponding US income for the foreign tax credit to offset, so in practice it is simply a cost to budget for. Your new Spanish bank account can also trigger FBAR and potentially FATCA reporting once balances cross the relevant thresholds.

If you're not American: what changes

Every Spanish-side rule on this page — Andalucía's flat 7% ITP, the Ley 20/2015 off-plan guarantee, VUT licensing, and the Costa del Sol's own town-by-town market — applies to any foreign buyer, regardless of passport. What's specifically American here is the wrapper: worldwide US taxation, FBAR/FATCA, and the US capital-gains treatment on a later sale. If you live outside the US, drop the IRS section above and substitute your own country's rules: whether it taxes foreign rental income and gains, whether it requires foreign-asset or account reporting, and whether a double-taxation treaty with Spain applies. EU/EEA residents, notably, pay a lower 19% rate on Spain's imputed and rental income rather than the 24% Americans pay.

Is buying on the Costa del Sol worth it in 2026?

For the right buyer, yes — and the regional specifics genuinely work in an American's favor more than the national guide suggests. A single flat 7% transfer tax across the whole province is simpler to budget than most of Spain's regional patchwork, the Ley 20/2015 guarantee makes off-plan buying here safer than in many other European new-build markets, and the towns themselves offer real range — Marbella's prestige, Estepona's value, Sotogrande's privacy, Fuengirola's affordability. But price it correctly: the flat ITP applies regardless of neighborhood, VUT capacity in the building you want may already be full, and Spain's imputed-income tax quietly taxes a vacation home even when it earns nothing. Pick a town for what it actually offers, confirm short-let licensing before you count on rental income, and have both a Spanish lawyer and a US cross-border CPA on the file before you close.

Frequently asked questions

Can an American buy property on the Costa del Sol?

Yes, with no restrictions. Spain places no limits on foreign ownership, so a US citizen can buy an apartment, villa or off-plan unit anywhere on the Costa del Sol with the same rights as a Spanish national, without residency or a visa. You need a NIE (foreigner tax number) and, for most of the process, an independent Spanish lawyer.

How much tax do I pay buying property in Marbella or Estepona in 2026?

Andalucía charges a flat 7% ITP transfer tax on resale homes, regardless of price, under Ley 5/2021. A new-build bought from a developer pays 10% IVA plus 1.2% AJD stamp duty instead. Add roughly 1%–1.5% for your lawyer and a few hundred euros for notary and registry, so all-in costs run about 8.5% on a resale and 13% on a new build.

Is my deposit protected if I buy off-plan on the Costa del Sol?

By law, yes. Since Ley 20/2015 took effect on 1 January 2016, any developer taking payments before completion must guarantee them with a bank aval or an individual insurance policy from the moment the building licence is granted. If the project is delayed or abandoned, you can claim your money back from the guarantor directly, and the guarantee must stay in force until the keys are handed over.

Can I rent out my Costa del Sol property short-term?

Only if it is registered as a vivienda de uso turístico (VUT) with the Junta de Andalucía under Decreto 28/2016, as amended by Decreto 31/2024. You file a declaración responsable before advertising, meet occupancy and safety standards, and check your municipality's own cap — Marbella and Estepona both limit VUT density by building or zone. Letting without registering is a serious infraction carrying fines of up to €18,000.

Does buying a home on the Costa del Sol give me Spanish residency?

No. Spain repealed its real-estate Golden Visa on 3 April 2025, so ownership alone no longer grants a residence permit. Americans who want to live on the Costa del Sol apply separately, typically through the non-lucrative visa (NLV, roughly €2,400/month in passive income for a single applicant) or the digital-nomad visa.

Do I owe US tax on a Costa del Sol property?

Potentially, because the US taxes citizens on worldwide income. Rental income is reportable on Schedule E, a future sale is a US capital gain, and the foreign tax credit generally offsets most double taxation since Spain also taxes the gain. A Spanish bank account can trigger FBAR and FATCA reporting, and if the property sits empty, Spain's imputed-income tax (IRNR) applies with no corresponding US income to credit it against.
