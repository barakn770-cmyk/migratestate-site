Form 8938 (FATCA) and Foreign Real Estate: What US Owners Must Report (2026)

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated September 2026  ·  12 min read
A house you own abroad in your own name is not reported on Form 8938. The IRS is explicit that foreign real estate is not a specified foreign financial asset. The trap is the wrapper: put the same house inside a foreign company, partnership or trust and your interest in that entity is reportable, at fair market value, once your specified foreign assets cross the threshold — $50,000 at year end for a single filer living in the US, rising to $400,000 for a couple living abroad. The bank account you use to buy and run the property is reportable on both Form 8938 and the FBAR.

What Form 8938 is — and why it exists alongside the FBAR

Form 8938, Statement of Specified Foreign Financial Assets, is the individual-taxpayer half of FATCA, the Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act of 2010. The statute lives in Internal Revenue Code section 6038D, and the form is attached to your Form 1040 every year in which the total value of your specified foreign financial assets exceeds a threshold that depends on your filing status and on whether you live in the United States or abroad. It is an information return: it raises no tax by itself, but it tells the IRS what you hold overseas and what income those holdings produced.

Americans who own property abroad usually meet the form through a different door. They already know about the FBAR — FinCEN Form 114 — and assume Form 8938 is a duplicate. It is not. The FBAR is a Bank Secrecy Act filing made to FinCEN, triggered by accounts whose aggregate value exceeds $10,000 at any time in the year. Form 8938 is a Treasury/IRS filing under the tax code, triggered by a broader category of assets at much higher thresholds. The IRS states in its own guidance that filing Form 8938 does not relieve you of the separate requirement to file the FBAR. The two overlap, they are cumulative, and a foreign property owner who has one will very often have both.

The question this guide answers is narrower and more useful than "what is FATCA": what, exactly, does owning real estate abroad put on Form 8938? The answer turns almost entirely on how the property is titled.

Directly held real estate: the clean answer

Start with the rule that surprises people in the right direction. The IRS says, in its published Form 8938 questions and answers, that foreign real estate is not a specified foreign financial asset, and that a personal residence or a rental property does not have to be reported on the form. The instructions define the reportable universe as financial accounts maintained by foreign financial institutions and, outside accounts, foreign-issued stock and securities, interests in foreign entities, and financial instruments or contracts with foreign issuers or counterparties. A deed to an apartment in Lisbon or a house in Tulum is none of those.

The consequences follow cleanly. If you hold the property in your own name (or jointly with your spouse, or through a US single-member LLC that is disregarded for federal tax purposes), then the property is not listed on Form 8938, and — this is the part owners miss — its value does not count toward the filing threshold. A retiree whose only foreign asset is a €350,000 villa held directly, with a small local checking account, does not file Form 8938 at all if the account stays below the threshold. The same goes for the foreign mortgage on the villa: a mortgage is a liability, not an asset, so it neither appears on the form nor offsets anything.

Note that "not reportable on Form 8938" is not the same as "invisible to the IRS". The rent still goes on Schedule E, depreciated over 30 years under ADS as we explain in the guide to foreign rental income and US tax, and any gain on sale goes on Form 8949 and Schedule D. Form 8938 is about disclosure of assets, not about whether the income is taxed.

Property through a company, partnership or trust

Now the trap. Many countries push, or effectively require, foreign buyers into a holding vehicle: a Panamanian or Costa Rican sociedad anónima, a Philippine or Thai company structure, an Indonesian PT PMA, a French SCI, a Portuguese or Spanish holding company, or a local trust. The moment the property sits inside a foreign entity, the IRS guidance flips: "If the real estate is held through a foreign entity, such as a corporation, partnership, trust or estate, then the interest in the entity is a specified foreign financial asset." You do not report the house. You report your shares, partnership interest or beneficial interest, and you report them at their fair market value.

Three things follow. First, the entity interest does count toward the threshold, and because it is worth roughly what the property is worth net of the entity's debt, a single holding company can push a US-resident single filer past $50,000 on its own. Second, the entity usually triggers a second information return: Form 5471 for a foreign corporation in which you hold 10% or more, Form 8865 for a foreign partnership, Form 3520 (and 3520-A) for a foreign trust. Third, and helpfully, the Form 8938 instructions provide that an asset you report on a timely filed Form 3520, 5471, 8621 or 8865 for the same year does not have to be detailed again on Form 8938 — you simply identify the other form in Part IV. But the value of that asset still counts toward whether you have to file Form 8938 in the first place.

The practical arithmetic of these structures — the Form 5471 penalty of $10,000 per form per year, the Subpart F and GILTI exposure on rental profit trapped inside a controlled foreign corporation, the US tax you may owe on money you never received — is the subject of our guide to holding property abroad through an LLC vs. personally. Form 8938 is the smallest of those problems, but it is the one that quietly extends the statute of limitations on your entire return, as explained below.

Buying through a local company because "everyone does"?

The foreign entity that makes the purchase easy locally is what puts your property onto Form 8938, Form 5471 and possibly Subpart F. Price the US compliance before you incorporate, not the following April.

The Mexican fideicomiso after Rev. Rul. 2013-14

Mexico is the special case, because the vehicle is compulsory rather than optional. A foreigner buying residential property in the restricted zone — within 50 km of the coast or 100 km of a border — must hold it through a bank fideicomiso, and the word "trust" in the English translation caused years of unnecessary Form 3520 filings and Form 8938 disclosures.

In Revenue Ruling 2013-14 the IRS settled the point. On the facts described — the Mexican bank holds bare legal title to a single identified property, has no duties other than to hold that title and transfer it at the beneficiary's direction, and the beneficiary has all the rights of management, use, income and sale and pays all taxes and liabilities directly — the fideicomiso is not a trust for federal tax purposes, and the beneficiary is treated as the direct owner of the real estate. Directly owned foreign real estate is not a specified foreign financial asset. The ruling therefore takes the standard fideicomiso off Form 8938 and off Form 3520 in one stroke, whether the beneficiary is an individual, a disregarded LLC or a corporation.

The ruling is fact-specific, and that matters. If your trust deed gives the bank discretionary powers, if the fideicomiso holds more than one asset, or if it holds a bank account or an investment portfolio alongside the house, it can fall outside the ruling and back into trust territory. Have a cross-border adviser read the actual contrato de fideicomiso before you decide you are covered. Our guides to buying property in Mexico and US taxes on Mexican property cover the wider picture.

Thresholds by filing status and residence

Form 8938 is required only if you are a specified individual — a US citizen, a resident alien, or a nonresident alien who elects to be treated as a resident — and you are required to file an income tax return, and the total value of your specified foreign financial assets exceeds the applicable threshold. The instructions set two tiers, and the difference between them is the single largest planning lever the form offers.

Filing statusLiving in the United StatesLiving abroad (presence-abroad test)
Unmarried> $50,000 at year end, or > $75,000 at any time> $200,000 at year end, or > $300,000 at any time
Married filing jointly> $100,000 at year end, or > $150,000 at any time> $400,000 at year end, or > $600,000 at any time
Married filing separately> $50,000 at year end, or > $75,000 at any time> $200,000 at year end, or > $300,000 at any time

You "live abroad" for this purpose if you meet the presence-abroad test: you are a US citizen who has been a bona fide resident of a foreign country for an uninterrupted period that includes the entire tax year, or a US citizen or resident who is physically present in a foreign country for at least 330 full days during any 12-month period ending in the tax year. These are the same yardsticks as the foreign earned income exclusion, and an American who has genuinely relocated to their property abroad usually qualifies — quadrupling the threshold. An American who keeps a US home and spends five months a year at the foreign property does not.

Two further points. The thresholds are stated in the instructions and are not indexed for inflation; the $50,000 figure has been the same since the form's first year in 2011. And specified domestic entities — closely held US corporations and partnerships, and certain domestic trusts, formed or used to hold foreign assets — have their own filing duty at the $50,000 / $75,000 level regardless of where their owners live. A US LLC that is not disregarded and holds shares in a foreign property company can be a filer in its own right.

The accounts around the property

Even when the property itself is off the form, the plumbing around it rarely is. Any deposit or custodial account maintained by a foreign financial institution is a specified foreign financial asset: the local checking account you opened to pay the notary, the account that receives the rent, the account your property manager sweeps expenses from, a foreign brokerage account holding the sale proceeds while you decide what to do with them. Each is reported at its maximum value during the year, and each counts toward the threshold. So does a foreign-issued life insurance or annuity contract with cash value, and an interest in a foreign pension or deferred-compensation plan.

The list of what is not covered is just as useful. An account at a foreign branch of a US bank is not reported on Form 8938 (though it is on the FBAR). An account at a US branch of a foreign bank is reported on neither. Foreign currency held directly — cash in a drawer — is not a specified asset. A safe deposit box is not a financial account. Precious metals held directly are not covered. And the property's own mortgage, as noted, is a liability outside the form.

The grey zone owners ask about most is the escrow or client account a foreign notary or lawyer holds during the purchase. If the funds sit in the professional's own trust account, you may not have a "financial account" of your own at a foreign financial institution for Form 8938 purposes — but you may still have a financial interest for FBAR purposes, which applies a broader test. Treat it as a question for your adviser rather than an assumption, and keep the closing statements that show how long the money sat there and where.

Valuation, exchange rates and Part III income

Form 8938 asks for the maximum value of each asset during the year, in US dollars. For an account, the instructions let you rely on periodic statements and, for currency conversion, on the rate shown on a statement the institution issues at least annually. Otherwise you convert at the US Treasury Bureau of the Fiscal Service exchange rate on the last day of the tax year, even for the "any time during the year" maximum. That is a different convention from the FBAR, which also uses the Treasury year-end rate but on a calendar-year basis and with its own rounding practice, and different again from Schedule E, where rental receipts are converted at the rate on the day received or at a yearly average. Keep the three conventions straight in your file.

For an interest in a foreign entity holding real estate, the value is the fair market value of your interest on the last day of the year. The instructions allow a reasonable estimate where you do not have a formal appraisal, and an owner of 100% of a single-asset company will generally use the property's market value less the company's debt. For a beneficial interest in a foreign trust, the maximum value is the value of cash or property actually distributed to you during the year plus the value of your right to receive mandatory distributions — which is why a discretionary trust that paid you nothing can carry a reported value of zero while still being disclosed.

Part III of the form then asks for the income attributable to each asset and where it appears on your return. For a foreign rental company that is a foreign corporation, that is the dividend income (or the Subpart F inclusion) you reported; for the account that collects the rent, it is the interest; for a foreign partnership, the Schedule K-1-style share. This is the mechanism by which Form 8938 does its real work: it lets the IRS reconcile the asset to the income line and notice when one exists without the other.

In plain terms: for most direct owners, Form 8938 is triggered by the bank accounts, not by the house. For owners who used a local company or trust, it is triggered by the company, and the house's full value walks onto the form through the back door.

Penalties and the statute of limitations that never closes

The penalty schedule in section 6038D is modest by the standards of Form 5471 or the FBAR, but it is not the real cost. A failure to file a complete and correct Form 8938 by the due date of the return (including extensions) carries a $10,000 penalty. If the failure continues for more than 90 days after the IRS mails a notice, an additional $10,000 accrues for each 30-day period, capped at a further $50,000. Reasonable cause is a defence, but the instructions note that the fact that a foreign jurisdiction would impose a civil or criminal penalty for disclosing the asset is not reasonable cause.

The second layer bites on the income side. If you underpay tax and the underpayment is attributable to an undisclosed specified foreign financial asset, the accuracy-related penalty is 40% of the underpayment rather than the usual 20%. Unreported rent flowing through an unreported foreign company is the textbook case.

The third layer is the one advisers worry about most. If you fail to file Form 8938, or omit an asset you should have reported, the statute of limitations for the entire tax year stays open — for all or part of the return — until three years after the date you finally file the form. And where you omit more than $5,000 of gross income relating to specified foreign financial assets, the IRS can assess tax for that year at any time within six years after the return was filed. In practice this means a return with a missing Form 8938 is never closed, and every other position on it stays exposed to audit indefinitely. That, far more than the $10,000, is why the form matters.

Missed a year — or several?

A late Form 8938 is usually fixable through the streamlined procedures or a delinquent-information-return filing with reasonable cause, but the route depends on whether tax was underpaid. We'll connect you with cross-border advisers who clean this up routinely.

Read the two real-estate rows together and the pattern is clear. Neither regime cares about a directly held house. Both care about the account that bought it. Only Form 8938 cares about the company or trust that holds it — which is why an American in Panama or Costa Rica who was told "the corporation makes it simpler" ends up with a Form 8938 obligation their neighbour in Portugal, holding the same value of property in their own name, does not have.

How to work through it, step by step

  1. Confirm how the property is titled. Own name or a disregarded US LLC: the property is off the form. Foreign corporation, partnership, trust, estate, or a fideicomiso that does not fit Rev. Rul. 2013-14: the interest is on the form.
  2. Establish which threshold applies. Work out your filing status and whether you meet the presence-abroad test for the year; the answer moves the line from $50,000 to as much as $600,000.
  3. List every specified asset, not just the property-related ones. Foreign accounts, foreign entity interests, foreign pensions, foreign insurance with cash value, directly held foreign stock. The threshold is tested on the total.
  4. Value each asset at its maximum for the year in US dollars, using account statements where available and the Treasury year-end rate otherwise. Value an entity interest at fair market value net of the entity's debt.
  5. Check the Part IV shortcut. Assets already detailed on a timely Form 3520, 5471, 8621 or 8865 are identified in Part IV rather than re-listed, but still count toward the threshold.
  6. Complete Part III so that the income from each asset ties to the line on your return where you reported it.
  7. File the FBAR separately with FinCEN if your foreign accounts exceeded $10,000 in aggregate at any point; Form 8938 does not replace it.
  8. Keep the file. Deed, entity documents, year-end statements, exchange-rate printouts and the fideicomiso contract. The three-year and six-year clocks run from the filing, and the evidence is what closes them.

If you're not American: what changes

Form 8938 is a purely US rule. It applies to US citizens, green card holders and other US tax residents — including a non-American spouse who elects to file jointly with a US citizen — and to no one else. The property, the local company and the local bank account are the same for every foreign buyer; only the disclosure wrapper is American.

If you are not a US person, ignore FATCA and look instead at your own country's foreign asset disclosure regime, because most developed countries now have one and several are stricter than the US on real estate specifically. Spain's Modelo 720 requires resident taxpayers to declare foreign real estate, accounts and securities above €50,000 per category. France requires resident taxpayers to declare foreign bank accounts and life insurance policies with the annual return, and its wealth tax on real estate reaches property held abroad. Canada's Form T1135 requires disclosure of specified foreign property above C$100,000 in cost, which, unlike Form 8938, does include directly held foreign rental real estate (though not a property held primarily for personal use). Check three things with an adviser where you are resident: whether directly held real estate abroad is disclosable at home, whether an interest in a foreign holding company is, and whether the local bank account is — and what the penalties are for silence. The answers differ from the US ones in every direction.

The bottom line for 2026

Form 8938 is not, for most American property owners, the filing they fear. A house or apartment abroad held in your own name is outside it, and the fideicomiso that Mexican law forces on you is outside it too, provided the trust deed is the plain-vanilla kind. What brings owners inside the form is almost always something adjacent to the property: the bank account with the rent in it, the brokerage account holding the sale proceeds, or the local company that a developer or lawyer recommended because it made the closing easier.

That last one deserves the most thought before you buy, because it is the decision that cannot be unwound cheaply. A foreign entity converts a non-reportable asset into a reportable one, adds a second and often a third information return, and can move rental profit into Subpart F or GILTI territory — all for a convenience that the local market usually offers other ways to achieve. Decide the ownership structure with a US cross-border adviser before the deposit, treat the accounts around the property as the real Form 8938 trigger, file the FBAR separately every year, and the form becomes what it should be: a routine attachment, not an open-ended audit invitation.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to report my foreign house or apartment on Form 8938?

Not if you own it directly in your own name. The IRS states that foreign real estate is not a specified foreign financial asset, so a directly held home, rental apartment or plot of land abroad is not reported on Form 8938 and its value does not count toward the filing threshold. The picture changes completely if the property is held through a foreign corporation, partnership, trust or estate: then your interest in that entity is a specified foreign financial asset and is reportable, valued at its fair market value.

What are the Form 8938 filing thresholds for 2026?

For a specified individual living in the United States: more than $50,000 in specified foreign financial assets on the last day of the year or more than $75,000 at any time during the year if unmarried or married filing separately, and more than $100,000 or $150,000 respectively if married filing jointly. For someone who meets the presence-abroad test: more than $200,000 at year end or $300,000 at any time if unmarried or filing separately, and more than $400,000 or $600,000 if filing jointly. The thresholds are set in the Form 8938 instructions and are not indexed for inflation.

Is a Mexican fideicomiso reportable on Form 8938?

Usually not, provided it fits the facts of Rev. Rul. 2013-14. In that ruling the IRS held that a Mexican land trust in which the bank's only duties are to hold legal title and transfer it at the beneficiary's direction is not a trust for US federal tax purposes; the beneficiary is treated as owning the real estate directly. Directly owned foreign real estate is not a specified foreign financial asset, so neither Form 8938 nor Form 3520 is triggered by the fideicomiso itself. If the bank has wider powers or the trust holds other assets, the analysis can change, so have the deed reviewed.

Does filing Form 8938 mean I can skip the FBAR?

No. The two filings are separate and cumulative. Form 8938 is a Treasury/IRS form attached to your income tax return under Internal Revenue Code section 6038D; the FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) is filed electronically with FinCEN under the Bank Secrecy Act and is never attached to a tax return. Their thresholds differ, and the lists of what they cover overlap but are not identical. The IRS states expressly that filing Form 8938 does not relieve you of the requirement to file the FBAR. A foreign bank account used to buy or run your property typically goes on both.

What are the penalties for not filing Form 8938?

A $10,000 penalty for failing to file a complete and correct Form 8938 by the due date, plus, if the failure continues more than 90 days after the IRS mails a notice, $10,000 for each 30-day period up to an additional $50,000. An underpayment of tax attributable to an undisclosed specified foreign financial asset is subject to a 40 percent accuracy-related penalty rather than the usual 20 percent. The statute of limitations on the whole return can also stay open until three years after the form is eventually filed, and six years where more than $5,000 of income from foreign assets was omitted.

Does the foreign mortgage on my property reduce what I report?

A mortgage is a liability, not an asset, so it is not reported on Form 8938 and does not net against anything. If the property is held directly there is nothing to report in any case. If it is held through a foreign company, you report the fair market value of your interest in the company; the mortgage matters only insofar as it reduces the value of that interest, which is how the entity's shares are worth less when it carries debt. The mortgage does create a separate hazard for Americans: a Section 988 currency gain on repayment, covered in our foreign mortgage guide.
