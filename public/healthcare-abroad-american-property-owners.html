Healthcare Abroad for American Property Owners (2026): Medicare Doesn't Travel — What Does

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated October 2026  ·  11 min read

Medicare generally does not pay for care outside the United States — there is no "Mexico border exception" for routine care, and Medicare Advantage and Part D plans stop working the moment you leave their US service area. What replaces it depends entirely on the country: Portugal opens its public SNS to any resident permit holder, Spain requires private insurance up front and only lets you buy into the public system after a year of residence, Greece ties residency to a private policy with no public-system shortcut, Costa Rica makes CCSS enrollment mandatory for every resident, Mexico offers a voluntary IMSS buy-in, and Panama has no comparable public route at all. Plan the insurance decision alongside the property purchase, not after you've already moved.

This article is informational and does not constitute legal, tax, medical, or insurance advice. Healthcare-system rules, visa requirements, and program costs change by country and by year, and your facts matter. Confirm current requirements with an immigration attorney, a licensed insurance broker in the destination country, and a US cross-border tax advisor before you buy a property, apply for residency, or cancel US coverage.

Why Medicare stops at the US border

The single fact every American buying property abroad needs to internalize early: Medicare was built for a country, not a passport. Medicare.gov states it without qualification — Original Medicare "usually doesn't cover health care while you're traveling outside the U.S." The handful of named exceptions are narrower than most retirees assume: a genuinely US-based emergency where the closest adequate hospital happens to sit across the Canadian border, emergency transit through Canada on the most direct route between Alaska and another US state, and care on a cruise ship docked in or within six hours of a US port. There is no general exception for Mexico, and none for simply living abroad full time — a surprisingly common misconception that this guide exists partly to correct.

Two further traps compound the problem for anyone actually relocating rather than just visiting. First, Medicare Advantage and Part D plans are built around a US service area — the official CMS guidance for citizens living abroad confirms that enrollment in these plans requires residing in the plan's coverage footprint, so they are, in practical terms, unusable once you've moved overseas full time. Second, dropping Part B to stop paying a premium you can't use triggers a late-enrollment penalty if you ever come back and re-enroll: a 10% surcharge for every full 12-month period you went without it, applied for the rest of your life, not just for the gap. Keeping or dropping Part B while abroad is therefore a real financial decision, not a formality, and worth modeling with a Medicare specialist before you leave rather than after.

Portugal: the SNS opens to any resident

Portugal is the most straightforward of the six. The official government guidance confirms that any foreign national holding a valid residence authorization can request a número de utente — a user number — from the SNS (Serviço Nacional de Saúde) at a public health unit on a first visit, without needing a health-specific visa category. For the SNS to actually cover your costs, that number needs to be linked to your Portuguese tax number (NIF), a Portuguese address, and a residence authorization with a current validity date — in other words, the paperwork trail from your residency application does double duty. This applies whichever route got you the permit — our Portugal D7 visa guide covers the most common path for retirees and remote-income holders. Many residents still carry a modest private top-up policy for shorter wait times or English-speaking private clinics, but it's a comfort purchase layered on public coverage, not a replacement for it.

Spain: private first, public later

Spain runs the opposite sequence. Official consulate documentation for the non-lucrative visa and most other residence routes requires a private or public health policy from an insurer authorized to operate in Spain, covering the full period of the requested residency with no reduction in benefits when it renews — this is a hard visa-approval condition, checked before you ever set foot in the country. (Some guides describe this policy as needing "no co-pays or deductibles"; that specific phrasing did not appear in the official consulate requirement documents checked for this article, so treat it as a common broker simplification rather than a confirmed legal rule, and have your insurer or attorney confirm the exact policy terms Spain will accept.) Once you've held 12 continuous months of registered residence (empadronamiento at your local town hall) you become eligible for the convenio especial — a paid buy-in to the Spanish public system (Sistema Nacional de Salud) priced, per the Ministry of Health's own schedule, at roughly €60 a month under 65 and €157 a month from 65 onward, with no separate copay on top. See our Spain non-lucrative visa guide for how the insurance requirement interacts with the rest of the NLV application.

Greece: private insurance, no public shortcut

Greece ties its residence programs directly to private coverage rather than to the public system. The official Golden Visa portal requires a private insurance contract covering health and safety risks both at initial issuance and at every renewal — there is no provision allowing residency alone to unlock EFKA (Greece's public social-security and health fund). EFKA access generally runs through employment or direct contributions, which is a different track from simply holding a residence permit as a property-based or income-based applicant. Budget for an ongoing private policy as a fixed cost of Greek residency, not a one-time visa-application expense — our Greece FIP visa guide covers the insurance condition alongside the program's income thresholds.

Costa Rica: CCSS is mandatory

Costa Rica takes the opposite approach from Greece: public enrollment isn't optional. Costa Rican immigration law requires both permanent residents and temporary residents to keep proving active CCSS (Caja Costarricense de Seguro Social) enrollment as a condition of renewing their residency card at all — miss it, and your immigration status is at risk, not just your health coverage. The monthly contribution is calculated as a percentage of your declared income under CCSS's own internal schedule rather than a flat fee set in the immigration statute itself, so the exact amount you'll pay is something to confirm directly with CCSS or an immigration attorney once you have a residency application underway — our Costa Rica Pensionado & Rentista visa guide walks through how the CCSS requirement interacts with the pension-based residency route specifically.

Mexico: IMSS's voluntary buy-in

Mexico offers a genuinely different model: a voluntary public-insurance product open for direct purchase rather than tied to employment or triggered automatically by residency. IMSS's Seguro de Salud para la Familia program, per IMSS's own published fee schedule effective March 2026, runs from roughly 9,300 Mexican pesos a year for a child up to 22,150 pesos a year at age 80 and above, and covers medical-surgical care, pharmacy and hospitalization, subject to IMSS's own list of pre-existing-condition exclusions and waiting periods. One caveat worth flagging honestly: IMSS's own program materials describe it as being for families living in Mexico and for Mexicans living abroad, without explicitly addressing a non-Mexican foreign national holding Mexican residency — the program does not appear to exclude you, but confirm eligibility directly with a local IMSS office before counting on it as your primary coverage. Our Mexico property guide covers residency routes that commonly pair with this option.

Panama: private is the default

Panama is the outlier on the list: no official government source describing a public-healthcare-via-residency route for foreign retirees or residents, comparable to Costa Rica's CCSS mandate or Mexico's IMSS buy-in, could be located for this guide. That is an absence of evidence rather than a confirmed "there is no such program" — if one exists, it wasn't published anywhere this research could reach — but in practice, private international health insurance is the overwhelming market norm for American residents in Panama, Pensionado visa holders included. The upside is that Panama's famous statutory discount package for Pensionado visa holders (covered in our Panama Pensionado visa guide) already cuts 15–20% off many medical, dental and hospital bills by law, which meaningfully softens the cost of paying privately.

The US tax angle: premiums, Schedule A and the ACA

Whatever you pay for coverage abroad has a US tax dimension most property buyers never think to check. IRS Publication 502 confirms that premiums for health insurance generally qualify as a deductible medical expense — the rule is not restricted to policies from US insurers — but like all medical expenses it is only deductible as an itemized deduction on Schedule A, and only to the extent your total qualifying medical expenses exceed 7.5% of your adjusted gross income. For most taxpayers who take the standard deduction instead, that means no practical tax benefit from the premium at all; model it with a cross-border CPA before assuming it offsets the cost.

What you generally cannot do once you've relocated is buy coverage on the ACA marketplace. Healthcare.gov's own eligibility rules require that you live in the United States, and marketplace plans are built to cover providers inside the US — not a policy designed with a move abroad in mind. The federal individual-mandate penalty has been $0 nationally since 2019, but a handful of states still run their own mandate with a real penalty for residents who go without qualifying coverage — Massachusetts confirms its own penalty schedule for 2026 directly on mass.gov, and several other states (California, New Jersey, Rhode Island, and the District of Columbia) are commonly cited as running similar state-level mandates; if you still maintain tax residency or a legal domicile in one of those states while living abroad, confirm your specific obligation with that state's own health-insurance authority rather than assuming the federal $0 penalty is the whole story.

Step-by-step: building your coverage plan

  1. Decide if you're keeping Medicare Part B. Model the lifetime 10% late-enrollment penalty against the premium savings with a Medicare specialist before you drop it.
  2. Confirm your destination's visa-stage insurance rule. Spain and Greece require an approved private policy before the visa is issued at all — shop this early, not after approval.
  3. Check whether public enrollment is mandatory, optional, or unavailable. Costa Rica makes CCSS a condition of residency; Mexico and Spain offer it as a later buy-in; Greece and Panama don't offer a comparable public route to a foreign resident.
  4. Budget the ongoing cost as a recurring line item, not a closing cost — public buy-ins and private premiums both renew annually (or more often) for as long as you stay.
  5. Model the US tax side — whether the premium clears the 7.5%-of-AGI floor, and whether you retain a state tax residency with its own individual mandate.
  6. Get local confirmation before you rely on anything here — a licensed insurance broker and immigration attorney in the destination country, not a guide like this one, should confirm the exact current rule for your specific visa category.

If you're not American: what changes

The country-by-country healthcare mechanics above are identical for any foreign resident. SNS, convenio especial, EFKA, CCSS and IMSS all apply the same way regardless of your passport. What's purely American is the Medicare, Schedule A and ACA layer.

If you're not a US citizen, drop the Medicare and IRS-specific sections above and swap in your own country's rules. Check three things: (1) whether your home country's public health system (or a private top-up you already carry) continues to cover you once you establish tax residency elsewhere; (2) whether your home country has a reciprocal healthcare or social-security agreement with the country you're moving to, which can sometimes substitute for a separate local enrollment; and (3) whether your home country taxes or otherwise treats foreign health-insurance premiums differently from the US rules described here. A Canadian, Brit, or Australian retiree runs a genuinely different healthcare-and-tax calculation than an American on the exact same Costa Rican or Portuguese policy.

Is planning healthcare abroad worth the extra step?

Yes — because the alternative is discovering the gap only when you need care. Medicare's silence outside the US is absolute enough that treating it as "probably fine" is the single most common and most expensive assumption American property buyers make abroad. The good news is that every one of the six countries in this guide has a credible, known path to coverage — public, private, or mandatory — once you know which one applies to your specific visa and residency route. Price it alongside the property, not after the closing.

Frequently asked questions

Does Medicare cover me if I retire abroad?

Almost never. Medicare.gov states that Original Medicare usually doesn't cover care outside the US, with only narrow exceptions (a US-based emergency where a foreign hospital is closer, and emergency Canada transit between Alaska and another US state) — there is no general Mexico exception. Medicare Advantage and Part D are tied to a US service area and are effectively unusable abroad.

How do I get public healthcare in Portugal as a resident?

Any valid Portuguese residence permit lets you request a número de utente from the SNS at a public health unit — no separate health visa category is needed. Link it to your NIF and Portuguese address, and the public system covers you alongside residents.

Does Spain require private health insurance for a visa?

Yes — the non-lucrative visa and most residence routes require a private or public policy from a Spain-authorized insurer covering the full residency period. After 12 continuous months of registered residence, you can usually buy into the public system through the convenio especial.

Is Costa Rica's CCSS mandatory for residents?

Yes. Both permanent and temporary residents must keep proving active CCSS enrollment to renew their residency card — it's a legal condition of the immigration status itself, with the monthly contribution set as a percentage of declared income.

Can I join Mexico's IMSS as a foreign resident?

IMSS's voluntary Seguro de Salud para la Familia runs roughly 9,300–22,150 Mexican pesos a year by age as of March 2026, covering medical, surgical, pharmacy and hospital care. IMSS's own materials describe it for families in Mexico and Mexicans abroad without explicitly addressing non-Mexican foreign residents — confirm eligibility locally before relying on it.

Can I deduct foreign health insurance premiums on my US taxes?

Generally yes, as a Schedule A itemized medical expense over the 7.5%-of-AGI floor (IRS Publication 502), regardless of whether the insurer is foreign or domestic — though only if you itemize instead of taking the standard deduction. ACA marketplace plans, by contrast, aren't available once you're living abroad.
