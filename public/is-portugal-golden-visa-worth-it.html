Is the Portugal Golden Visa Still Worth It in 2026? An Attorney's Honest Assessment

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated October 2026  ·  13 min read

Yes, but only for a narrower buyer than the brochures imply. The Portugal Golden Visa still delivers what it always promised — Schengen access and an EU option on roughly 7 days a year of presence — for an American who wants that without relocating. What changed in 2026 is the fine print: the citizenship clock is now 10 years, not 5; the fund route carries a real US tax trap (PFIC status and Form 8621); AIMA's backlog can add a year or more before that clock even starts; and the real-estate shortcut is gone for good. If your actual goal is to live in Portugal, the D7 visa gets you the same eventual passport for a few hundred euros instead of half a million. What actually changed by 2026

Three things happened to the Portugal Golden Visa between 2023 and 2026, and most of the content still ranking for this topic online was written before at least two of them. First, in October 2023, the real-estate investment route was removed entirely — the program that most Americans still picture, "buy a €500K apartment and get an EU visa," has not existed for three years. Second, the processing agency itself changed: AIMA (Agência para a Integração, Migrações e Asilo) replaced the old SEF in a 2023 reorganization and inherited — and, by most accounts, grew — a serious application backlog. Third, and most consequential for anyone buying the program for its endpoint rather than its journey: Lei Orgânica n.º 1/2026, in force since 19 May 2026, roughly doubled the naturalization timeline for most non-EU applicants, Americans included, from 5 years to 10.

None of this makes the program bad. It makes the program different from the one that built its reputation, and our job here is to tell you honestly what you're actually buying in October 2026 — not what you'd have bought in 2019.

The PFIC trap most brochures skip

This is the single most expensive line item nobody selling a fund mentions, and it has nothing to do with Portugal — it is purely a US tax rule. A Golden Visa qualifying fund earns its income from dividends, interest, capital gains, and rents rather than an active trade or business, which makes it, for US tax purposes, almost always a Passive Foreign Investment Company (PFIC). That status triggers annual Form 8621 filing, and — critically — if you do nothing, the default "excess distribution" regime taxes your eventual gain at top ordinary rates plus a punitive interest charge computed back across your entire holding period, which can push your effective rate on the gain above 50%.

The fix is usually a QEF (Qualified Electing Fund) election, which taxes your pro-rata share of the fund's income annually at ordinary rates whether or not it's distributed — materially better than the default regime, but it requires the fund manager to furnish an annual PFIC information statement, and not every fund will. Ask that question, in writing, before you subscribe, not after your first Form 8621 is due. A €500,000 investment that returns well in euros can still be a poor outcome in dollars after US tax, if the PFIC question was never asked.

AIMA's backlog: the second hidden cost

Even applicants who get the investment and the US tax side right still run into a timeline most marketing understates. As of 2026, applicants commonly report 6 to 24 months from filing to a biometrics appointment, and a further 6 to 18 months from biometrics to physical card issuance — a realistic total of roughly one to well over three years, not the few months implied by statutory processing targets. A consortium of law firms filed a formal ombudsman complaint on behalf of more than a thousand affected applicants during 2026, and complaints against AIMA more broadly have continued to climb.

This matters for a reason beyond frustration: because the citizenship clock now starts only when your first residence card is physically issued (see below), a slow AIMA queue doesn't just delay your card — it pushes your entire naturalization timeline back by the same amount, on top of the 10 years the law itself now requires.

The citizenship math: 10 years, not 5

Historically, Golden Visa holders could apply for citizenship after 5 years — one of the program's signature selling points. Lei Orgânica n.º 1/2026, in force since 19 May 2026, extended that to 10 years of legal residence for most non-EU nationals (7 years for EU/EEA and CPLP nationals), counted from the date your first residence card is issued — not your application or investment date. Applications filed with the IRN on or before 18 May 2026 remain under the old 5-year rule; everyone else is on the new clock, and Portugal's Constitutional Court upheld the core extension in December 2025 while striking down a narrower provision on automatic revocation. A2-level Portuguese and a new civic-knowledge test now sit on top of the residence requirement.

The part worth internalizing: 10 years is the floor, not the ceiling. Add AIMA's current one-to-three-year card-issuance timeline before the clock even starts, and a 2026 applicant is realistically looking at 11 to 13 years from investment to passport eligibility — a very different commitment than "5 years" still implies in most search results. The good news sitting underneath that number: permanent residence remains available at year 5, under the unchanged Aliens Act, giving you nearly everything short of the passport itself — indefinite residence and work rights, just not the EU-citizen voting rights or the passport's own visa-free network.

What the 7-day rule is actually worth

The program's enduring, genuine selling point is the minimal physical-presence requirement: an average of about 7 days a year (14 days across each 2-year permit). No other EU residency-by-investment route this site covers asks so little of your actual calendar. For an American who wants to keep their US job, their US home, and their US tax residency, while holding a standing option to relocate to an EU country on short notice — or simply to travel Schengen-wide without visa friction — that is a real and somewhat unusual product. It is not, and was never meant to be, a fast or cheap route to a passport. Conflating the two is where most buyer disappointment in this program originates.

The alternative nobody selling a Golden Visa mentions

If your honest answer to "will you actually live in Portugal" is yes, the D7 visa reaches the identical 10-year (or 7-year) citizenship clock, under the identical Lei Orgânica 1/2026 rules, for roughly €920/month in demonstrated passive income and a few hundred euros in fees — no €500,000 at investment risk, no PFIC exposure, no fund due diligence. The trade is real: the D7 requires you to actually reside in Portugal (broadly, no more than six consecutive or eight non-consecutive months away per permit cycle), where the Golden Visa asks for a week a year. For someone who was never going to use the "don't have to relocate" feature anyway, that trade overwhelmingly favors the D7. If you decide to proceed

  1. Separate the two decisions. Decide first whether you want to relocate (favors D7/D8) or keep optionality without relocating (favors the Golden Visa) — don't let the investment size make this decision for you.
  2. Price the PFIC question before you wire anything. Get the fund manager's QEF-statement commitment in writing and have a cross-border CPA model both election paths against the default regime.
  3. Ask your lawyer for their office's current, specific AIMA wait times — not a national average — and budget 1–3 years to card issuance, not the statutory target.
  4. Confirm the current AIMA fee schedule directly before budgeting; Portuguese government fee tables are adjusted periodically.
  5. Track the citizenship clock from card issuance, not investment date, and build your 10-to-13-year expectation around AIMA's actual pace, not the law's floor.

If you're not American: what changes

The investment routes, AIMA process, fees, backlog and the 2026 citizenship timeline are identical for every nationality. What's specifically American here is the PFIC/Form 8621 analysis — most other countries don't have an equivalent punitive default regime for foreign passive funds.

If you're not a US citizen, drop the PFIC section entirely and check instead: (1) whether your home country taxes foreign investment income or gains while you hold the fund, and how; (2) whether your home country has its own version of a punitive default regime for offshore funds (several do, under different names); and (3) the citizenship-clock distinction between EU/EEA nationals (7 years) and everyone else (10 years) under Lei Orgânica 1/2026, which applies by your nationality, not your residence.

Our bottom line

The Portugal Golden Visa in 2026 is a legitimate, still-valuable product for the specific buyer who wants EU optionality without relocating and can absorb a six-figure, multi-year commitment with real investment risk attached. It is a poor fit for anyone who actually wants to move to Portugal soon, anyone expecting a fast passport, and anyone who hasn't asked their fund manager the PFIC question. Go in with the 2026 numbers on this page, not a 2022 blog post, and the honest verdict is: worth it for the right buyer, overpriced and over-promised for everyone else.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Portugal Golden Visa still worth it in 2026?

For a narrower buyer than the marketing suggests: yes. If you want EU optionality without relocating — Schengen access now, a path to residency later, citizenship eventually — it still delivers that for about 7 days a year of presence. It stops being worth it if you're buying it as a disguised real-estate play (closed since 2023) or as a fast, cheap route to citizenship (it's neither, once AIMA's backlog and fund costs are counted).

How long does it really take to become a Portuguese citizen through the Golden Visa now?

Under Lei Orgânica n.º 1/2026 (in force 19 May 2026), it's 10 years of legal residence for most non-EU nationals, counted from first-card issuance — not application or investment date. With AIMA's current backlog added before the clock starts, the realistic span is often 11 to 13 years, not the 10 the statute implies on paper.

What is the single biggest hidden cost of the Golden Visa fund route for Americans?

US PFIC tax treatment, not the government fees. Nearly every qualifying fund is a Passive Foreign Investment Company, triggering annual Form 8621 filing; without a timely QEF election, the default regime can push your effective rate on the gain above 50%. Fund management and performance fees compound on top of that — and none of it shows up in the headline €500,000 figure.

Who should pick the D7 visa instead of the Golden Visa?

Anyone genuinely willing to live in Portugal. The D7 reaches the same 10-year citizenship clock for a few hundred euros in fees plus about €11,040/year in demonstrated passive income — against €500,000 at real investment risk. The Golden Visa only earns its premium if staying US-based while keeping a Portuguese option open is the actual goal.

Does the Golden Visa still make sense if AIMA's backlog is this bad?

Depends on the goal. For a long-term EU option where you can tolerate the card arriving one to three years late, the backlog is an inconvenience, not a dealbreaker. If the goal was a fast citizenship timeline, the backlog compounds directly with the new 10-year clock and makes the program considerably less attractive than it looks on paper.
