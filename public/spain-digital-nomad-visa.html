Spain Digital Nomad Visa for Americans: The 2026 Guide

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated November 2026  ·  12 min read
Spain's international-telework residence visa — universally called the "digital nomad visa" — is now the main relocation route for Americans who want to live in Spain on income they earn remotely, now that the investor Golden Visa has been abolished. The income bar is 200% of Spain's minimum wage (SMI), roughly €2,800–€2,850/month depending on how your consulate converts the 2026 SMI. It is open to both remote employees and freelancers, as long as no more than 20% of your work comes from Spanish clients. The headline tax pitch — the 24% Beckham-law flat rate — genuinely helps a remote employee, but most freelancers on this visa do not automatically qualify for it, which is the single most common misunderstanding in how this visa gets written about.

Every "digital nomad visa" roundup lumps Spain's version in with a dozen other countries, and most of them repeat the same two mistakes: quoting a stale euro figure for the income test (it is pegged to the minimum wage and moves every year) and selling the Beckham law as an automatic perk of the visa, when it is really an automatic perk of being an employee. Since Spain's investor Golden Visa stopped accepting new applications in April 2025, this visa — officially the visado para teletrabajo de carácter internacional — has become the most realistic route into Spain for an American who earns money remotely rather than by investing a lump sum.

What is Spain's digital nomad visa?

Spain's digital nomad visa was created by the 2022 Startup Law (Ley 28/2022, de fomento del ecosistema de las empresas emergentes) and is administered through Spain's immigration authorities and consulates, with large or complex files routed through the Unidad de Grandes Empresas (UGE-CE), the same economic-migration unit that handles Spain's other investor and entrepreneur visas. It authorizes a non-EU/EEA national to live in Spain while working remotely for employers or clients based outside the country. Unlike a short-stay Schengen arrangement, it is a genuine residence authorization: it gives you the right to live in Spain, enroll in the public healthcare system once you register as a resident, travel visa-free across Schengen, and build time toward permanent residence and citizenship.

The visa comes in two flavors that get conflated constantly. You can apply for the visa from a Spanish consulate in the US before you travel, which — per Spain's own official FAQ — authorizes you to live and work in Spain for one year. Alternatively, if you are already legally in Spain, you can apply directly for the residence authorization (autorización de residencia), which can run up to three years and is renewable for a further two. Both routes share the same underlying income and work-structure tests described below; the choice is mainly about whether you want to secure the right to travel first or are already present in Spain.

Who qualifies — the income rule

The income test is built around a multiple of the SMI (Salario Mínimo Interprofesional), Spain's national minimum wage, which is fixed by Royal Decree each year. For 2026, Real Decreto 126/2026 (BOE-A-2026-3815) set the SMI at €1,221/month, paid in 14 installments — the raise that took the national minimum wage from €1,184 in 2025 to €1,221 in 2026.

Spain's official digital-nomad FAQ (Ministerio de Inclusión, Seguridad Social y Migraciones) states the income threshold as "200% of the SMI" per month, without printing a converted euro figure. Practitioners apply that 200% to the SMI's 12-payment annual equivalent (the 14 monthly payments divided across 12 months, so €1,221 × 14 ÷ 12 ≈ €1,424.50), which puts the main applicant's monthly bar at roughly €2,849/month — about €34,190 a year. A note on precision: because the official text names the percentage rather than a fixed euro number for the year, and because the exact conversion method a given consulate applies can vary, treat €2,849/month as the standard working figure and have your lawyer confirm the precise number your consulate is using before you assemble your file, the same caution that applies to every SMI-linked Spanish visa.

Income can come from a salary, from freelance billings, or from a mix of both, as long as it is active, work-based income — this is not a passive-income visa like the non-lucrative visa. Evidence requirements differ by employment type: employees submit an employment contract or employer letter confirming remote authorization and salary; the self-employed submit at least one service contract with a non-Spanish client that has run, or is agreed to run, for a minimum of three months, plus invoices and bank statements showing the income is real and ongoing.

Employee vs freelancer, and the 20% rule

Spain's version of this visa was deliberately built to work for both remote employees and the self-employed, which is more flexible than several countries' schemes that only accept one or the other. What both paths share is a single hard limit: no more than 20% of your total professional activity may be for Spanish companies or clients, and any work you do perform for a Spanish entity must be structured as a professional, civil-law relationship — never an employment relationship with a Spanish employer. The visa is for people whose economic center of gravity sits outside Spain; a small amount of local client work is tolerated, a Spanish job is not.

For employees, the practical requirement is a letter or contract from a company based outside Spain that explicitly authorizes remote work. For freelancers, Spain's official guidance asks for a services contract with a foreign client lasting a minimum of three months — a single one-off invoice, or income that looks like a short-term project rather than an ongoing arrangement, invites the same scrutiny a thin file gets on any income-based Spanish visa.

The application process and timeline

Most Americans apply from the consulate serving their US state of residence, or through the UGE-CE unit in Madrid for complex or company-sponsored files. The core document file includes proof of the remote work relationship (contract or client agreements), proof of income clearing the SMI-linked threshold, a clean criminal record (apostilled FBI background check), private health insurance valid in Spain, and proof of qualifications or at least three years of relevant professional experience if your role requires them. Processing for the consular route is commonly quoted at a matter of weeks to a few months, materially faster than the Golden Visa ever was, though timelines vary by consulate and by how complete the file is at submission.

Once approved, you enter Spain, register your address (empadronamiento), obtain your foreigner ID number (NIE) if you do not already have one, and collect your physical residence card (TIE). From there the clock toward permanent residence (five years) and citizenship (ordinarily ten years for Americans) starts running from the date your residence is registered, not from your application date.

The tax catch: the Beckham law mostly helps employees

This is the nuance almost every consumer guide to this visa gets wrong, and it can be worth tens of thousands of dollars a year. Spain's impatriate special tax regime — Article 93 of the Personal Income Tax Law (LIRPF), universally nicknamed the "Beckham law" — lets a new Spanish tax resident who meets the conditions pay a flat 24% on qualifying employment income up to €600,000 (47% on the excess above that), instead of Spain's ordinary progressive rates, which also top out at 47% but bite far sooner on the income scale. It runs for the tax year of the move plus the following five tax years — six years in total — and the core eligibility condition for everyone is not having been a Spanish tax resident during the preceding five tax periods.

Where guides go wrong is assuming the digital nomad visa itself grants Beckham-law access. It does not. Per the Tax Agency's own manual, the regime's qualifying moves are: an employment contract with remote work performed exclusively through IT and telecommunications systems (this route exists specifically for remote employees, including digital nomads); becoming a company director within shareholding limits; starting a qualifying entrepreneurial activity under Article 70 of the Startup Law; or working as a highly qualified professional providing services to emerging companies, or doing training, research, development or innovation, earning over 40% of your income from that activity. A remote employee of a US company clears this cleanly through the first route. A freelancer simply invoicing clients as an autónomo does not automatically qualify — unless their specific activity happens to fit the entrepreneurial or highly-qualified-professional categories, a self-employed digital nomad is typically taxed under Spain's ordinary progressive IRPF scale, which reaches 47% at the top bracket, with none of the flat-rate relief. This is the single detail worth confirming with a Spanish tax adviser before you decide whether to structure your move as an employee secondment or as independent freelancing.

US tax: FEIE, self-employment tax, and FBAR

Becoming a Spanish tax resident changes nothing about your US filing obligations. The United States taxes citizens and green card holders on worldwide income regardless of where they live, so a digital nomad visa holder keeps filing a US return every year on top of any Spanish return.

The mechanism that actually helps a remote worker abroad is the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (Form 2555), not the foreign tax credit that property owners typically rely on. An American who meets either the bona fide residence test or the 330-day physical presence test can exclude a set amount of foreign earned income each year (the limit is adjusted annually for inflation) from US tax. This genuinely helps a salaried remote employee or a freelancer whose income is earned income rather than passive income — which is exactly the category this visa requires. Two traps to flag: the FEIE excludes income from income tax, but it does not exclude self-employment income from US self-employment tax (Social Security and Medicare, currently 15.3% on net self-employment earnings), which keeps applying to a US sole proprietor or independent contractor regardless of residence, unless a US Social Security totalization agreement with Spain covers the specific work arrangement. And if your Spanish tax bill (whether at the Beckham 24% rate or the ordinary scale) ends up higher than the US tax on the same income, the foreign tax credit (Form 1116) — not the FEIE — is usually the better lever, since the two cannot be stacked on the same dollars of income.

Once you open a Spanish bank account to receive local payments or pay rent, it becomes reportable on the FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) the moment your combined foreign accounts exceed $10,000 at any point in the year, and potentially on Form 8938 depending on your filing status and whether you live inside or outside the US for the year. None of this is exotic — it is the same reporting regime that applies to any American with a foreign bank account — but it catches first-time movers who assume a "visa for remote work" has no US paperwork consequences.

Step-by-step checklist: digital nomad visa from decision to residence card

  1. Confirm your income qualifies — total your remote salary or freelance billings and check it clears roughly €2,849/month for a single applicant (confirm the exact 2026 figure with your consulate), with no more than 20% from Spanish sources.
  2. Lock down your work documentation — an employer letter authorizing remote work, or a client services contract running at least three months, plus invoices and bank statements.
  3. Decide employee vs freelancer structure early — this decision drives whether the Beckham law is realistically available to you, so get the tax analysis done before you file, not after you move.
  4. Get your apostilled FBI background check and Spain-valid health insurance — both are standard-issue requirements across Spain's residence visas.
  5. Apply at your consulate or via UGE-CE — for the US state where you live, or through the dedicated unit for company-sponsored or complex files.
  6. Enter Spain and register — empadronamiento, NIE, and your TIE residence card.
  7. File Modelo 149 within six months of Social Security registration if you intend to elect into the Beckham-law regime and you qualify under one of its routes.
  8. Plan your US filing — Form 2555 (FEIE) if you meet the residence or presence test, Form 1116 if Spanish tax exceeds what the FEIE shelters, and FBAR/Form 8938 once you open a Spanish account.
  9. Renew and accumulate residence — track the clock toward five-year permanent residence and the ordinary ten-year citizenship threshold.

If you're not American: what changes

The visa itself works the same for every non-EU/EEA national. A Briton, Canadian, Australian or South African follows the identical Spanish process — same 200%-SMI income test, same 20% Spanish-client cap, same consular-or-UGE-CE route. What changes is the tax wrapper from your home country and, for some nationalities, the citizenship timeline.

Non-US readers should swap the US-specific analysis above for their own country's rules and check three things: (1) whether your home country taxes worldwide income or only domestic income once you leave — most countries tax on residence rather than citizenship, so becoming a Spanish resident may remove you from your home tax net rather than layering two systems on top of each other; (2) your home country's foreign-account and foreign-asset reporting duties, which differ from the US FBAR/Form 8938 regime; and (3) whether your nationality qualifies for Spain's reduced two-year naturalisation track (available to nationals of Ibero-American countries, Andorra, the Philippines, Equatorial Guinea, Portugal, and people of Sephardic descent) rather than the ordinary ten years most other nationals face. Always confirm with an adviser licensed in your own country of residence.

Is Spain's digital nomad visa worth it in 2026?

For the right applicant, yes, and increasingly by default — with the Golden Visa gone, this is now the most realistic residence route into Spain for an American who earns a living remotely rather than retiring on passive income. The income bar is meaningfully higher than a pension-based visa, but it is still far more attainable for a working American than parking €500,000+ in Spanish assets, and the visa grants the one thing the Golden Visa never did: the right to actually work while you live there.

Where 2026 demands clear eyes is the tax structuring decision. Setting up as a remote employee of your US company, where that is genuinely possible, opens a clean path to the Beckham law's flat 24% rate on employment income — a real, multi-year tax saving. Setting up as a freelancer keeps the visa fully available but usually leaves you on Spain's ordinary progressive scale unless your specific activity fits one of the regime's narrower qualifying categories. Neither path is wrong, but confusing them — assuming the visa itself carries the tax break — is the mistake that costs people the most money. Get the employment-structure and tax analysis done before you file the visa application, not after you have already moved.

Frequently asked questions

How much income do you need for Spain's digital nomad visa in 2026?

The official rule is 200% of Spain's SMI. For 2026 the SMI is €1,221/month (14 payments) under Real Decreto 126/2026. Converting to a 12-payment basis puts the 200% threshold at roughly €2,849/month (~€34,190/year) for a single applicant — confirm the exact figure your consulate is using, since the official text names the percentage rather than a fixed euro figure. A second family member adds 75% of the SMI, and each further dependent adds 25%.

Can freelancers get Spain's digital nomad visa, or only employees?

Both. Employees need a contract or employer letter authorizing remote work; the self-employed need at least one foreign-client service contract running three months or more. Either way, no more than 20% of your professional income may come from Spanish clients, and that work must be structured as a professional relationship, never employment with a Spanish company.

Does Spain's digital nomad visa come with the Beckham law tax break?

Not automatically. The Beckham law (Article 93 LIRPF) taxes qualifying employment income at a flat 24% up to €600,000 instead of Spain's ordinary progressive rates. It clearly covers remote employees under a foreign employment contract. It does not automatically cover freelancers — a self-employed digital nomad needs to separately qualify under the entrepreneurial-activity or highly-qualified-professional routes, or they are taxed on the ordinary scale, which reaches 47%.

How does this visa affect US taxes?

You remain a US taxpayer on worldwide income. A qualifying remote employee or freelancer abroad can typically use the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (Form 2555), but the FEIE does not exclude self-employment income from US self-employment tax (15.3%), which still applies to independent contractors regardless of residence. Spanish tax paid can also be creditable on Form 1116, and a Spanish bank account becomes FBAR/Form 8938 reportable once balances cross the usual thresholds.

How long does the visa last, and does it lead to citizenship?

The consular visa authorizes one year; applying from inside Spain gets a residence authorization valid up to three years, renewable for two more. It counts toward Spain's five-year permanent-residence threshold and toward citizenship, which for most Americans requires ten years of continuous legal residence — Spain's reduced two-year track applies only to specific nationalities (Ibero-American countries, Andorra, the Philippines, Equatorial Guinea, Portugal, and Sephardic-descent applicants), not to US citizens generally.
