Buying Property in Lisbon as an American (2026): Neighbourhoods, Prices and Traps

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated October 2026  ·  12 min read

Lisbon is open to American buyers, and the purchase itself is straightforward — the part that trips people up is which parish they land in. The capital carries Portugal's highest prices (the city's official median sits around €4,800 per square metre), the flat 7.5% non-resident IMT that applies nationwide hits the biggest euro amount here, and whether you can legally short-let the unit now depends entirely on which freguesia it's in. This guide is the city-level layer on top of our general buying property in Portugal guide — read that one for the national process, this one for Lisbon's specifics.

This article is informational and does not constitute legal, tax, or investment advice. It reflects the MigrateState editorial team's assessment of publicly available rules as of October 2026; Lisbon municipal regulations, IMT/IMI rates, and Alojamento Local zoning all change. Every general "buying in Portugal" guide, including our own, has to speak to the whole country — the Algarve, Porto, the Silver Coast, Lisbon. But Lisbon is where most American buyers actually end up, and it is different enough from the national average to deserve its own page: it is Portugal's most expensive market by a wide margin, its oldest housing stock, and the city where short-term-rental zoning now varies block by block rather than by a single national rule. This guide assumes you already know the national basics — NIF, fiscal representative, the IMT change — and goes one level deeper into what is specifically true of buying inside the city of Lisbon.

Lisbon's neighbourhoods, in plain terms

Lisbon's official statistics agency, INE, puts the municipality's own median sale price at roughly €4,800 per square metre as of 2025 — the highest of any municipality in the country. That is a city-wide average, and it hides a genuinely wide spread between parishes. Real-estate portals and valuation firms (not an official government series at the street level) consistently describe the same broad pattern, which is worth knowing before you fall for a listing:

What it actually costs: a €600,000 flat, worked out

Our national guide covers the 2026 IMT change in full — the short version is that any non-resident now pays a flat 7.5% transfer tax on a residential purchase, in force since 25 May 2026 under Decreto-Lei n.º 97/2026, regardless of price and with no ordinary exemption. Because Lisbon prices sit well above the national average, that flat rate bites on a bigger number here than almost anywhere else in the country. Here is what it means on a representative €600,000 Lisbon flat — roughly what a well-located two-bedroom in Campo de Ourique or a smaller unit in Chiado runs in 2026:

As on any Portuguese purchase, that 7.5% is potentially refundable down to the ordinary progressive rate if you become a Portuguese tax resident within two years of buying, or commit the unit to regulated long-term rental (currently capped around €2,300/month) within six months and keep it rented for 36 of the first 60 months. In a city where the flat rate's euro cost runs this high, it is worth discussing both refund routes with your lawyer before you sign the CPCV, not after.

Condominium rules: quotas, reserve fund, assembly

Almost every Lisbon apartment sits inside a condomínio — Portugal's horizontal-property regime for multi-unit buildings. Three things an American buyer should understand before closing:

  • Your share is fixed at the deed. Each unit carries a permilagem — a fixed per-mille share of the whole building, set when the property was first divided — and that share, not square footage alone, is what your monthly condo quota and your vote at the owners' assembly are based on.
  • A reserve fund is mandatory. Under Decreto-Lei n.º 268/94 (as amended), every condominium must pay at least 10% of its ordinary annual budget into a common reserve fund, held separately and earmarked for building-wide repairs — the roof, the façade, the elevator — rather than routine upkeep.
  • The annual assembly sets the budget. Owners vote, weighted by permilagem, on the administrator, the annual budget and any special works. A lawyer running due diligence should pull the last two years of assembly minutes, not just the current monthly quota, since a looming façade or elevator project can turn into a large special assessment shortly after you close.

Older buildings in Alfama, Chiado or the Baixa are the ones most likely to be weighing a significant special assessment — ask for the building's maintenance plan and reserve-fund balance before making an offer, not after.

Can you short-let it? Alojamento Local, parish by parish

If part of the appeal of a Lisbon purchase is Airbnb-style income, read this section before you fall in love with a listing. Portugal's 2023 nationwide freeze on new Alojamento Local (AL) registrations was revoked by Decreto-Lei n.º 76/2024 — but that same law handed municipalities, Lisbon included, the power to declare local containment zones where new AL registrations are refused once short-term-rental density in a parish crosses a set threshold. Lisbon's own containment rules apply most heavily in exactly the central, tourist-dense parishes — the Baixa, Alfama, parts of Chiado and Príncipe Real — that American buyers gravitate toward for the same reasons tourists do.

The practical consequences for a buyer:

  • A flat that already carries a transferable AL licence is a genuinely different, more valuable asset than an identical flat without one, in a parish at or near its containment threshold — confirm the licence transfers with the sale and is in good standing before you price that income in.
  • A flat with no existing licence in a contained parish may simply never be short-let-able, whatever the listing agent implies. Ask the parish's current AL status directly rather than assuming last year's zoning still holds.
  • This is a fast-moving area of municipal policy — our dedicated guide to short-term rental rules abroad tracks the current thresholds and is the page to check right before you commit to a rental-income thesis.

The building is older than the United States: diligence that matters

A meaningful share of Lisbon's housing stock — most of the Baixa, much of Chiado and Alfama — was rebuilt after the catastrophic 1755 earthquake using the gaiola pombalina, an early timber anti-seismic frame that was genuinely innovative for its era but predates every modern structural code by roughly two centuries. Buying in these quarters is not a reason to avoid them — it's a reason to diligence them properly:

  1. Commission an independent structural survey, not just the seller's own report, especially on anything pre-dating the mid-20th century.
  2. Request the ficha técnica da habitação — the technical building file — and confirm the habitation licence (licença de utilização) actually matches the unit's current layout; informal extensions and knocked-through walls are common in older buildings and can complicate both insurance and resale.
  3. Check the condominium's reserve fund and maintenance plan (see above) specifically for façade, roof and foundation items, which are where the real cost of an old building shows up.
  4. Confirm insurance is actually available on the specific building before you're contractually committed — older, un-retrofitted structures can carry higher premiums or stricter conditions than a newer Parque das Nações tower.

None of this should be read as a reason to prefer new construction by default — the premium for character in Lisbon's historic core reflects real, durable demand. It is a reason to budget for a proper survey as a cost of doing business, not an optional extra.

Non-resident mortgages in Lisbon

The national picture — Portuguese banks lending to non-resident Americans at roughly 60%–70% loan-to-value, Euribor-based rates, and a documentation-heavy process — applies in Lisbon as everywhere else; see our guide to foreign mortgage rates for US citizens for the mechanics. What's specific to Lisbon is simply the arithmetic: at the city's higher price points, a 30%–40% down payment is a materially larger cash commitment than the same LTV on a Silver Coast or Algarve property, which is part of why a meaningful share of American buyers in the capital pay cash and consider financing only later, if at all. Mortgage borrowing itself carries its own 0.6% stamp duty on the loan amount for terms of five years or more, on top of the purchase-side costs above.

Buying ≠ residency: the D7/D8 pairing

As everywhere in Portugal, owning a Lisbon apartment does not, by itself, grant residency — the property route left the Golden Visa in 2023, and the program now runs through qualifying funds rather than real estate (see our full assessment of whether the Golden Visa is still worth it). If your actual plan is to live in the apartment rather than just own it, the realistic paths are the D7 passive-income visa or the D8 digital-nomad visa. A Lisbon purchase pairs naturally with either — Lisbon is where most D7 and D8 applicants actually want to live — but the visa application and the property purchase remain two separate processes with two separate timelines, and owning the flat does not fast-track the visa.

Step-by-step: closing on a Lisbon flat

The mechanics mirror the national process in our buying property in Portugal guide, with a Lisbon-specific note at each step most relevant to the capital:

  1. Appoint a fiscal representative and get your NIF. Done remotely; essentially identical across Portugal.
  2. Open a Portuguese bank account. Lisbon-based banks and branches are the most used to foreign-buyer paperwork, which can modestly speed this step.
  3. Engage an independent lawyer who, in Lisbon specifically, should pull condominium minutes and AL registration status alongside the standard title and debt checks.
  4. Sign the CPCV (promissory contract) and pay a deposit, typically 10% — binding on both sides.
  5. Pay IMT and stamp duty before the deed, structuring the refund route (residency or rental) now if it applies to you.
  6. Sign the escritura before a notary, in person or via power of attorney — see our guide to closing without flying out if you won't be in Lisbon for signing.
  7. Register the property at the Land Registry (Conservatória do Registo Predial) in your name.
  8. Set up US tax compliance — FBAR for the new Portuguese bank account, and a plan for future rental or resale reporting.

Lisbon's transaction volume and professional infrastructure mean the two-to-four-month national timeline typically runs toward the shorter end here, assuming your own paperwork — NIF, fiscal rep, financing if any — is ready in parallel.

The US tax rules that still apply

Nothing about buying specifically in Lisbon changes the IRS side of the transaction, which is covered in full in our national guide. In short: the US taxes citizens on worldwide income, so rental income is reportable on Schedule E and a future sale is a US capital gain; the foreign tax credit generally offsets most of the double taxation because Portugal also taxes the gain; your new Portuguese bank account can trigger FBAR and potentially FATCA reporting once balances cross the relevant thresholds; and currency movement between the euro and the dollar between your purchase and a later sale or mortgage payoff is part of your taxable position, not a separate item. A cross-border CPA should be briefed before you sign, not after.

If you're not American: what changes

Every Portuguese-side rule on this page — the flat 7.5% non-resident IMT, condominium law, Alojamento Local zoning, and the structural-diligence points — applies to any foreign buyer, regardless of passport. What's specifically American here is the wrapper: worldwide US taxation, FBAR/FATCA, and the US capital-gains and currency rules on a later sale.

If you live outside the US, drop the IRS section above and substitute your own country's rules: whether it taxes foreign rental income and gains, whether it requires foreign-asset or account reporting, and whether a double-taxation treaty with Portugal applies. The Portuguese-side mechanics — IMT, IMI, condominium law, AL zoning — are identical whatever your nationality.

Is buying in Lisbon worth it in 2026?

For the right buyer, yes — Lisbon remains one of Europe's most liveable capitals, with a real, deep rental and resale market that smaller Portuguese cities and towns simply don't have. But go in pricing the city correctly: the flat 7.5% non-resident IMT lands on Portugal's highest base prices here, so the euro cost of entry is the largest in the country even though the percentage is the same everywhere. Pick a neighbourhood for what it actually offers rather than general reputation — Chiado and Príncipe Real for walkable prestige, Alfama for character if you budget for a real structural survey, Campo de Ourique and Alvalade for family value, Parque das Nações for modern construction — confirm the Alojamento Local status before counting on rental income, and have both a Portuguese lawyer and a US cross-border CPA on the file before you sign.

Frequently asked questions

Can an American buy an apartment in Lisbon?

Yes, with no restrictions. Portugal places no limits on foreign ownership, so a US citizen can buy any apartment or house in Lisbon with the same rights as a Portuguese national, without residency or a visa. You need only a Portuguese tax number (NIF) and, because the US sits outside the EU/EEA, a fiscal representative to obtain it.

How much does it cost to buy property in Lisbon as a non-resident in 2026?

Budget roughly 9%–11% on top of the price. On a representative €600,000 Lisbon flat that is about €60,000: a flat 7.5% IMT transfer tax (€45,000, in force since 25 May 2026), 0.8% stamp duty (€4,800), a lawyer at roughly 1%–2% (about €9,000), and notary and registry fees of around €1,250. Lisbon's own prices are Portugal's highest, so the percentage cost is the same nationwide but the euro amount is bigger.

Which Lisbon neighbourhoods do American buyers usually look at?

Chiado and Príncipe Real for walkable, restored historic-centre living at the top of the price range; Alfama for character and short-let potential in older, pre-earthquake-code buildings that need real structural diligence; Campo de Ourique and Alvalade for family-oriented, mid-market residential streets below the city median; and Parque das Nações for new-build, elevator-equipped apartments with modern infrastructure, generally at a smaller premium than the historic core despite being newer construction.

Can I rent out my Lisbon apartment on Airbnb?

Only if the unit already holds a valid Alojamento Local (AL) licence or sits outside one of Lisbon's containment parishes. The 2023 nationwide freeze on new AL registrations was revoked in 2024, but Lisbon's municipal rules let the council bar new registrations parish by parish once local AL density crosses a set threshold, and the historic centre is where that bites hardest. Confirm a listing's AL status, or the parish's current containment rules, before assuming short-let income.

Does buying property in Lisbon give me Portuguese residency?

No. Portugal removed real estate from the Golden Visa in 2023, so owning a Lisbon apartment is ownership, not a residence permit. Americans who want to actually live there apply separately, typically through the D7 passive-income visa or the D8 digital-nomad visa — owning a home in Lisbon can support either application without being a substitute for it.

Do I owe US tax on a Lisbon property?

Potentially, because the US taxes citizens on worldwide income. Rental income is reportable on Schedule E, a future sale is a US capital gain, and the foreign tax credit generally offsets most double taxation since Portugal also taxes the gain. A Portuguese bank account can trigger FBAR and FATCA reporting, and currency movement between the euro and the dollar is part of your taxable gain, not separate from it.
