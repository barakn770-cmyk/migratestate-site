Costa Rica's three residency categories, at a glance

Costa Rica offers three non-work residency categories built for foreigners who bring money rather than a local job offer: Pensionado (retiree, built on a guaranteed pension), Rentista (passive-income earner, built on stable monthly income, not on a deposit that is paid back in installments), and Inversionista (investor, built on a qualifying investment). All three are administered by the Dirección General de Migración y Extranjería (DGME) under the general immigration law (Ley General de Migración y Extranjería, Ley 8764) and, for Inversionista specifically, the investor-incentives law (Ley 9996). None require you to have a Costa Rican employer or speak Spanish, and none grant citizenship directly — they are residency routes with a path to naturalization much further down the line.

For Americans, these three categories are the standard menu: retirees with a pension use Pensionado, people living off investments or rental income use Rentista, and buyers who want their real-estate or business purchase to double as a residency ticket use Inversionista. Many readers of this site end up choosing between Rentista and Inversionista specifically because they're already buying property — see our guide to buying property in Costa Rica for the purchase side of that decision.

Pensionado: the retiree route

Pensionado is built for anyone — of any age — receiving a guaranteed lifetime pension of at least US$1,000 per month. "Guaranteed" and "lifetime" are doing real work here: DGME wants a payment that will keep arriving for the rest of your life, not a fixed-term annuity or an account balance that can run out. The classic qualifying source is US Social Security retirement benefits, which satisfy the requirement cleanly and are the single most common Pensionado income source among American applicants. A private pension, a government pension, or a qualifying employer retirement plan can also work, provided the paying institution will certify it is lifetime and guaranteed.

There is no minimum age for Pensionado — the requirement is about the pension's nature, not your birthdate, so a 45-year-old collecting a qualifying disability or early pension can apply as readily as a 65-year-old on Social Security. Spouses and dependent children under 25 (or dependent regardless of age if disabled) can be included on the same application, and Costa Rica does not require the additional income multiplier for dependents that some countries impose — the US$1,000/month threshold covers the family unit.

Rentista: the passive-income route

Rentista is for income that is not a lifetime pension. Decreto Ejecutivo 37112, article 99, as replaced by Decreto Ejecutivo 43926, asks for a document showing that for a period of not less than two years the applicant will receive stable, permanent monthly income of at least US$2,500. A bank in the national system can certify income generated there. Income from abroad is certified by a bank in the country where it arises, with the signature authenticated or the document apostilled. If a bank will not issue that document, a Costa Rican authorized public accountant can certify it from reliable records, with a Spanish translation where the papers are in another language.

A single deposit that the bank pays back in monthly fractions is not this income. Article 30 of Decreto Ejecutivo 43926 added a definition to article 2 of Decreto 37112: income is the profit or benefit something yields each year, or what is collected from it. A deposit made so that a bank returns it in monthly fractions is not income, because what comes back is the reimbursement of the money first handed to the bank. The arithmetic that turns US$2,500 times 24 months into US$60,000 is not a route the regulation recognizes. Interest the deposit actually earns can be income. The return of the principal is not.

Inversionista: the investor route — and the 2026 threshold question

Inversionista is an investment in Costa Rica. Article 4 of Decreto Ejecutivo 43926, the regulation of Ley 9996, says the person invests at least US$150,000 at the Central Bank's selling rate, in registrable real estate or movable property, shares, securities, productive projects, projects of national interest, venture-capital funds, or sustainable tourism infrastructure. Ley 9996, article 8, uses the same dollar figure and adds the words "for the period this law establishes."

Confirm the figure DGME is applying before you wire funds. The regulation SCIJ still publishes, Decreto Ejecutivo 43926, article 4, states US$150,000. Ley 9996, article 12, limits the benefits in article 5 — the import-duty and tax incentives — to the first five years from the law's entry into force on 14 July 2021. That window closed on 14 July 2026. People who opted in during the five years keep those incentives for ten years from the grant. Article 8's phrase "for the period this law establishes" is not the same sentence as article 12. No text read for this check replaces US$150,000 with US$200,000. Ask DGME, in writing, what amount a new file must show.

Whatever the exact current number, the mechanics of the Inversionista route are otherwise stable: you make the qualifying investment, register it (for real estate, at the National Registry), and document its value with an appraisal or purchase deed. Unlike Pensionado and Rentista, Inversionista residents are permitted to collect income directly from the underlying investment — rental income from qualifying real estate, or profit from a qualifying business — without that being treated as prohibited local employment.

CCSS enrollment: the mandatory health-insurance piece

All three categories carry the same obligation once residency is granted: enrollment in the Caja Costarricense de Seguro Social (CCSS), Costa Rica's public health and social-security system. This is not optional — it's a condition of maintaining the residency status, not a marketing add-on. Monthly CCSS contributions are calculated as a percentage of your declared income (roughly in the high single digits to low double digits of a percent, and periodically adjusted by CCSS), which for Pensionado and Rentista applicants is generally based on the income you declared to qualify. The contribution percentage itself is not restated here, because this check did not read a current CCSS tariff. CCSS enrollment gives you access to Costa Rica's public healthcare system, widely regarded as one of the stronger public health systems in Latin America, and in practice most residents carry it alongside private supplemental insurance for elective and specialist care.

DGME process and the three-year path to permanent residency

All three categories start as temporary residency. Pensionado renewal under article 228 of Decreto 37112 is every two years, and the inversionista renewal article in Decreto 43926 is also every two years, in each case while the qualifying pension, income, or investment still exists. Ley 8764, article 78, lets a person who has held temporary residency for three consecutive years apply for permanent residency. The article does not impose a 180-day presence test. Article 129(6) is the absence rule that does exist: temporary residency is cancelled if the person is outside Costa Rica for more than two consecutive years, unless a proved health, study, family, or other exception applies. Permanent residents are cancelled for a consecutive absence of more than four years, under article 129(5). Naturalization is a later, separate statute. This page does not restate a citizenship year-count it has not re-read.

The application itself runs through DGME with a Costa Rican attorney handling the filing: apostilled and translated documents (birth certificate, police-clearance certificate, proof of the qualifying pension, income, or investment), a passport-photo set, and biometrics registration in Costa Rica. Processing timelines vary by category and DGME's current caseload; budgeting for several months to over a year from filing to card issuance is realistic, and — as with most Latin American immigration agencies — the paperwork discipline of your attorney matters more to your timeline than anything else in your control.

The US tax angle: pensions, Social Security, and the account's FBAR

None of the three Costa Rica categories change your US tax obligations. The United States taxes citizens on worldwide income regardless of residency, so becoming a Costa Rican resident under any of these routes does not reduce what you owe the IRS.

  • Social Security and pensions stay fully US-taxable. The Social Security income that qualifies you for Pensionado is taxed by the US exactly as it would be if you'd stayed home — Costa Rica's residency status changes nothing about the US side of that income. If Costa Rica also taxes the pension locally (Costa Rica generally does not tax foreign-source pension income for residents under its territorial system, but confirm your specific situation with a local advisor), a foreign tax credit may apply against any double-taxed portion.
  • A Costa Rican account can trigger foreign-account reporting. The account that receives the pension or the Rentista income, and a Costa Rican account tied to an Inversionista investment, is a foreign financial account. Once the aggregate of foreign accounts exceeds US$10,000 at any point in the year, an FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) is required, and Form 8938 can apply on top of that, depending on where you live and how you file. These are information returns, not extra tax. A US$60,000 deposit drawn down at US$2,500 a month is not the Rentista test, so it is not a reason to open that structure.
  • Rental or business income under Rentista/Inversionista is ordinary US-taxable income, reported the same way it would be from a US source, with a foreign tax credit available for Costa Rican tax paid on the same income.
  • Real estate bought for the Inversionista route follows the same US rules as any foreign property purchase — see our guides on US taxes on foreign property and FBAR for foreign property owners for the reporting mechanics that carry over directly to a Costa Rican purchase.

Step-by-step: applying as an American

  1. Pick your category. Pensionado if you have a qualifying lifetime pension; Rentista if you can document US$2,500 a month of stable income for two years; Inversionista if you're already buying property or investing in Costa Rica and want the residency to ride along — confirm the live Inversionista threshold first.
  2. Gather and apostille your documents. Birth certificate, police-clearance certificate (FBI background check for Americans), marriage certificate if including a spouse, and proof of the qualifying pension, income, or investment.
  3. Engage a Costa Rican immigration attorney to translate documents into Spanish, prepare the DGME filing, and track category-specific requirements that change over time (like the Inversionista threshold above).
  4. File with DGME and complete biometrics registration in Costa Rica.
  5. Enroll in CCSS once your residency is approved — this is mandatory, not optional.
  6. Maintain the qualifying pension, income, or investment throughout temporary residency. Article 78 counts three consecutive years. Article 129 cancels temporary residency after an absence of more than two consecutive years.
  7. Line up US compliance — FBAR on the Costa Rican account, Form 8938 if applicable, and ordinary US tax reporting on any rental or business income, with a cross-border advisor.

If you're not American: what changes

The Costa Rican side of Pensionado, Rentista and Inversionista is the same for any nationality — the same US$1,000 a month, US$2,500 a month, and the Inversionista amount in the regulation, the same CCSS enrollment, and the same three-year path to permanent residency. What differs is only your home-country tax treatment.

If you're not a US citizen, drop the FBAR/Form 8938/worldwide-taxation layer above and substitute your own country's rules. Check three things with a tax adviser where you're resident: (1) whether your home country taxes foreign pension, rental or investment income while you hold Costa Rican residency; (2) whether you must report foreign bank accounts or assets at home — many countries have their own FBAR-equivalent regimes; and (3) whether a double-taxation treaty exists between Costa Rica and your country. Costa Rica's own rules — the income thresholds, the rule that a repaid deposit is not income, the CCSS requirement, and the Inversionista amount in Decreto 43926 — apply identically regardless of your passport.

Which route — and is it worth it in 2026?

For Americans, the category follows the paper you already have. A lifetime pension of at least US$1,000 a month is Pensionado, under article 100 of Decreto 37112. Income of at least US$2,500 a month for two years, certified as the regulation requires, is Rentista. An investment of at least US$150,000 of the kinds article 4 of Decreto 43926 lists is Inversionista, and the article 5 incentives are closed to a new opt-in after 14 July 2026. All three are temporary residency, with article 78's three consecutive years as the door to permanent residency, and article 129's two-year absence rule as the cancellation line. None of them changes US worldwide taxation. A Costa Rican account that holds the pension, the income, or the investment is an FBAR account once the US$10,000 aggregate is crossed.

Frequently asked questions

What are Costa Rica's three main residency categories for foreigners?

Pensionado is a lifetime pension of at least US$1,000 a month, at any age. Rentista is stable, permanent income of at least US$2,500 a month for no less than two years. Decreto Ejecutivo 43926 says a bank deposit that is returned in monthly fractions is not income. Inversionista, in article 4 of that decree, is an investment of at least US$150,000. None of these categories is a job offer.

What is the current Inversionista investment threshold?

Decreto Ejecutivo 43926, article 4, still states US$150,000, and that is the figure on the regulation SCIJ publishes. Ley 9996, article 8, sets the same amount for the period the law establishes. Article 12 limits the article 5 import and tax incentives to the first five years from 14 July 2021, so that window closed on 14 July 2026. No official text fetched for this check states a US$200,000 floor for a file opened after that date. Confirm the figure DGME is applying before you wire funds.

Do I have to work with a Costa Rican company to keep Pensionado or Rentista status?

No — the opposite. Pensionado and Rentista residents cannot work as an employee of a Costa Rican company, though they can own a Costa Rican business and earn income from it. Inversionista residents can additionally collect income directly from their qualifying investment.

Is health insurance mandatory for these residency categories?

Yes. All three categories require enrollment in the CCSS (Caja Costarricense de Seguro Social), Costa Rica's public health and social-security system, as a condition of maintaining residency. Contributions are calculated as a percentage of your declared qualifying income.

How long until permanent residency?

Ley 8764, article 78, lets a person who has held temporary residency for three consecutive years apply for permanent residency. The statute does not add a 180-day presence test. Article 129 cancels temporary residency for an absence of more than two consecutive years, with exceptions for proved health, study, family, or other reasons.

Do Americans pay US tax while living in Costa Rica on one of these visas?

Yes, fully. The US taxes citizens on worldwide income regardless of residency. Social Security and pension income stay fully US-taxable, rental or business income is reported the same as US-source income (with a foreign tax credit for any Costa Rican tax paid), and a Costa Rican account that receives the pension or the Rentista income, or that holds an Inversionista investment, is a foreign financial account. An FBAR is required once aggregate foreign accounts exceed US$10,000, and Form 8938 can apply as well.
