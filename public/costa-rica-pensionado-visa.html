Costa Rica's three residency categories, at a glance

Costa Rica offers three non-work residency categories built for foreigners who bring money rather than a local job offer: Pensionado (retiree, built on a guaranteed pension), Rentista (passive-income earner, built on verifiable outside income or a bank deposit), and Inversionista (investor, built on a qualifying investment). All three are administered by the Dirección General de Migración y Extranjería (DGME) under the general immigration law (Ley General de Migración y Extranjería, Ley 8764) and, for Inversionista specifically, the investor-incentives law (Ley 9996). None require you to have a Costa Rican employer or speak Spanish, and none grant citizenship directly — they are residency routes with a path to naturalization much further down the line.

For Americans, these three categories are the standard menu: retirees with a pension use Pensionado, people living off investments or rental income use Rentista, and buyers who want their real-estate or business purchase to double as a residency ticket use Inversionista. Many readers of this site end up choosing between Rentista and Inversionista specifically because they're already buying property — see our guide to buying property in Costa Rica for the purchase side of that decision.

Pensionado: the retiree route

Pensionado is built for anyone — of any age — receiving a guaranteed lifetime pension of at least US$1,000 per month. "Guaranteed" and "lifetime" are doing real work here: DGME wants a payment that will keep arriving for the rest of your life, not a fixed-term annuity or an account balance that can run out. The classic qualifying source is US Social Security retirement benefits, which satisfy the requirement cleanly and are the single most common Pensionado income source among American applicants. A private pension, a government pension, or a qualifying employer retirement plan can also work, provided the paying institution will certify it is lifetime and guaranteed.

There is no minimum age for Pensionado — the requirement is about the pension's nature, not your birthdate, so a 45-year-old collecting a qualifying disability or early pension can apply as readily as a 65-year-old on Social Security. Spouses and dependent children under 25 (or dependent regardless of age if disabled) can be included on the same application, and Costa Rica does not require the additional income multiplier for dependents that some countries impose — the US$1,000/month threshold covers the family unit.

Rentista: the passive-income route

Rentista is for people living off income that isn't a lifetime pension — dividends, rental income, royalties, trust distributions, or other verifiable outside earnings. It requires proof of at least US$2,500 per month in qualifying income, sustained for a minimum of two years, documented through bank statements, an accountant's letter, or a similar verifiable paper trail from the source.

The route most Rentista applicants actually use, though, is the deposit alternative: rather than proving a two-year income stream, you deposit US$60,000 into a Costa Rican bank account for a minimum of two years. The money stays yours — it isn't a fee or a donation — but it must sit, on deposit, in Costa Rica for the full term. For an American who doesn't want to restructure trusts or rental agreements just to satisfy an immigration officer's documentation checklist, the flat deposit is usually the simpler path, at the cost of tying up US$60,000 for two years and creating the FBAR filing discussed below.

Inversionista: the investor route — and the 2026 threshold question

Inversionista is built on a qualifying investment in Costa Rica: registered real estate, a Costa Rican business, shares in a Costa Rican company, a reforestation project, or other categories the regulations recognize. Historically the threshold was US$200,000. Ley 9996 (the 2021 "law to attract investors, rentiers and pensioners") temporarily cut that to US$150,000 and layered on one-time import-duty exemptions for household goods, up to two personal vehicles, and professional/scientific equipment — a genuinely attractive package for a buyer who was going to import a container of belongings anyway.

⚠️ Confirm the live number before you wire funds. Ley 9996's reduced US$150,000 threshold and its import-duty exemptions carried a statutory window that ran to 14 July 2026. As of this article's research, no extension law had been located on Costa Rica's official legislative and judicial database (SCIJ), which would mean the threshold reverts to the standard US$200,000 and the import exemptions lapse for anyone who files after that date. At the same time, some practitioners report that DGME's own published investor checklist had not yet been updated to reflect the reversion. This is a live, unresolved conflict between the statute's sunset language and the regulator's own published guidance — don't rely on either a blog post or an old checklist. Confirm the figure directly with DGME or a Costa Rican immigration attorney before you commit funds, and if you already filed on or before 14 July 2026, current guidance suggests the lower threshold and exemptions apply to your filing regardless of when it's later approved.

Whatever the exact current number, the mechanics of the Inversionista route are otherwise stable: you make the qualifying investment, register it (for real estate, at the National Registry), and document its value with an appraisal or purchase deed. Unlike Pensionado and Rentista, Inversionista residents are permitted to collect income directly from the underlying investment — rental income from qualifying real estate, or profit from a qualifying business — without that being treated as prohibited local employment.

CCSS enrollment: the mandatory health-insurance piece

All three categories carry the same obligation once residency is granted: enrollment in the Caja Costarricense de Seguro Social (CCSS), Costa Rica's public health and social-security system. This is not optional — it's a condition of maintaining the residency status, not a marketing add-on. Monthly CCSS contributions are calculated as a percentage of your declared income (roughly in the high single digits to low double digits of a percent, and periodically adjusted by CCSS), which for Pensionado and Rentista applicants is generally based on the income or deposit figure you declared to qualify. CCSS enrollment gives you access to Costa Rica's public healthcare system, widely regarded as one of the stronger public health systems in Latin America, and in practice most residents carry it alongside private supplemental insurance for elective and specialist care.

DGME process and the three-year path to permanent residency

All three categories start as temporary residency, renewed periodically (commonly every one to two years depending on category and DGME's current renewal cadence) while you maintain the qualifying pension, income, deposit, or investment. After three years of temporary residency — provided you have spent at least 180 days per year in Costa Rica — you become eligible to apply for permanent residency, which removes the ongoing income/investment-maintenance requirement and, eventually, opens a path toward naturalized citizenship after Costa Rica's separate, longer residency-and-language requirements for that step.

The application itself runs through DGME with a Costa Rican attorney handling the filing: apostilled and translated documents (birth certificate, police-clearance certificate, proof of the qualifying pension/income/deposit/investment), a passport-photo set, and biometrics registration in Costa Rica. Processing timelines vary by category and DGME's current caseload; budgeting for several months to over a year from filing to card issuance is realistic, and — as with most Latin American immigration agencies — the paperwork discipline of your attorney matters more to your timeline than anything else in your control.

The US tax angle: pensions, Social Security, and the deposit's FBAR

None of the three Costa Rica categories change your US tax obligations. The United States taxes citizens on worldwide income regardless of residency, so becoming a Costa Rican resident under any of these routes does not reduce what you owe the IRS.

  • Social Security and pensions stay fully US-taxable. The Social Security income that qualifies you for Pensionado is taxed by the US exactly as it would be if you'd stayed home — Costa Rica's residency status changes nothing about the US side of that income. If Costa Rica also taxes the pension locally (Costa Rica generally does not tax foreign-source pension income for residents under its territorial system, but confirm your specific situation with a local advisor), a foreign tax credit may apply against any double-taxed portion.
  • The Rentista deposit and Inversionista investment account trigger foreign-account reporting. A US$60,000 Costa Rican bank deposit, or a Costa Rican investment/business bank account tied to an Inversionista filing, is a foreign financial account. Once your aggregate foreign accounts exceed US$10,000 at any point in the year, you must file an FBAR (FinCEN Form 114), and depending on your total foreign-asset picture, Form 8938 (FATCA) may apply as well. These are information returns, not additional tax — but the penalties for missing them are real, and the US$60,000 Rentista deposit specifically and reliably crosses the FBAR line for every applicant who uses it.
  • Rental or business income under Rentista/Inversionista is ordinary US-taxable income, reported the same way it would be from a US source, with a foreign tax credit available for Costa Rican tax paid on the same income.
  • Real estate bought for the Inversionista route follows the same US rules as any foreign property purchase — see our guides on US taxes on foreign property and FBAR for foreign property owners for the reporting mechanics that carry over directly to a Costa Rican purchase.

Step-by-step: applying as an American

  1. Pick your category. Pensionado if you have a qualifying lifetime pension; Rentista if you have outside income or are willing to place the US$60,000 deposit; Inversionista if you're already buying property or investing in Costa Rica and want the residency to ride along — confirm the live Inversionista threshold first.
  2. Gather and apostille your documents. Birth certificate, police-clearance certificate (FBI background check for Americans), marriage certificate if including a spouse, and proof of the qualifying pension, income, deposit, or investment.
  3. Engage a Costa Rican immigration attorney to translate documents into Spanish, prepare the DGME filing, and track category-specific requirements that change over time (like the Inversionista threshold above).
  4. File with DGME and complete biometrics registration in Costa Rica.
  5. Enroll in CCSS once your residency is approved — this is mandatory, not optional.
  6. Maintain the qualifying pension, income, deposit, or investment throughout your temporary-residency period, and track your 180-day-per-year presence toward the three-year permanent-residency milestone.
  7. Line up US compliance — FBAR on the deposit or investment account, Form 8938 if applicable, and ordinary US tax reporting on any rental or business income, with a cross-border advisor.

If you're not American: what changes

The Costa Rican side of Pensionado, Rentista and Inversionista is the same for any nationality — the same US$1,000/month, US$2,500/month or US$60,000 deposit, and Inversionista thresholds, the same CCSS enrollment, and the same three-year path to permanent residency. What differs is only your home-country tax treatment.

If you're not a US citizen, drop the FBAR/Form 8938/worldwide-taxation layer above and substitute your own country's rules. Check three things with a tax adviser where you're resident: (1) whether your home country taxes foreign pension, rental or investment income while you hold Costa Rican residency; (2) whether you must report foreign bank accounts or assets at home — many countries have their own FBAR-equivalent regimes; and (3) whether a double-taxation treaty exists between Costa Rica and your country. Costa Rica's own rules — the income thresholds, the deposit alternative, the CCSS requirement, and the Inversionista threshold uncertainty — apply identically regardless of your passport.

Which route — and is it worth it in 2026?

For Americans, the choice mostly makes itself: a qualifying Social Security or pension income points straight to Pensionado, an investment or property purchase you were making anyway points to Inversionista (once you've nailed down the live threshold), and everyone else — living off savings, dividends, or rental income without a lifetime pension — lands on Rentista, usually via the US$60,000 deposit for simplicity. All three lead to the same three-year path to permanent residency and the same mandatory CCSS enrollment, and none of them touch your US tax bill, which keeps running on worldwide income regardless of which box you check. The genuine trap in 2026 is the Inversionista threshold: don't let an out-of-date blog post — or an out-of-date government checklist — cost you an unplanned extra US$50,000. Confirm the live figure with DGME or your attorney before you commit, budget for the FBAR filing the deposit or investment account will trigger, and treat the residency filing and your US cross-border tax planning as one project, not two.

Frequently asked questions

What are Costa Rica's three main residency categories for foreigners?

Pensionado (US$1,000/month guaranteed lifetime pension, any age), Rentista (US$2,500/month verifiable outside income for two years, or a US$60,000 bank deposit held for two years), and Inversionista (a qualifying investment, historically US$200,000, temporarily reduced to US$150,000 under Ley 9996 through 14 July 2026). None require a local job offer or Spanish fluency.

What is the current Inversionista investment threshold?

This is genuinely unsettled as of this writing. Ley 9996's reduced US$150,000 threshold carried a statutory window to 14 July 2026; no extension law had been located on Costa Rica's official SCIJ database, which points to a reversion to the standard US$200,000, but some DGME-published checklists had reportedly not yet been updated. Confirm the live figure directly with DGME or a Costa Rican immigration attorney before committing funds.

Do I have to work with a Costa Rican company to keep Pensionado or Rentista status?

No — the opposite. Pensionado and Rentista residents cannot work as an employee of a Costa Rican company, though they can own a Costa Rican business and earn income from it. Inversionista residents can additionally collect income directly from their qualifying investment.

Is health insurance mandatory for these residency categories?

Yes. All three categories require enrollment in the CCSS (Caja Costarricense de Seguro Social), Costa Rica's public health and social-security system, as a condition of maintaining residency. Contributions are calculated as a percentage of your declared qualifying income.

How long until permanent residency?

Three years of temporary residency, provided you spend at least 180 days per year in Costa Rica, makes you eligible to apply for permanent residency, which removes the ongoing income/investment-maintenance requirement.

Do Americans pay US tax while living in Costa Rica on one of these visas?

Yes, fully. The US taxes citizens on worldwide income regardless of residency. Social Security and pension income stay fully US-taxable, rental or business income is reported the same as US-source income (with a foreign tax credit for any Costa Rican tax paid), and the Rentista deposit or an Inversionista investment account is a foreign financial account that triggers FBAR filing once your aggregate foreign accounts exceed US$10,000, with Form 8938 potentially applying as well.
