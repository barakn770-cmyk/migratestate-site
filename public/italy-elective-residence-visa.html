Italy Elective Residence Visa for Americans: The 2026 Guide

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated September 2026  ·  12 min read
The elective residence visa (visto per residenza elettiva) is Italy's residence route for people who can live entirely off passive income and never work. There is no legal minimum written into the decree — consulates judge "substantial and steady" income case by case — but roughly €31,000 a year for a single applicant is the figure most consulates and immigration lawyers actually cite. It bans all work, including remote work, and once you actually live in Italy you become an Italian taxpayer on worldwide income.

Italy has no golden visa and no digital-nomad-friendly investor track for retirees — what it has instead is the elective residence visa, a decades-old route built for exactly one kind of applicant: someone who can support themselves indefinitely without working a single day in Italy. For Americans drawn to a Tuscan hill town or a Puglian coastal city rather than Lisbon or Málaga, it is effectively the only door in. It is also one of the more consulate-dependent, judgment-call visas in Europe — the law sets no hard income number, and getting the file right means understanding both the immigration side and the Italian tax system you are about to join. Here is the 2026 picture.

What is the elective residence visa?

The visto per residenza elettiva is a long-stay Italian national visa (a "type D" visa) for non-EU citizens who intend to live in Italy on their own resources without engaging in any professional activity — employed, self-employed, or otherwise. "Elective" refers to the applicant's choice to relocate; it has nothing to do with elections or public office. It is squarely designed for retirees living on pensions, annuities, or investment income, and for financially independent people who have stopped working by choice.

In return you get genuine residence rights: the ability to live in Italy year-round, access to Italy's national health service once you register and pay the annual voluntary contribution, freedom of movement across the Schengen area, and — eventually — a path to permanent residence and citizenship. The visa itself is typically issued for one year and converts, after arrival, into a residence permit (permesso di soggiorno) that is then renewed.

The income requirement — and why it varies

This is the detail every applicant wants a single clean number for, and Italy deliberately doesn't provide one. Unlike Spain's IPREM-indexed threshold, the Italian decree implementing the elective residence visa requires only "adequate", "substantial and steady" private income — leaving each of Italy's roughly 60 US-facing consulates to set and apply its own practical bar. Some consulate pages, like New York's, publish a concrete benchmark; others, like Los Angeles and Chicago, describe the standard in general terms and evaluate each file individually.

Qualifying income is passive only: pensions and Social Security, rental income, dividends, interest, and annuity payments. Two years of US tax returns and recent bank or brokerage letters are the standard proof. Because the number floats by consulate — and because officers retain real discretion even above the commonly cited benchmark — immigration lawyers routinely advise showing comfortably more than the figure your specific consulate publishes, and confirming that figure directly rather than relying on a generic blog total. The one constant across every consulate: the income must be visibly passive, well-documented, and clearly sufficient to support the applicant (and any dependents) without needing to work.

Accommodation and the no-work rule

Before you can apply, you need a place to live already lined up: either a property deed for a home you own in Italy, or a lease registered with the Agenzia delle Entrate (Italy's tax authority) covering the full period requested. An informal rental agreement or a booking confirmation will not satisfy this requirement — the lease has to be the real, registered article, in your name, for a specific address.

The second non-negotiable condition is the one in the visa's name: no work of any kind. That means no local employment, no self-employment, no consulting, and — this is the part applicants most often misjudge — no remote work for a US employer or US clients either. Italian consulates evaluate the visa on the premise that your income is entirely unearned and will remain so. If you intend to keep any form of paid work going, including freelance or remote employment, the elective residence visa is the wrong instrument; Italy's separate digital nomad visa exists for exactly that situation and permits work.

Application process, fees and the permesso di soggiorno

Americans apply at the Italian consulate with jurisdiction over their US state of residence — this cannot be done from inside Italy or through a different consulate. The standard national-visa application fee is modest (roughly €116, payable in the local currency equivalent), but the real cost is in the supporting file: apostilled and translated proof of income, the registered lease or deed, proof of private health insurance valid in Italy, a clean criminal-background check, and — depending on the consulate — an in-person interview.

Once the visa is issued and you enter Italy, the clock starts immediately: you must report to the local Questura (police headquarters) to apply for your permesso di soggiorno (residence permit) within eight days of arrival. This is not a formality to get to later — missing the eight-day window can jeopardize your legal status even though the visa itself was validly issued. Fingerprinting and an in-person appointment follow, and the physical permit itself can take weeks to months to be issued depending on the province, though you are legally resident from the date you apply, not the date the card arrives.

Renewal, EU long-term residence and citizenship

The first permesso di soggiorno is typically valid for one year and is renewed thereafter, generally in further one- or two-year increments, each time re-demonstrating the same passive-income and accommodation conditions. Renewal is where consulate-level scrutiny becomes Questura-level scrutiny — if your circumstances or income have materially changed, be ready to show it clearly.

After five years of continuous, legal residence, you become eligible to apply for EU long-term resident status (the Italian implementation of EU Directive 2003/109/EC) — a more durable status with fewer periodic re-proofs and easier onward mobility within the EU. Italian citizenship by naturalization for a non-EU national takes considerably longer: ten years of continuous legal residence, a certified B1-level Italian language exam, a clean criminal record, and demonstrated adequate income at the time of application. There is no shortcut for elective-residence-visa holders specifically — the ten-year clock is the general rule for non-EU, non-spousal applicants.

Italian taxes: the 2024 residency test and your options

Since a 1 January 2024 reform of Article 2 of the TUIR (Italy's income-tax code), you are an Italian tax resident for a given year if you meet any one of four tests for more than 183 days (184 in a leap year): physical presence in Italy; your habitual abode (the place you return to and intend to keep as home); your domicile, now defined mainly by where your personal and family relationships are centered, rather than economic ties as under the old rule; or registration in the Anagrafe (the civil registry), which now creates only a rebuttable presumption of residence rather than an automatic one. In practice, anyone actually living year-round on an elective residence visa will trip at least the physical-presence test and become Italian tax resident.

Once resident, you owe IRPEF — Italy's progressive national income tax — on worldwide income. Following a recent simplification, the bracket structure runs roughly 23% up to €28,000, 33% from €28,000–€50,000, and 43% above €50,000, plus a regional surtax (roughly 1.2%–3.3%) and a smaller municipal surtax on top — pushing the effective top marginal rate close to 47% in some regions. Italy also levies IVIE (a wealth tax on foreign real estate, generally 1.06% of value) and IVAFE (a wealth tax on foreign financial assets) on tax residents, which catches a retained US brokerage account or a US home you didn't sell before moving.

Two alternative regimes are worth knowing before you pick a town. Foreign pensioners who move their tax residence to an eligible small municipality — up to 30,000 residents as of a 2026 threshold increase, across Sicily, Calabria, Sardinia, Campania, Basilicata, Abruzzo, Molise, Puglia, and designated earthquake-zone municipalities in Lazio, Marche and Umbria — can elect a flat 7% tax on all foreign-source income (pensions, rent, dividends, everything) for up to 10 years, under Article 24-ter TUIR, instead of ordinary progressive IRPEF. It is one of the most generous retiree tax regimes in Europe, but strictly geography-gated — it doesn't apply in Rome, Florence, or most of the north.

Separately, high-net-worth new residents anywhere in Italy can elect the flat-tax regime under Article 24-bis TUIR: a single flat annual charge on all foreign-source income regardless of amount, in place of IRPEF on that income. The charge doubled from €100,000 to €200,000 for anyone transferring residence after 10 August 2024, and Italy's 2026 Budget Law (Law 199/2025) raised it again — to €300,000 a year, plus €50,000 per qualifying family member, for residence transfers from 1 January 2026 onward. This regime is a different tool aimed at very high foreign income, not at typical elective-residence retirees, and pairs more naturally with Italy's investor visa; see our guide to Italy's investor visa for how the two interact.

US tax: your pension, Social Security and the treaty

Moving to Italy changes nothing about your US filing obligation: citizens and green-card holders keep filing US returns on worldwide income regardless of residence. The Foreign Earned Income Exclusion is essentially useless on this visa — it only shelters earned income, and by design an elective-residence applicant has none. Your pension, Social Security, rental income, dividends and interest all remain fully US-reportable.

Under Article 18 of the US-Italy income tax treaty, a private pension arising from past employment is generally taxable only in your country of residence — once you are Italian tax resident, that means Italy has the primary taxing right. US Social Security benefits are generally exempt from Italian tax under the treaty's protocol provisions. The catch that trips up even careful retirees: the treaty's saving clause preserves the United States' right to keep taxing its own citizens on this income essentially as if the treaty didn't exist, so in practice most American retirees in Italy end up reporting the pension and Social Security income on both returns and using the foreign tax credit to avoid paying twice — rather than the treaty article cleanly exempting either side. Your Italian bank accounts will also cross the FBAR threshold ($10,000 aggregate) and, above the higher thresholds, Form 8938; a property you own directly in Italy is not itself FBAR- or 8938-reportable, though the account you use to pay its bills usually is.

Step-by-step: how Americans get the elective residence visa

  1. Secure accommodation first. Buy a property or sign and register a lease covering the full requested period — you cannot apply without it.
  2. Confirm your consulate's current income expectation. Don't assume the ~€31,000 benchmark applies everywhere; ask the specific consulate covering your state.
  3. Assemble the financial file. Two years of US tax returns, recent bank/brokerage statements, pension or Social Security award letters — all apostilled and translated.
  4. Buy compliant health insurance valid in Italy for the full visa period.
  5. Book and attend the consulate appointment, pay the visa fee, and submit the complete file.
  6. Enter Italy and report to the Questura within 8 days to apply for your permesso di soggiorno; complete fingerprinting when scheduled.
  7. Register your tax residence and plan year one — decide, with a cross-border advisor, whether the 7% southern regime, the standard IRPEF brackets, or (for very high foreign income) the flat-tax regime fits your situation, and confirm your US filing obligations before your first full Italian tax year begins.
  8. Calendar renewal well before your permesso expires, re-proving income and accommodation each cycle.

If you're not American: what changes

The Italian rules above — the consulate-set income bar, the accommodation requirement, the total prohibition on work, the permesso di soggiorno process, and the tax regimes — are the same for every non-EU applicant regardless of passport. Only the home-country tax wrapper differs.

If you hold a non-US passport, swap the US-specific layer for your own country's rules. Check three things: (1) whether your home country continues to tax your pension and investment income after you leave — some tax by citizenship, most by residence, and exit-tax rules vary; (2) what foreign-asset and foreign-account reporting your home country requires once you hold Italian accounts and property; and (3) whether your country has a double-taxation treaty with Italy and how its pension article is worded — treaty pension provisions differ meaningfully between countries. EU/EEA citizens don't need this visa at all — they register residence directly under EU free-movement rules. Confirm the specifics with a tax adviser in your country of residence before you apply.

Is the elective residence visa worth it in 2026?

For a genuinely retired or financially independent American who wants to actually live in Italy — not just visit — the elective residence visa remains one of the more accessible European residence routes: no investment requirement, a passive-income bar that is real but not extreme for most comfortable retirees, and a clear if unhurried path to permanent residence and citizenship. The honest complication in 2026 is less the immigration file than the tax decision behind it: the 2024 residency-test reform makes it harder to quietly avoid becoming Italian tax resident while actually living there, so the real planning question is which regime fits — ordinary IRPEF, the 7% southern-Italy pensioner rate if your target town qualifies, or, for the small minority with very large foreign income, the €300,000 flat-tax route. Price the Italian and US tax pictures together, with a coordinated cross-border adviser, before you sign a lease — that decision matters more to your total cost of living than the visa fee ever will.

Frequently asked questions

How much income do you need for Italy's elective residence visa?

There is no single legal figure. The most commonly cited consular benchmark is around €31,000/year for a single applicant, roughly €38,000-€46,000 for a couple — but consulates evaluate "substantial and steady" passive income case by case, and some publish no fixed number at all. Confirm the current expectation with your specific consulate.

Can I work remotely for a US employer on this visa?

No. The elective residence visa is built around passive income and bans all work, including remote work for a foreign employer. If you plan to keep working, use Italy's separate digital nomad visa instead.

Does the elective residence visa make me an Italian tax resident?

In most cases yes, once you actually live there. Since a 2024 reform, Italy applies four alternative tests — physical presence, habitual abode, domicile (family ties), and Anagrafe registration — and meeting any one for 183+ days makes you tax resident on worldwide income.

How long until permanent residency or citizenship?

EU long-term resident status after five years of continuous legal residence. Italian citizenship by naturalization after ten years, plus a B1 Italian language certificate, a clean record and demonstrated income — the same general rule as other non-EU, non-spousal applicants.

Is there a lower-tax option for retirees?

Yes — foreign pensioners moving to an eligible town of up to 30,000 residents in Southern Italy can elect a flat 7% tax on all foreign-source income for up to 10 years under Article 24-ter TUIR, instead of ordinary progressive IRPEF.

What happens to my US pension and Social Security once I'm an Italian tax resident?

Under the US-Italy treaty, a private pension is generally taxable only in Italy once you're Italian tax resident, and US Social Security is generally treaty-exempt from Italian tax. But the treaty's saving clause lets the IRS keep taxing US citizens regardless, so most retirees file in both countries and use the foreign tax credit to avoid double taxation.
