Portugal D8 Digital Nomad Visa for Americans: The 2026 Guide

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated November 2026  ·  11 min read
The D8 is Portugal's visa for people whose income follows them — remote employees and freelancers paid by clients outside Portugal. In 2026 the income test is four times the Portuguese minimum wage: about €3,680/month. It runs through the same two-stage consular-then-AIMA process as the D7, leads toward citizenship on the same ten-year clock set by the 2026 nationality law, and comes with its own tax surprise: NHR 2.0 (IFICI) almost never reaches a D8 holder's income, but the US Foreign Earned Income Exclusion — which does nothing for a D7 pensioner — genuinely helps here.

Every "digital nomad visa" roundup lumps Portugal's D8 in with a dozen other countries' schemes, and most of them get the Portuguese version wrong in the same two places: the income number, which moves every year because it is pegged to the minimum wage, and the tax pitch, which still gets sold as an NHR-era story years after NHR closed. If you're an American drawing a US salary or freelance income while living in Portugal, this is the visa built for exactly that income type — and the two things that actually decide whether it works for you are the income math and the tax math, not the lifestyle marketing.

What is the D8 visa?

The D8, formally the visa for the exercise of professional activity provided remotely ("nómades digitais"), is Portugal's residence route for people who work for an employer or clients outside Portugal while living inside it. It was created as part of Portugal's immigration-law framework for non-EU/EEA nationals and sits alongside, not instead of, the older D7. Where the D7 is for income you receive whether you work or not — pensions, rent, dividends — the D8 is for income you actively earn: a salary from a US employer who lets you work remotely, or billings from freelance or consulting clients based outside Portugal.

Like the D7, the D8 residence version is a genuine relocation visa. It makes you a legal Portuguese resident, gives you access to the national health service and school enrolment for children, Schengen-area travel, and a path toward permanent residence and citizenship. It is not a loophole or a tourist-visa workaround — AIMA and the consulates treat it as a real immigration category with its own income test and its own paperwork.

Who qualifies — the income rule

The income test is the single number everyone wants, and it is simple in structure even though the exact euro figure changes every year. Portuguese law sets the D8 threshold at four times the national minimum wage (RMMG), averaged over your most recent three months of income before you apply. The minimum wage for 2026 is €920/month. Four times that is €3,680/month, or roughly €44,160 a year.

A note on that figure: the 4× formula itself, and the €920 2026 minimum wage, are each independently set by Portuguese law and regulation. The €3,680 figure is the arithmetic result of applying one to the other — at the time of writing, consulate and AIMA paperwork that explicitly prints "€3,680" for 2026 had not yet circulated widely, though the underlying formula is well established (the 2025 cycle used €870 × 4 = €3,480 on the same basis). Confirm the exact figure quoted by your consulate before you assemble your file, since a late-year update to the minimum wage occasionally lags into early-year visa paperwork.

Unlike the D7, which documents pensions and investment statements, the D8's income evidence is built around your work itself: an employment contract or a letter from your employer confirming remote work and salary, or — for freelancers and the self-employed — service contracts, invoices, and bank statements showing a steady stream of payments from outside Portugal. Three months of pay slips or deposits at or above the threshold is the baseline most consulates expect; a thin margin above the minimum, or income that looks like a single one-off project rather than an ongoing arrangement, invites the same discretionary scrutiny a borderline D7 file gets.

Residence visa vs temporary-stay visa

"The D8" is actually two different visas sharing one income test, and conflating them is the most common mistake in how this visa gets written about.

The Visto de Estada Temporária (temporary-stay visa) is a Schengen short-stay authorization for remote workers who want to try Portugal for under a year without becoming residents. It uses the same four-times-minimum-wage income test, but it creates no Portuguese residence, builds no time toward permanent residence or citizenship, and cannot simply be converted into one at the end of the stay.

The Visto de Residência (residence visa) is the long-stay version, and it is what almost every American asking about "the D8 digital nomad visa" actually means. Like the D7, it is a national visa obtained at a consulate abroad, then exchanged for an AIMA residence permit after you enter Portugal. Everything in the rest of this guide — the tax analysis, the citizenship timeline, the process — is about the residence version, not the short-stay one. If a "digital nomad visa" quote or checklist you've been sent doesn't specify which one it covers, ask before you pay anything.

The application process and timeline

The structure mirrors the D7 closely. You apply for the visa first, at the Portuguese consulate (or VFS Global visa centre) responsible for your US state of residence, submitting your income evidence, a Portuguese NIF, proof of accommodation in Portugal, and the standard background-check and insurance documents. Once granted, that visa lets you travel to Portugal and attend an appointment with AIMA (the Agency for Integration, Migration and Asylum) to be issued the actual residence permit.

AIMA sets the residence permit's specific validity period and renewal cadence, and — as with every AIMA-issued title — it is worth confirming the current figures directly with AIMA or your consulate at the time you apply rather than relying on an older guide, since residence-permit administration has been in flux since the 2025 immigration-law reform (Lei n.º 9/2025) restructured several of the underlying procedures. What does not change is the shape of the process: a NIF and Portuguese financial footprint arranged before you apply, a consular stage measured in weeks to a few months, and an AIMA stage completed after you arrive.

The tax catch: IFICI mostly doesn't reach you

This is the piece almost every digital-nomad roundup gets wrong, because it still writes as if the old NHR regime were available. It isn't. NHR closed to new applicants on 1 January 2025. Its replacement, IFICI (the Tax Incentive for Scientific Research and Innovation, often called "NHR 2.0"), offers a flat 20% rate on qualifying Portuguese-source income for ten years — a genuinely good deal, for the people who can actually use it.

The catch is that IFICI is gated by who employs you, not by your job title. The qualifying routes run through a Portuguese university or scientific research body, a company recognized by AICEP or IAPMEI as having relevant economic interest, an export-oriented company in specific listed activity codes or one holding an investment-incentive contract, R&D-incentive personnel certified through ANI, or a certified startup under the Startup Portugal program. A software engineer, designer, or consultant who is a genuine remote worker — paid by an ordinary foreign employer, or invoicing foreign clients directly — has no route into any of those employer-side gateways. The profession can look exactly like the kind of role IFICI was built for; the employer relationship is what disqualifies it. The practical result: most D8 holders get none of IFICI's benefit and are taxed like any other new Portuguese resident.

And that ordinary treatment is not nothing. Once you spend more than 183 days in Portugal in a calendar year — or otherwise maintain a home there as your habitual residence — you become a Portuguese tax resident, and Portugal taxes your worldwide income, including the US salary or freelance income that got you the visa in the first place, under its normal progressive rates. The US–Portugal tax treaty and the foreign tax credit prevent that income from being taxed twice in full, but they do not make it tax-free. Budget for an actual Portuguese tax bill, not an NHR-era fantasy.

US tax: where the FEIE actually helps

Here is the one place the D8 genuinely beats the D7 on US tax, and it is worth stating plainly because the two visas are so often treated as interchangeable. A D7 retiree's pension is passive, unearned income, and the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion simply does not apply to it, whatever that retiree reads online. A D8 holder's remote salary or freelance billings are earned income — wages or self-employment pay for personal services — which is exactly what the FEIE is built to shelter.

To claim it, you need a foreign tax home and either the bona fide residence test or the physical presence test (at least 330 full days outside the United States within any 12 consecutive months; the days don't need to be consecutive or fall inside one calendar year). Meet either test and you can exclude up to $132,900 of your 2026 earned income from US tax. Two things the exclusion does not do: it does not touch any passive income you also hold (investment income, a rental you keep on the side — that still needs the regular foreign-tax-credit analysis described in our foreign rental income guide), and it does not reach self-employment tax — a freelancer's excluded income still counts toward the 15.3% SE tax, a detail that surprises a lot of first-year D8 freelancers who assume the FEIE makes their US tax bill disappear entirely.

Stack the two systems correctly and the sequence looks like this: exclude up to the FEIE limit on the US return if you meet the residence or presence test; pay Portuguese tax on your worldwide income once you cross 183 days there; and use the foreign tax credit, not the FEIE, for whatever US tax liability remains on income above the exclusion or on income the FEIE doesn't reach. A cross-border accountant who files in both countries is the only way to actually get this sequencing right in your specific case.

Step-by-step checklist: D8 from decision to residence card

  1. Confirm your income is the right type. Active remote-work pay from a foreign employer or foreign clients qualifies; pensions and investment income do not — that's the D7.
  2. Check the current threshold with your consulate. Four times the minimum wage, roughly €3,680/month for 2026 — confirm the exact figure your consulate is applying before you assemble evidence.
  3. Get a Portuguese NIF remotely, through a fiscal representative or lawyer.
  4. Build the income-evidence file — an employer remote-work letter and pay slips, or freelance contracts, invoices, and three months of qualifying deposits.
  5. Secure accommodation in Portugal and the standard document package: passport, apostilled background check, health insurance, certified translations.
  6. Decide residence vs temporary-stay and apply at the correct consulate or VFS centre for your US state.
  7. Enter Portugal and attend your AIMA appointment to collect the residence permit, if you applied for the residence version.
  8. Model both tax systems before you cross 183 days — the FEIE on the US side, ordinary Portuguese tax (no NHR, almost never IFICI) on the Portuguese side.

If you're not American: what changes

The D8 itself works the same for every non-EU/EEA national. The same four-times-minimum-wage income test, the same residence-vs-temporary-stay split, and the same two-stage consular-and-AIMA process apply to a Briton, Canadian, Australian or Brazilian exactly as they apply to an American. What's US-specific is the FEIE analysis above and the worldwide-income starting point for US citizens.

If you're not a US person, drop the FEIE discussion and substitute your own country's rules. Check three things: (1) whether your home country taxes worldwide income or only domestic-source income once you move — many countries (the UK, Canada, Australia among them) tax primarily on residence, so relocating to Portugal may take you out of your home country's tax net rather than layering a second system on top; (2) your home country's own foreign-income and foreign-account reporting duties, which differ from the US FBAR/Form 8938 regime; and (3) whether a double-taxation treaty between your country and Portugal governs how your remote-work income is taxed once you're Portuguese tax resident. CPLP-country nationals also get the shorter seven-year naturalization clock under the 2026 nationality law, the same carve-out that applies on the D7. Confirm specifics with a tax adviser in your country of residence.

Is the D8 worth it in 2026?

For the remote worker or freelancer it was built for, yes — with the same clear eyes this guide has tried to bring to the D7. The income test is honest and predictable because it's tied to a public number every year, the process is no more burdensome than the D7's, and the visa does what it says: it lets you live in Portugal while working for people who aren't there. What it does not do is deliver the old NHR tax fantasy — budget for ordinary Portuguese tax on your worldwide income once you're resident, and treat IFICI as something almost nobody on this visa actually gets, rather than a benefit to plan around.

Where the D8 has a genuine edge over the D7 is on the US side: the FEIE is real relief on real earned income, in a way it simply never will be for a D7 pensioner. Get the income-type question right at the start — D8 for active work, D7 for passive income — model both tax systems before you cross the 183-day line, and the D8 delivers exactly the relocation it promises.

Frequently asked questions

How much income do you need for the Portugal D8 digital nomad visa in 2026?

Four times the Portuguese minimum wage. With the 2026 minimum wage at €920/month, that works out to €3,680/month (about €44,160/year) in average remote-work income over the three months before you apply. Confirm the exact figure with your consulate, since it is a calculated figure rather than one yet widely printed in 2026 paperwork.

What's the difference between the D8 residence visa and the D8 temporary-stay visa?

Both share the same income test. The temporary-stay visa (Visto de Estada Temporária) is a Schengen short-stay authorization for under a year that creates no residency. The residence visa (Visto de Residência) is the long-stay version, exchanged for an AIMA residence permit, and is what "the D8 digital nomad visa" almost always means.

Can D8 holders get the NHR or IFICI tax break?

Almost never. NHR closed to new applicants on 1 January 2025. Its replacement, IFICI, is gated by employer type — a Portuguese university or research body, an AICEP/IAPMEI-recognized company, an export-oriented or investment-incentive company, or a certified startup. A typical remote worker paid by an ordinary foreign employer or foreign clients has no route into any of those gateways.

How does the US Foreign Earned Income Exclusion work for a D8 holder?

Because D8 income is earned income (unlike a D7 pension), the FEIE genuinely applies. Meeting the bona fide residence test or the 330-day physical presence test lets you exclude up to $132,900 of 2026 earned income from US tax. It does not cover self-employment tax or any passive income you also hold.

How long does the D8 visa take, and how long until citizenship?

Like the D7, it's a two-stage consular-then-AIMA process, with AIMA setting the permit's validity and renewal terms. Under the nationality law in force since 19 May 2026 (Lei Orgânica 1/2026), naturalization requires ten years of legal residence for most non-EU nationals — the same clock as the D7, with no separate D8 rule.

What is the difference between the D8 and the D7 visa?

The D7 is for passive income — pensions, rent, dividends — at roughly €920/month. The D8 is for active remote-work income from outside Portugal, at roughly four times that, €3,680/month for 2026. Retirees and investors use the D7; salaried remote workers and freelancers use the D8.
