FBAR for Foreign Property Owners: When FinCEN Form 114 Applies (2026)

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated September 2026  ·  12 min read
The property is not the problem — the bank accounts around it are. Directly-owned foreign real estate is never itself reportable on the FBAR. But buying, closing on, and running that property almost always routes money through a foreign financial account — a notary escrow account, a local account for utilities and a property manager, a joint account with a co-owner — and those accounts are reportable if your combined foreign account balances exceed $10,000 at any single moment in the year. Miss the filing and the penalties run from a few thousand dollars for an honest mistake to criminal exposure for willful concealment.

What the FBAR actually reports

The Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts — universally called the FBAR, filed as FinCEN Form 114 — is not a tax form. It is a Bank Secrecy Act disclosure administered by the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN), a bureau of the Treasury Department, and it exists to give the US government visibility into money Americans hold outside the country. FinCEN's own guidance is direct about who owes it: a US person who has a financial interest in, or signature authority over, one or more foreign financial accounts must file an FBAR if the aggregate value of those accounts exceeded $10,000 at any time during the calendar year.

Two words in that sentence do all the work for a property buyer: accounts and aggregate. The FBAR reports accounts — bank accounts, brokerage accounts, and similar financial holdings — not real property. And it aggregates every qualifying account you touch, anywhere in the world, into a single test. A buyer who has never thought of themselves as having a "foreign bank account" in any meaningful sense can still trip the threshold the moment a notary, a property manager, or a co-buyer opens an account that has your name or your signature on it.

The $10,000 aggregate threshold, explained

The number itself has not changed in decades and it is genuinely low by the standards of a real estate transaction: $10,000, in US dollars, aggregated across every reportable account, tested at the highest point each account reached at any time during the year — not the year-end balance, not an average, and not the amount that was "really yours" if the account also held other people's money.

The mechanics matter more than the headline number:

  • It is a peak test, not a snapshot. If $180,000 sat in a notary escrow account for eleven days before closing and the account was empty by December 31, the $180,000 high balance is what counts, not the zero balance at year-end.
  • It is aggregated across every account, not tested account-by-account. A $4,000 utilities account, a $3,500 property-management account, and a $3,000 balance left in the notary's account after closing look immaterial individually. Added together they clear $10,000 and the filing requirement is triggered even though no single account ever came close on its own.
  • Every account is converted to US dollars at the Treasury's year-end exchange rate for the purpose of the FBAR calculation, so a foreign-currency balance that looks modest at home can convert into a larger dollar figure depending on the rate that year.
  • The threshold is per person, not per account and not per property. A married couple who each have their own signature authority over the same joint account may each independently owe the filing.

Owners frequently misjudge this in one direction: they assume that because the property cost far more than $10,000, of course they must be "over the FBAR limit," and separately assume that because no single account held much money, they are safely under it. Both instincts are wrong for the same reason — the property's price is irrelevant to the FBAR calculation, and the accounts add together.

Why the property itself is never on the form

This is worth stating plainly because it is the single most common point of confusion: directly-held foreign real estate is not a foreign financial account, and it is never listed on the FBAR, regardless of its value. A $2 million villa held in your own name generates zero FBAR exposure by itself. Neither does a mortgage on it, directly-held stock certificates, or physical gold or precious metals you hold yourself rather than through an account. The FBAR's scope, as FinCEN defines it, covers accounts maintained with a financial institution — bank accounts, securities and brokerage accounts, mutual funds and pooled investment funds, and certain insurance or annuity products with cash value. A deed is not one of those things.

Where this goes wrong for buyers is the assumption that "the property is clean, so I'm clean." The real estate escapes the FBAR entirely — but almost no international property purchase happens without money moving through at least one foreign financial account along the way, and it is that account, not the villa, that FinCEN wants to know about. Our companion guide on US tax reporting for property owned abroad walks through the fuller set of forms — FATCA, Form 8938, and the entity-level filings that appear if you hold the property through a foreign company — that sit alongside the FBAR once ownership gets more complex than a straightforward personal purchase.

The accounts a property purchase actually creates

Almost every cross-border purchase creates at least one, and usually several, foreign financial accounts along the way, even when the buyer never set out to "open a foreign bank account." The most common are:

AccountTypically appears whenFBAR-relevant?
Notary or attorney escrow / trust accountClosing, in civil-law countries where the notary or a lawyer holds funds pending transferUsually, if held for your benefit or you can direct it
Local checking account opened to pay for the propertyWiring the purchase price, paying depositsYes, once funded
Local account to pay utilities and a property managerAfter closing, for ongoing operating costsYes, if you have a financial interest or signature authority
Joint account with a co-owner or local property managerShared ownership, or a manager who needs to pay bills locallyYes, for every US person who is a joint owner or signatory
Foreign mortgage or loan accountFinancing the purchase locallyNo — a liability, not a reportable financial account
The real estate itselfAlwaysNo — never reportable on the FBAR

The pattern across the reportable rows is the same: whenever money you have an interest in, or control over, sits with a foreign bank, notary, or financial institution, it is a candidate for the FBAR. Whenever it is a debt you owe, or an asset you hold directly without an institution in between, it is not.

Three concrete examples

Example 1 — the notary escrow account. An American buys an apartment in a civil-law country where closings run through the notary's client trust account rather than a direct bank-to-bank wire. The buyer wires $220,000 to the notary two weeks before closing; the notary disburses it to the seller and pays transfer taxes at signing. The account touched $220,000 during the year and was empty before and after. Because the funds were held for the buyer's benefit pending the closing, this account is a strong candidate to count as a foreign financial account the buyer had a financial interest in — meaning the $10,000 threshold was cleared for that calendar year, and an FBAR is owed for it, even though the buyer never thought of it as "my bank account" and the balance is zero by December 31.

Example 2 — the utilities and property-manager account. A retiree buys a condo to use seasonally and opens a small local checking account so a property manager can pay the electricity, water, HOA dues, and their own modest fee. The account never holds more than $4,000 at a time. Standing alone it looks trivial. But if the retiree also left $3,000 in the closing account after settlement and has signature authority on a joint account with their adult child who co-signed the purchase, the three balances aggregate — and $4,000 plus $3,000 plus a joint account balance can cross $10,000 even though no single account ever felt significant.

Example 3 — the joint account with a local property manager. An owner of a short-term rental abroad sets up a joint local account with the property-management company that handles bookings, so guest deposits and cleaning costs flow through one place both parties can access. The owner is a named joint holder with the ability to direct funds. Even though the property manager funds and largely operates the account, and even though most of the money passing through belongs functionally to the rental business rather than the owner personally, the owner's status as a joint accountholder with control over disposition brings the account into the FBAR calculation in full. Signature authority: when it isn't even your money

The most overlooked trigger in this whole area is signature authority, because it does not require that any of the money be yours. FinCEN's guidance treats a person as having signature authority when they can control the disposition of money or assets in an account by direct communication to the institution holding it — whether by signature, oral instruction, or another means. Financial interest and signature authority are two independent, either/or tests: meeting either one is enough on its own to bring an account into your FBAR calculation.

This is precisely the situation many property buyers land in without noticing. If you gave a local property manager, an attorney, or a family member a power of attorney to close on your behalf, or if you can call the bank and direct payments out of an account your property manager mainly uses, you likely have signature authority over that account — and its balance counts toward your $10,000 threshold regardless of whose money is actually sitting in it. A US person who never deposited a dollar into a foreign account, but who can instruct the bank on how funds in it are disbursed, can still owe an FBAR filing.

How and when to file

The FBAR is filed electronically through FinCEN's BSA E-Filing System, using FinCEN Form 114, and it is filed entirely separately from your federal tax return — it is never attached to, or submitted alongside, your Form 1040. The IRS's own FBAR guidance is explicit on the deadline: the FBAR is an annual report, due April 15 of the year following the calendar year being reported, and FinCEN grants an automatic extension to October 15 to any filer who misses the April date, with no separate extension request required. In practice, this means a US property buyer effectively has until mid-October to get the prior year's FBAR filed without needing to ask permission — but the report still covers the full calendar year, including any brief spike through a notary account back in January or February.

Filing money that moved through your own accounts to buy the property is a separate question from how you funded the wire in the first place. Civil penalties: non-willful vs. willful

The penalty structure under 31 U.S.C. § 5321 splits sharply on a single question: was the failure to file willful? The statute sets base amounts, and both figures are adjusted for inflation each year — the numbers below reflect the most recent published adjustment.

Violation typeStatutory baseInflation-adjusted (most recent)Key condition
Non-willfulUp to $10,000 per violationUp to $16,536 per violationNo penalty at all if there was reasonable cause and the account balance was otherwise properly reported
WillfulGreater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balanceMinimum of $165,353, or 50% of the balance if higherApplies per year, per violation, and can be assessed for each account and each year not filed

Two features of this table matter more than the raw numbers. First, the non-willful penalty carries a genuine reasonable-cause exception — an honest, well-documented mistake by someone who did not know about the requirement is not automatically penalized, though relying on that exception after the fact is a much weaker position than filing correctly from the start. Second, "willful" in this context does not require an intent to evade tax or hide money from the government specifically; courts have found willfulness in cases of reckless disregard for a known or knowable legal duty, which means a buyer who was told about the FBAR requirement and simply never got around to it is in a meaningfully more exposed position than one who never had reason to suspect an obligation existed at all.

Criminal exposure

Willful FBAR violations are not only a civil matter. Under 31 U.S.C. § 5322, a person who willfully violates the FBAR filing or recordkeeping requirements can face a fine of up to $250,000, imprisonment of up to five years, or both. Where the violation occurs while also violating another federal law, or as part of a pattern of illegal activity involving more than $100,000 in a twelve-month period, the exposure rises to a fine of up to $500,000, imprisonment of up to ten years, or both. Criminal FBAR cases are rare in absolute numbers and are concentrated in cases involving deliberate concealment — undisclosed accounts, false statements to the bank or to the IRS, or funds moved specifically to stay under the radar — rather than ordinary property buyers who simply did not realize an escrow or utilities account counted. But the statute exists, and it is the backstop that makes "I'll deal with it later" a genuinely bad strategy once you know an account is reportable.

FBAR vs. Form 8938 (FATCA)

The FBAR is frequently confused with Form 8938, the Statement of Specified Foreign Financial Assets required under FATCA, and property buyers sometimes assume filing one satisfies the other. It does not — they are separate filings, with separate agencies, separate thresholds, and meaningfully different scope.

Married filing jointly$10,000 aggregate, same for both spouses' combined interestRoughly double the single thresholds above, depending on residence

A US property buyer with modest foreign accounts can easily owe the FBAR while falling well short of the much higher Form 8938 threshold — which is exactly the trap this article is about. Fixing a missed or late FBAR

Discovering a past-due FBAR after the fact is common, and it is not automatically a criminal-exposure event. For taxpayers whose non-compliance was not willful, the IRS's Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures remain the standard route back into compliance: a taxpayer certifies, under penalty of perjury, that the failure resulted from negligence, inadvertence, mistake, or a good-faith misunderstanding of the requirements — not willful conduct — and then files delinquent FBARs alongside amended or delinquent tax returns for the relevant years, provided they are not already under IRS civil examination or criminal investigation. Whether a given owner's facts genuinely support a non-willful certification is not something a general guide can determine; it depends on what they knew, when they knew it, and what a reasonable person in their position would have understood about the requirement. Get that judgment wrong in either direction — over-claiming non-willfulness, or filing late without any correction process at all — and the position gets worse, not better. This is squarely a conversation to have with a cross-border tax advisor before you file anything, not after.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to file an FBAR just because I own property abroad?

Not because of the property itself. Directly-held foreign real estate is not a reportable account and is never listed on the FBAR. What triggers the filing is the foreign bank or brokerage accounts you use to buy, fund, and run the property — an escrow account at the notary, a local account to pay utilities and a property manager, or a joint account with a co-owner. If the combined high balance of all your foreign financial accounts exceeds $10,000 at any point in the year, you must file, even if the property itself never appears on the form.

What is the FBAR threshold and how is it calculated?

$10,000, aggregated across every foreign financial account you have a financial interest in or signature authority over, tested at any single moment during the calendar year — not on December 31 and not as an average. You find the highest balance each account reached during the year in its local currency, convert each to US dollars using the Treasury's year-end exchange rate, and add every account together. A wire that passes through a notary escrow account for two weeks can push you over the line even if the account is empty by year-end.

Is a notary or escrow account used to close on foreign property reportable?

Usually, yes, if the funds are held in your name or for your benefit and you can direct their disposition. Many closings abroad route the purchase price through a notary trust account, an escrow account, or the seller's attorney's client account before it reaches the seller. If you have a financial interest in that account, or signature authority to instruct where the money goes, it counts as a foreign financial account for FBAR purposes for the year the funds passed through it, even if the balance was zero on both January 1 and December 31.

What happens if I don't file the FBAR?

It depends on whether the failure was willful. A non-willful violation carries a civil penalty of up to $10,000 per violation under the statute, adjusted for inflation to $16,536 as of the 2025 adjustment, though no penalty applies if there was reasonable cause and the account was otherwise properly reported. A willful violation carries a civil penalty of the greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance at the time of the violation, adjusted for inflation to a $165,353 minimum as of the 2025 adjustment. Willful violations can also carry criminal penalties under 31 U.S.C. 5322 of up to $250,000 and five years in prison, or up to $500,000 and ten years if combined with another federal violation or a pattern of illegal activity.

What's the difference between the FBAR and Form 8938?

They are separate filings with separate thresholds, forms, and destinations, and you may owe both. The FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) is filed with FinCEN through the BSA E-Filing System, entirely separate from your tax return, and its threshold is a flat $10,000 aggregate for every filer. Form 8938 is filed with the IRS attached to your Form 1040, and its threshold is much higher and varies by filing status and residence — for example $50,000/$75,000 for a single filer living in the US, or $200,000/$300,000 for a single filer living abroad. Form 8938 also covers some assets the FBAR does not, such as foreign securities held directly outside an account. Directly-held foreign real estate is not required on either form.

I only have signature authority over an account my property manager uses — do I still have to report it?

Yes. FBAR reporting is triggered by a financial interest in an account or by signature authority over it, and the two are independent tests. If you can instruct the foreign bank on how funds in the account are disposed of — even an account funded and mainly used by a local property manager to pay bills on your behalf — you have signature authority and the account counts toward your $10,000 aggregate, regardless of whose money is actually inside it or how small the balance runs.

How and when do I file the FBAR?

Electronically, through FinCEN's BSA E-Filing System, using FinCEN Form 114 — it is never filed with, or attached to, your federal tax return. The annual due date is April 15 of the year following the calendar year being reported, and FinCEN grants filers who miss that date an automatic extension to October 15, with no extension request required.

Can I fix a late or missed FBAR without being penalized?

There is a path for taxpayers whose non-compliance was not willful. The IRS's Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures let a taxpayer who can certify that a failure to report resulted from negligence, inadvertence, mistake, or a good-faith misunderstanding of the rules — not willful conduct — come into compliance by filing delinquent FBARs and amended or delinquent returns under defined terms, provided they are not already under IRS civil examination or criminal investigation. Whether you qualify as non-willful, and which track applies, depends on your specific facts, so this is worth confirming with a cross-border tax advisor before you file anything.
