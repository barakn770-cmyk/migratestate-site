Selling Property in Mexico as a US Citizen, 2026

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated July 2026  ·  11 min read
Two tax systems bill the same sale, and the reliefs fight each other. In Mexico a non-resident seller pays income tax (ISR) that the notary withholds at closing — either 25% of the gross price or 35% of the net gain. The US then taxes the same gain at up to 23.8%, offset by a foreign tax credit for the Mexican ISR and, if the home qualifies, the Section 121 exclusion. The trap: claim Section 121 and you may have no US tax left for the Mexican credit to cancel — so the ISR becomes a pure cost.

What Mexico charges: the 25% vs 35% choice

Mexico taxes the profit on a property sale through ISR (impuesto sobre la renta), its income tax. For a seller who is a Mexican non-resident for tax purposes — which most US-based owners are — the law offers two ways to compute it, and the notario público calculates and withholds the tax at closing, remitting it to the tax authority (SAT) before the deed can transfer. You do not get to pay it later on a US-style return; it comes out of your proceeds on the day.

The two methods are:

MethodRate & baseWhat it takes to use it
25% of grossFlat 25% of the total sale price, no deductionsThe default. Applies when you lack an RFC or documentation of your cost and improvements
35% of netUp to 35% on the net gain after inflation-adjusted cost basis, documented improvements and selling expensesRequires a Mexican tax ID (RFC) and valid tax invoices (facturas) for everything you deduct

The instinct is to grab whichever number is smaller, and on a long-held, well-documented property the 35% net route usually wins because inflation indexing (using Mexico's INPC price index) inflates your original cost and shrinks the taxable gain. But the choice is not purely a Mexican decision for an American — the two methods produce different amounts of creditable foreign tax, and as we explain below, paying a little more in Mexico is sometimes the cheaper answer once the US side is added in.

To use the 35% net method you need to have an RFC in place and hold facturas for every improvement you want to deduct — the receipt from your contractor is not enough; it must be a CFDI tax invoice tied to your RFC. Owners who renovated in cash years ago and kept no facturas are often pushed into the 25% gross method by default, which is exactly the situation to avoid.

The residence exemption you probably can't use

Mexico does have a generous main-home exemption — the casa habitación relief — that exempts the gain on a primary residence up to roughly 700,000 UDIs (about 5.9 million pesos in 2026, at a UDI of around 8.5 pesos), usable once every three years. The problem for most readers of this page is eligibility: the exemption is designed for Mexican tax residents who hold an RFC and can prove the property was genuinely their home with utility bills, bank statements and official ID (INE) showing the address in their name.

A US citizen who lives in the United States and owns a Mexican beach condo as a non-resident generally cannot claim it, and is taxed under the 25%/35% non-resident rules instead. An American who has actually relocated to Mexico, holds temporary or permanent residency, has an RFC and can document the home as their residence may qualify — but that is a different taxpayer profile, and the notario will want the paperwork. Do not assume the exemption applies because you have heard Mexico has one; confirm your residency status with the notario well before closing, because it changes the whole calculation.

Fideicomiso: payoff and why it isn't a foreign trust

If your property sits inside the restricted zone — roughly 50 km from the coast or 100 km from a border, which covers most of the resort markets Americans buy in — you almost certainly hold it through a fideicomiso, a bank trust that holds legal title while you keep all the economic rights. When you sell, the trust has to be closed out or assigned to the buyer, and the trustee bank charges a one-time cancellation fee of roughly US$1,000–$1,500. Budget for it; it is separate from the ISR and the agent commission.

For US tax the fideicomiso is easy to get wrong. Because it has the word "trust" in it, sellers panic about foreign-trust reporting. The IRS settled this: under Revenue Ruling 2013-14, a Mexican land trust of this kind is not treated as a foreign trust — you are regarded as owning the underlying real estate directly. That means no Form 3520 or Form 3520-A is required merely because you hold Mexican property in a fideicomiso, and the sale is reported exactly as a direct property sale would be. (This is the ruling to cite; it is not Revenue Procedure 2020-17, which deals with certain foreign retirement trusts and is sometimes confused with it.)

What it actually costs to sell

The transaction taxes on a Mexican purchase — the acquisition tax (ISAI) and the bulk of the notary fees — fall on the buyer. The seller's costs are the capital-gains ISR, the agent, and the trust wind-down. Here is the seller's side of the ledger.

A power of attorney to a Mexican lawyer or the notary's office lets an American who no longer lives in Mexico complete the sale remotely, which matters because the notario is central to a Mexican closing in a way US sellers do not expect.

How the US taxes your Mexican sale

The US result turns on three questions: was the property your principal residence, how did the dollar move against the peso, and did you ever rent it out. Here is the whole picture on one page.

ElementTreatment for a US citizen selling in 2026
Mexican ISR paidCreditable on Form 1116 as a foreign income tax, up to the US tax on the same gain
US capital gainsWorldwide gain taxable at 0/15/20% long-term, plus 3.8% NIIT
Section 121 exclusionUp to $250K / $500K if it was your main home 24 of the last 60 months
Gain measured inUS dollars — basis at the purchase-date rate, proceeds at the sale-date rate
Peso mortgage repaidSeparate Section 988 ordinary-income gain, up to 37%
Depreciation if ever rentedRecaptured as unrecaptured Section 1250 gain, up to 25%
Fideicomiso holdingDirect ownership under Rev. Rul. 2013-14 — no Form 3520/3520-A
Where it is reportedForm 8949 and Schedule D (plus Schedule E / Form 4797 if rented)

The dollar-basis rule is the one that quietly enlarges the US number. The IRS ignores what your condo did in pesos: your basis is the purchase price translated at the exchange rate on the day you bought, and your proceeds are translated at the rate on the day you sold. Because the peso has swung widely against the dollar over the past decade, a modest peso gain can translate into a much larger — or smaller — dollar gain than the Mexican return shows. The two tax authorities are measuring different things.

The Section 121 exclusion is your main US defence, and it works on a Mexican home exactly as on a house in Arizona: up to $250,000 of gain excluded for a single filer, $500,000 filing jointly, provided you owned and lived in it as your principal residence for at least 24 of the 60 months before sale. A vacation rental that was never your main home does not qualify. For the detail, see our guide to the Section 121 exclusion on a foreign home sale.

The credit trap most sellers miss

Here is the nuance almost every "selling in Mexico" article skips. A US citizen has, in principle, three reliefs available: the Mexican residence exemption, the US Section 121 exclusion, and the foreign tax credit for Mexican ISR. You very rarely get to use all three, because they cannibalise one another.

The foreign tax credit only shelters US tax that actually exists on the same foreign-source gain. If Section 121 excludes your entire gain, your US capital-gains tax on the sale is zero — and a credit against zero is worth nothing. The Mexican ISR you paid at the notary's desk does not come back to you; it sits as an unused credit in the passive-income basket of Form 1116, carries forward ten years, and in most personal-residence cases is never usable. In that scenario the "free" US exclusion has effectively cost you the full Mexican tax.

The counterintuitive result: when Section 121 covers the gain, paying more Mexican tax is pure waste — so the goal flips to minimising the Mexican ISR (the 35% net method, full facturas, residency exemption if you truly qualify). But when the gain runs past the Section 121 limit, or the home never qualified, the Mexican ISR becomes valuable because it is creditable against real US tax — and a higher, fully-creditable Mexican bill can leave your combined worldwide tax unchanged while a lower Mexican bill just hands the difference to the IRS. The right Mexican election depends on your US position, which is why the two sides must be modelled together, not in sequence.

This is also why the Mexican ISR is worth documenting carefully even when it feels painful at closing: on a large, taxable gain it is the single biggest lever you have against the US bill through Form 1116. The mortgage twist: Section 988

If you financed the purchase with a peso-denominated mortgage from a Mexican bank, the loan has its own separate US tax life. Under Section 988, repaying foreign-currency debt when the dollar is stronger than it was at origination produces a currency gain on the debt itself, taxed as ordinary income at rates up to 37%. It is computed independently of the house, and — crucially — Section 121 does not touch it, and the property's foreign tax credit does not offset it either. You can fully exclude the gain on the residence and still owe ordinary-income tax on the mortgage payoff.

Most Americans buy in Mexico for cash or with US-dollar developer financing, in which case Section 988 does not arise. But if you took a peso loan, run the calculation on its own. We walk through the mechanics in our guide to the foreign mortgage phantom gain.

Selling step by step

  1. Model both sides first. Translate your basis and expected proceeds into dollars, apply Section 121 if the home qualifies, and see what US tax actually remains — that answer decides whether you want the Mexican ISR high or low.
  2. Assemble your facturas. Gather CFDI invoices for every improvement and get (or confirm) your RFC, so the 35% net method is available and your Mexican cost basis is as high as the documents allow.
  3. Check your Mexican residency status. Confirm with the notario whether you are a resident who can use the casa habitación exemption or a non-resident taxed at 25%/35% — it changes everything.
  4. Clear the predial. Settle the annual property tax; the notary cannot close until it is current.
  5. Wind down the fideicomiso. If the property is in a bank trust, coordinate the cancellation or assignment and budget the US$1,000–$1,500 fee.
  6. Close at the notary. The notario computes and withholds the ISR, remits it to SAT, and executes the deed; keep the withholding receipt — it is your proof of foreign tax paid.
  7. Handle any peso mortgage separately. Run the Section 988 calculation on the loan payoff and report any gain as ordinary income.
  8. Report the sale in the US. File Form 8949 and Schedule D, claim Section 121 with adjustment code H if it applies, and claim the Mexican ISR on Form 1116 — but only expect a real benefit if there is US tax left to credit.

If you're not American: what changes

The Mexican side of the sale is identical for any foreign seller. The 25%/35% non-resident ISR, the notary withholding, the casa habitación eligibility rules, the fideicomiso cancellation fee, the agent commission and the predial clearance do not change with your passport. What is purely American is the wrapper: worldwide taxation, Section 121, the dollar-basis measurement, Section 988 on a peso mortgage, Form 1116 and Form 8949.

If you are not a US person, drop the IRS layer and substitute the rules of your own country of tax residence. Check three things. First, whether your home country taxes gains on foreign property at all, and whether it offers its own main-residence relief — Canada's principal residence exemption, the UK's private residence relief and Israel's single-dwelling exemption each have their own tests, and Canadians in particular should note Canada also taxes the currency element of the gain. Second, whether you have foreign asset or account reporting obligations at home covering the Mexican property and the sale proceeds. Third, whether a double-taxation treaty exists between your country and Mexico, remembering that immovable property is almost always taxable first where it sits and relieved second at home — so the Mexican ISR is usually creditable against your domestic tax in much the way it is for an American. Confirm the specifics with a tax adviser where you are resident.

Is it worth selling in 2026?

Mexican resort markets have appreciated sharply since the pandemic, and for a well-positioned owner 2026 is a reasonable time to take the gain. The question is not really whether to sell but how to structure the exit so two tax authorities do not both take a full bite. The owners who do well are the ones who decide the Mexican ISR method with the US answer already in hand: minimise Mexican tax when Section 121 will cover the US gain, and maximise the creditable Mexican tax — with complete facturas — when the gain is large and fully taxable in the US.

Get it wrong and the failure modes are expensive and familiar: pushed into the 25% gross method because the improvement facturas were never kept; a fully-excluded US gain that stranded a five-figure Mexican credit; a peso mortgage payoff that produced an ordinary-income Section 988 bill nobody warned about. Get it right — basis translated properly, the ISR method chosen against the US position, the fideicomiso wound down cleanly, depreciation recapture identified if the place was ever rented — and a Mexican sale can be one of the more tax-efficient exits an American can make abroad, precisely because the ISR, when it is needed, is genuinely creditable.

Frequently asked questions

How much capital gains tax does a US citizen pay selling property in Mexico?

On the Mexican side, a non-resident seller pays income tax (ISR) computed by the notary at closing as either 25% of the gross sale price with no deductions, or 35% of the net gain after inflation-adjusted cost, improvements with valid facturas, and selling expenses. The 25% gross figure is the default; the 35% net option requires a Mexican tax ID (RFC) and proper documentation but usually produces a lower bill on a long-held property. On top of that, the United States taxes the same gain, offset by a foreign tax credit for the Mexican ISR and, where it applies, the Section 121 exclusion.

Can I use the Mexican primary-residence exemption if I live in the US?

Usually not. Mexico's casa habitación exemption removes tax on up to roughly 700,000 UDIs of gain (about 5.9 million pesos in 2026, once every three years), but it is available to Mexican tax residents who hold an RFC and can prove the home was their residence with utility bills and official ID in their name. A US citizen who lives in the United States and holds the property as a non-resident generally cannot claim it, and is taxed under the 25%/35% non-resident rules instead.

Can I claim the US Section 121 exclusion on a Mexican home?

Yes, if it was genuinely your principal residence. Section 121 has no geographic limit, so a US citizen who owned and used a Mexican home as their main home for at least 24 of the 60 months before sale can exclude up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 filing jointly. A vacation home or a rental that was never your main residence does not qualify. Note the interaction: if Section 121 wipes out the US gain, there may be no US tax left for the Mexican ISR credit to offset, so the Mexican tax becomes a real out-of-pocket cost.

Do I have to report the sale to the IRS even though Mexico already taxed it?

Yes. As a US citizen you report the sale on Form 8949 and Schedule D regardless of what Mexico collected. You then claim the Mexican ISR as a foreign tax credit on Form 1116 to avoid double taxation, but the credit is limited to the US tax on that same foreign-source gain. A fideicomiso (bank trust) holding is treated as direct ownership under IRS Revenue Ruling 2013-14, so it does not trigger foreign-trust forms 3520 or 3520-A.

What does it cost to sell a Mexican property held in a fideicomiso?

Beyond the ISR capital-gains tax, a seller typically pays an estate-agent commission of 5%-8% plus 16% IVA, and if the property sits in a fideicomiso a one-time trust cancellation fee of roughly US$1,000-$1,500 to the trustee bank. The buyer, not the seller, pays the acquisition tax and most notary fees. You must also be current on your annual predial (property tax) before the notary will close.
