Philippines SRRV Retirement Visa for Americans (2026): Deposit Options, Property Conversion, Tax

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated October 2026  ·  11 min read
The Special Resident Retiree's Visa (SRRV) is the Philippine Retirement Authority's indefinite-stay visa for foreign retirees. The mainstream SRRV Classic route requires a US-dollar time deposit of $15,000–$50,000, depending on your age and whether you have a qualifying pension, which can later be converted into a condo purchase or long-term lease. It grants multiple-entry, indefinite stay with no minimum day requirement — but it does not open the door to owning land, and the deposit itself can create US FBAR and Form 8938 filings.

Of all the retirement-visa marketing aimed at Americans, the Philippines' Special Resident Retiree's Visa is one of the oldest and most misunderstood programs still running. It is not a golden visa in the European sense — there is no property purchase requirement baked into eligibility, no path to citizenship through investment alone, and the headline number people quote (a flat "$10,000" or "$20,000") usually reflects an outdated or incomplete reading of a program with several tiers. Here is what actually qualifies an American in 2026, run by the Philippine Retirement Authority (PRA) under the Bureau of Immigration.

What the SRRV actually is

The SRRV is a special non-immigrant visa, administered by PRA, that lets a qualifying foreign retiree live in the Philippines indefinitely with multiple-entry travel privileges — no annual tourist-visa extensions, no re-entry permits, no minimum number of days you must spend in the country each year to keep it. The core mechanism is a US-dollar time deposit placed with a PRA-accredited Philippine bank, sized to your age and pension status, which stands as your qualifying "investment" for as long as you hold the visa.

Because the deposit (rather than a property purchase) is the default qualifying asset, the SRRV is best understood as a deposit-based retirement residency that can later be redirected into real estate if you want — not a property-investment visa in its own right. That distinction matters for both your Philippine real-estate planning and your US tax reporting, covered below.

SRRV program options and deposit tiers

PRA runs several SRRV categories. The one the large majority of American retirees use is SRRV Classic; the others exist for narrower situations.

Age bracketWith a qualifying pensionWithout a pension
50 or olderUS$15,000US$30,000
40–49US$25,000US$50,000

A "qualifying pension" means a documented lifetime pension of at least US$800 per month for a single applicant or US$1,000 per month for an applicant with dependents. The deposit must be placed in US dollars with a PRA-accredited bank and generally cannot be withdrawn while the visa is active, except as described under "Exit and withdrawal rules" below.

Beyond Classic, PRA also publishes narrower categories — SRRV Smile (a deposit-only option without the pension-based tiering), SRRV Human Touch (aimed at retirees who need ongoing medical or caregiving support, historically paired with a monthly health-care spending requirement), and two Courtesy categories reserved for former Filipino citizens and for retired diplomats, international-organization officers, retired foreign military from countries with Philippine defense ties, and certain high-achieving or philanthropic applicants, each at a substantially lower deposit level than Classic. PRA restructured several of these deposit amounts in 2025, and this session could not confirm the current post-restructure dollar figures for Smile and Human Touch against a live PRA publication — if either program fits your situation, verify the exact current deposit directly with PRA or a Philippine immigration lawyer before relying on any third-party figure, including older figures published elsewhere on this site.

Converting the deposit into a condo or lease

One of the SRRV's more useful — and more frequently misunderstood — features is that the required time deposit does not have to stay a time deposit forever. Once your visa is issued, PRA allows you to apply to convert the deposit into an "active investment," most commonly a condominium unit purchased in your own name or a long-term lease of a house and lot. If you convert, the investment must be worth at least the minimum qualifying deposit amount and must remain in place for as long as you hold the SRRV; PRA requires notice of the conversion and ongoing proof that the qualifying investment is intact. This is the mechanism that lets an SRRV holder end up owning an actual Manila, Cebu or Davao condo rather than parking cash indefinitely — see our guide to buying property in the Philippines for the purchase mechanics, closing costs and taxes once you convert.

Why the SRRV doesn't open the door to land

The SRRV is an immigration status, not a property-rights exception, and it does not touch the Philippine Constitution's foreign land-ownership ban. Under Article XII, Section 7 of the 1987 Constitution, land may be transferred or conveyed only to Filipino citizens or to corporations or associations at least 60% Filipino-owned — a rule that applies to SRRV holders exactly as it applies to any other foreigner. What an American on an SRRV can do: own a condominium unit outright, subject to the 40% foreign-ownership cap on any single condominium project under the Condominium Act; own a building separately from the land beneath it; or lease land long-term. None of that changes because you hold an SRRV rather than a tourist visa — the visa buys you residency and stay privileges, not a different property-rights regime. Stay privileges and dependants

The practical appeal of the SRRV is what it removes from your life, not just what it grants. Holders get indefinite, multiple-entry stay — no tourist-visa extensions, no annual bureaucratic renewal of your right to be in the country, no re-entry permit before an international trip. A spouse and dependent children can generally be included on the same application for an additional deposit and fee per dependent, and the visa does not require you to spend a set number of days per year in the Philippines to remain valid, provided your qualifying deposit or converted investment stays in place and your annual PRA fee is current.

Exit and withdrawal rules

The deposit is not meant to be a liquid savings account you dip into while keeping the visa. To withdraw it, you generally need to formally cancel the SRRV with PRA first — surrendering the visa and going through PRA's exit-clearance process, which checks that any annual fees are current and, if you converted the deposit into a condo or lease, addresses the status of that investment. Keeping the SRRV active while withdrawing or reducing the underlying deposit below the qualifying amount is not permitted. If you are only testing the Philippines as a retirement base, factor this illiquidity into your planning before you wire the full deposit.

US tax: what Americans still owe

Moving to the Philippines on an SRRV changes nothing about your US tax obligations: American citizens are taxed on worldwide income no matter where they live or hold residency. What changes is the Philippine side, and it cuts in Americans' favor in one specific way: under the National Internal Revenue Code, an alien individual — resident or not — is taxed by the Philippines only on Philippine-source income, unlike a Filipino citizen, who is taxed on worldwide income. In practice, that means a US-source pension, Social Security benefit, or US investment income generally is not subject to Philippine income tax at all, even once you are living in the Philippines full-time on an SRRV.

That favorable Philippine-side rule does not touch your US return. You will still report worldwide income on Form 1040 every year, and the SRRV deposit itself creates its own US reporting footprint: once your Philippine financial accounts (the SRRV deposit plus any other local bank balances) exceed US$10,000 in aggregate at any point in the year, you must file an FBAR (FinCEN Form 114), and Form 8938 (FATCA) can apply once you cross the applicable threshold for a taxpayer living abroad. If you later convert the deposit into a directly-held condominium, the property itself is not FBAR/8938-reportable — only the cash accounts are — but rental income and a future sale remain US-reportable on Schedule E and Schedule D/Form 8949 respectively, with a Philippine capital-gains tax (generally 6% of the higher of the gross selling price or zonal value on a sale) available as a foreign tax credit against the US tax on the same gain.

Step-by-step: how Americans apply

  1. Confirm your category and deposit. Work out your SRRV Classic tier by age and pension status (or confirm current Smile/Human Touch/Courtesy terms with PRA if one of those fits better).
  2. Gather documents. Passport, police clearance (NBI/FBI-equivalent), medical certificate, proof of pension if claiming the pensioner tier, and marriage/birth certificates for any dependents.
  3. Open the required PRA-accredited bank account and prepare to transfer the qualifying US-dollar deposit once your application is provisionally approved.
  4. File the application with PRA, either in the Philippines or through a PRA-accredited marketing representative/attorney from the US.
  5. Complete the deposit transfer and biometrics, and receive your SRRV ID and special non-immigrant visa.
  6. Decide later whether to convert the deposit into a condo purchase or long-term lease, notifying PRA and keeping the investment at or above the qualifying value.
  7. Set up US tax compliance — flag the FBAR/Form 8938 thresholds for your Philippine deposit and accounts before your first US filing deadline after the move.

If you're not American: what changes

The PRA rules above — the deposit tiers, the condo/lease conversion option, the 40% foreign-ownership cap, and the exit process — are the same for any foreign SRRV applicant regardless of passport. Only the home-country tax wrapper changes: swap the US-specific FBAR/Form 8938/worldwide-taxation sections for your own country's equivalents.

If you are reading this from outside the US, check three things with a tax adviser in your own country: (1) whether your home country taxes your worldwide income (many only tax residents, which can make a genuine Philippine relocation more tax-efficient than it is for a US citizen); (2) whether your home country has its own foreign-account or foreign-asset reporting regime that the SRRV deposit or a converted condo would trigger; and (3) whether a double-taxation treaty exists between your country and the Philippines that affects how any Philippine-source income or gain is taxed back home.

Is the SRRV worth it in 2026?

For an American who genuinely wants to retire in the Philippines — rather than just visit for extended stretches on tourist-visa extensions — the SRRV remains one of the more straightforward retirement residencies available: no property purchase required to qualify, a deposit that can later become a condo instead of sitting idle, indefinite multiple-entry stay, and a Philippine tax system that, unusually, does not tax your foreign-source pension at all. The trade-offs are real: the deposit is illiquid while the visa is active, land ownership is never on the table regardless of visa status, and a couple of the narrower SRRV categories currently lack a clearly published current deposit figure, which is reason enough to get written confirmation from PRA before you commit funds rather than relying on any guide, including this one, for the Smile and Human Touch tiers specifically.

Frequently asked questions

How much deposit do I need for the Philippines SRRV visa?

For SRRV Classic, PRA requires $15,000 (age 50+, with a qualifying pension), $30,000 (age 50+, no pension), $25,000 (age 40–49, with a pension), or $50,000 (age 40–49, no pension). A qualifying pension is at least $800/month single or $1,000/month with dependents. Separate, lower-deposit Courtesy categories exist for former Filipinos and qualifying diplomats/military.

Can I use my SRRV deposit to buy a condo in the Philippines?

Yes. PRA allows the deposit to be converted into an active investment after your visa is issued — typically a condo purchase (within the 40% foreign cap per project) or a long-term lease — provided the investment stays at or above the qualifying value for as long as you hold the visa.

Can Americans own land in the Philippines on an SRRV visa?

No. The SRRV doesn't change the Constitution's foreign land-ownership ban. You can own a condo unit (within the 40% cap), own a building separately from its land, or lease land long-term — but not hold title to land itself.

Do I pay US tax on my SRRV deposit or Philippine pension income?

Yes, worldwide US taxation applies regardless of the SRRV. Separately, the Philippines taxes aliens only on Philippine-source income, so a US-source pension generally isn't Philippine-taxable. Philippine accounts over $10,000 aggregate trigger an FBAR, with Form 8938 potentially applying above its own threshold.

Can I withdraw my SRRV deposit and leave the Philippines?

Yes, but only after formally cancelling the SRRV and completing PRA's exit-clearance process. You cannot withdraw the deposit below the qualifying amount while keeping the visa active.

Does the SRRV allow unlimited stay and multiple entries?

Yes. It's a special non-immigrant visa with indefinite, multiple-entry privileges and no minimum annual stay requirement, as long as the qualifying deposit or investment and your annual PRA fee stay current.
