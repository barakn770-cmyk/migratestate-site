Italian Citizenship by Descent After Decree-Law 36/2025: What Americans Can Still Claim (2026)

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated October 2026  ·  12 min read
Italy's jure sanguinis reform did not abolish citizenship by descent — it narrowed who can still claim it without having already filed. Under Decree-Law 36/2025, converted with amendments by Law 74/2025, a person born abroad who holds another citizenship is now treated as never having been Italian unless a parent or grandparent held Italian citizenship exclusively, or a parent lived in Italy for at least two continuous years after naturalizing and before the child's birth — or unless the applicant is grandfathered by a filing made before 23:59 Rome time on 27 March 2025. Many Italian-American family lines still qualify. Many others, resting on a great-grandparent or an ancestor who naturalized abroad before the next generation was born, no longer do.

For two decades, Italy was the generous outlier among European citizenship regimes: no generational limit on jure sanguinis, so an American whose great-great-grandparent left Calabria or Sicily in 1905 without ever naturalizing could, in principle, still recognize Italian citizenship today. That changed overnight on 28 March 2025, when the Italian government published Decreto-Legge n. 36/2025 in the Gazzetta Ufficiale (no. 73), effective the following day. Consulates worldwide suspended their appointment calendars within days. Parliament then converted the decree, with amendments, into Legge n. 74/2025, published 23 May 2025 (Gazzetta Ufficiale no. 118). The result is now the operative law, and it is considerably more restrictive — but not, as some headlines suggested, a wholesale repeal of citizenship by descent.

What changed, and why

The Italian government's stated justification was volume: a surge of jure sanguinis applications, heavily driven by the United States, Brazil, Argentina and other large diaspora countries, had overwhelmed consular capacity and, in the government's framing, diluted citizenship from people with no genuine connection to Italy. The reform's mechanism is a new Article 3-bis inserted into Law 91/1992 (Italy's main citizenship statute). It does not change who is a citizen by birth in Italy, and it does not touch citizenship acquired by marriage or ordinary naturalization. It is narrowly aimed at one scenario: a person born outside Italy who also holds another country's citizenship at birth. For that person only, the law now says they are treated as if they never acquired Italian citizenship automatically — unless one of a short list of exceptions applies.

The new rule: who still qualifies automatically

Article 3-bis sets out the exceptions precisely, and the exact wording matters more than any informal "two generations" shorthand. A person born abroad with another citizenship is not cut off from automatic Italian citizenship if any of the following is true:

  • The exclusivity test. A parent, adoptive parent, or grandparent (a first- or second-degree ascendant) holds, or held at the time of death, exclusively Italian citizenship — meaning that ascendant never acquired a second nationality (most commonly, never naturalized in the country they emigrated to). This is what, in practice, usually caps a workable chain at the grandparent generation: go back one generation further, to a great-grandparent, and the exception no longer reaches far enough, regardless of whether anyone in between naturalized.
  • The two-year residence test. A parent or adoptive parent lived in Italy for at least two continuous years after acquiring Italian citizenship and before the applicant's birth or adoption. This exception does not depend on the ascendant's citizenship being exclusive — it is a residence-based substitute.
  • Grandfathering. The applicant falls under the transitional protection described below, tied to a filing made before 27 March 2025.

The practical consequence for Italian-American family research: the question is no longer simply "how many generations back was my Italian ancestor," but "did the specific parent or grandparent in my chain ever become a citizen of another country — and if so, before or after my parent was born." A grandparent who emigrated and never naturalized preserves the chain for a grandchild born abroad. A grandparent who naturalized in the US before their own child (the applicant's parent) was born generally breaks it, unless the two-year residence exception or grandfathering rescues the claim. Historic cases also intersect with the old pre-1912 rule under which a father's naturalization automatically naturalized his minor children — a separate line of case law that predates this reform and that a citizenship attorney should check against your specific dates before you conclude a chain is broken or intact.

Grandfathering: protected if you filed by 27 March 2025

The decree protects three categories of people who acted before the cutoff, regardless of how many generations separate them from their Italian ancestor:

Grandfathering categoryWhat had to happen by 23:59 Rome time, 27 March 2025
Administrative filingA complete application was submitted to an Italian consulate or to the mayor (comune) of an Italian municipality
Scheduled appointmentAn appointment date had already been communicated by the consulate by that deadline, even if the appointment itself falls later
Judicial filingA citizenship-recognition claim had been filed in an Italian court

If you already have a filed application, a confirmed consular appointment booked before the cutoff, or an active court case, the pre-reform rules — including the old unlimited-generation chain — continue to apply to your claim. If you are only now looking into your family's eligibility and have done none of these things, you are assessed under the new Article 3-bis exceptions described above, not the old rule, however many generations your Italian ancestry actually spans.

Minors and the transitional declaration

The decree also carved out a narrower protection for minor children. Where a parent who is a citizen by birth filed their own application by the 27 March 2025 cutoff, and their child had not yet turned 18 by 24 May 2025, that child can be recognized through a separate declaration rather than a full application, with a lower administrative fee than an adult's application. The exact filing deadline and documentation for this minors' declaration are fact-specific and have been adjusted since the decree's original text, so confirm the current deadline directly with the consulate handling the parent's case — this is one of the areas where getting the date wrong can forfeit the protection entirely.

Separately, and unrelated to this reform's main thrust, Italy opened a window from 1 July 2025 to 31 December 2027 for people who lost Italian citizenship under the old Law 555/1912 regime — for example, a woman who automatically lost Italian citizenship on marrying a foreign national under rules that predate the 1948 Constitution — to reacquire it by a simple declaration, for a 250 euro administrative fee. If your family history includes an ancestor who lost citizenship that way rather than one who simply emigrated, this reacquisition window may be a more direct route than a descent claim.

Consular recognition vs. the court route

Most claims that still qualify under the new exceptions proceed as an administrative recognition: filed with the Italian consulate covering your place of residence in the US, or with the comune if you are already resident in Italy. The consulate reviews your documented chain of descent against the exclusivity or residence exception and issues (or denies) recognition without a court being involved.

The judicial route — filing a citizenship-recognition claim in an Italian court, typically through counsel in Italy — remains available for two distinct situations. First, it is one of the three ways to lock in grandfathering, if filed by the 27 March 2025 cutoff. Second, it has long been the route for the separate "1948 rule" issue: before 1 January 1948, Italian law did not allow a woman to transmit citizenship to her children, a rule the Italian Constitutional Court and Court of Cassation have since treated as unconstitutional in specific litigated cases, but which has historically required a court — not a consular official — to resolve. How that line of cases interacts with the new Article 3-bis exceptions is still being litigated and is not yet settled by a single clear statute; if your claim runs through a female ancestor before 1948, treat this as a specialist question for Italian citizenship counsel rather than something to resolve from this guide alone.

Documents, apostilles and the process

Whichever route applies, the documentary burden is the same in substance: you must prove an unbroken chain from the Italian ancestor down to you, and — critically, under the new law — prove the citizenship status of the relevant parent or grandparent at the right moment in time. In practice that means assembling:

  • The Italian ancestor's Italian birth certificate (estratto di nascita), obtained from the comune of birth in Italy.
  • Birth, marriage and (where relevant) death certificates for every person in the chain between the ancestor and the applicant, issued by the relevant US state or other country of residence.
  • Naturalization records for the ancestor and any intermediate ascendant — or, just as importantly under the new exclusivity test, official confirmation that an ascendant never naturalized, typically a certified "no record" letter from US Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) or the National Archives.
  • Apostilles (for documents from Hague Convention countries, which includes the United States) or consular legalization on every foreign-issued document, plus certified translation into Italian for documents not already bilingual.
  • Any prior Italian government determination affecting the chain — for example, evidence the ancestor did not lose citizenship through Italian military service abroad or other historical grounds for loss.

Under the amended Article 19-bis, a 2025 addition, courts hearing a citizenship-recognition dispute can no longer accept oaths or witness testimony as proof — only documents — and the burden sits with the claimant to affirmatively show that no ground for non-acquisition or loss of citizenship applies to their chain. That tightens the evidentiary bar precisely where immigrant-era records are often thin, so budget real time and, for Italian-side records, real cost for a professional genealogist or Italian legal correspondent to pull complete comune and state archive records.

Timelines remain the least predictable part of the process. Even before the 2025 reform, US consulates handling Italian citizenship had multi-year appointment backlogs in several jurisdictions — one of the pressures the government cited in justifying the reform. Expect a consular administrative recognition that clears the new exceptions to still take many months to a few years from documentation to appointment to decision, and a court case considerably longer. None of that is new to 2025; what is new is that a growing share of applicants will be told at the outset that their chain simply no longer qualifies, rather than waiting years to find out.

What a passport does — and doesn't — change for US tax

This is the one part of the picture that did not change in 2025, and it is worth saying plainly because it is the question American claimants ask most. Recognizing Italian citizenship by descent is not naturalization in the ordinary sense — you are not acquiring a new nationality through choice and investment, you are having Italy confirm that you were an Italian citizen, by its law, since birth. Either way, the US side is unaffected:

  • The US permits dual and multiple citizenship. Being recognized as an Italian citizen does not cost you your US citizenship, and you do not need to disclose, elect, or choose between the two.
  • Worldwide US taxation continues unchanged. You remain a US citizen taxed on worldwide income under US law regardless of any other citizenship you hold or acquire. An Italian passport changes nothing about your Form 1040 filing obligation.
  • The US exit tax is a separate, voluntary step you have not taken. Under Internal Revenue Code Section 877A, the expatriation tax applies only if a "covered expatriate" formally renounces US citizenship — an entirely different, optional act. Acquiring Italian citizenship by descent does not trigger it, and does not require you to ever consider it.
  • Ordinary cross-border reporting can still apply. If claiming Italian citizenship leads you to open an Italian bank account, inherit Italian property, or receive Italian-source income, the usual FBAR (once foreign accounts exceed $10,000 in aggregate) and Form 8938 (FATCA) reporting rules apply exactly as they would for any American with foreign accounts or assets — the Italian passport itself is incidental to that, not the trigger.

Americans who do eventually want to use an Italian passport as the foundation for a future US renunciation can read our broader comparison of golden visas versus a second passport, which covers the exit tax mechanics in detail — but that is a distinct, later decision, not a consequence of recognizing citizenship you already held by descent.

If you're not American: what changes

The Italian-law analysis above — the exclusivity test, the two-year residence alternative, the 27 March 2025 grandfathering, the minors' declaration, and the documentary and procedural rules — is identical for a claimant of any nationality. Only the home-country tax and citizenship-law wrapper differs.

If you are not a US citizen, the Section 877A exit-tax discussion above does not apply to you at all — most countries tax residents, not citizens, on worldwide income, so simply holding a second passport rarely has any home-country tax consequence by itself. What is worth checking in your own country: (1) whether it permits dual citizenship at all (a small number of countries still require renouncing your original nationality to naturalize elsewhere, though recognizing citizenship you already held by Italian law is a different situation again and worth confirming specifically); (2) whether your country taxes worldwide income and would treat Italian-source income or assets as reportable; and (3) whether a double-taxation treaty between your country and Italy affects how any Italian income is taxed. None of these change the Italian citizenship analysis itself — they only affect what you do with the passport once you have it.

Is it still worth pursuing in 2026?

For the American whose parent or grandparent emigrated from Italy and never naturalized elsewhere, or whose family can document two continuous years of a parent's Italian residence before the applicant's birth, the 2025 reform changes little beyond the paperwork: the claim is narrower in theory but was always going to succeed on those facts. For the American whose connection runs through a great-grandparent, or through a grandparent who naturalized before their own child was born, the honest 2026 answer is that an ordinary descent claim is very likely closed — unless an unused grandfathering filing, a pre-1948 maternal-line issue, or the separate 1912-loss reacquisition window applies to the specific facts. Given how fact-sensitive the exceptions are, and how unforgiving Article 19-bis's new evidentiary rule is on thin immigrant-era records, this is one of the few areas on this site where we'd actively discourage spending money on document-gathering before a citizenship attorney has reviewed your exact dates and confirmed, in writing, that a path still exists.

Frequently asked questions

Did Italy limit jure sanguinis citizenship to two generations?

Not exactly, though it is often described that way. Decree-Law 36/2025, as converted by Law 74/2025, says a person born abroad who holds another citizenship is treated as never having been Italian unless a parent or grandparent held Italian citizenship exclusively at the relevant time, or a parent lived in Italy for at least two continuous years before the applicant's birth. In practice this usually stops an unbroken chain at the grandparent, but the operative test is exclusivity of citizenship, not simply being a grandchild.

Am I grandfathered under the old rules?

Only if, by 23:59 Rome time on 27 March 2025, you had filed a complete application with a consulate or comune, had a consular appointment already communicated by that date, or had filed a court claim. The decree was published the next day, 28 March 2025, so the deadline predates the public announcement for most applicants.

What if my great-grandparent, not my grandparent, was the Italian-born ancestor?

You are very likely outside the new exceptions unless grandfathering or the two-year residence alternative applies. The exclusivity exception only reaches a parent or grandparent, not a great-grandparent, so a claim resting on an ancestor two steps further back no longer qualifies for administrative recognition going forward.

Do I apply at a consulate or go to court?

Most surviving claims go through the consulate (or an Italian comune) as an administrative recognition. Court claims remain the route for grandfathered judicial filings and for the separate pre-1948 maternal-line issue. Confirm which route fits your facts with an Italian citizenship attorney before filing.

I was a minor when the law changed — does anything protect me?

Decree-Law 36/2025 added a transitional declaration for minors not yet 18 on 24 May 2025, where the citizen-by-birth parent's own application was filed by 27 March 2025. The declaration has its own deadline and a lower fee than an adult application — confirm the current deadline with your consulate.

Does becoming an Italian citizen change my US taxes?

No. The US taxes citizens on worldwide income regardless of any other citizenship, and the US permits dual citizenship, so Italian recognition does not touch your US filing obligations. The exit tax under Section 877A only applies if you later renounce US citizenship — a separate, voluntary step. Opening Italian accounts or inheriting Italian property can still trigger ordinary FBAR and FATCA reporting.
