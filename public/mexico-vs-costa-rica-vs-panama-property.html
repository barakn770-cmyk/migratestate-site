Mexico vs Costa Rica vs Panama for Americans (2026): Property, Residency & Tax Compared

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated October 2026  ·  11 min read

All three let Americans own real property with no residency required, but the similarities end there. Mexico is open everywhere but splits into direct title inland and a fideicomiso bank trust along the coasts and borders where most buyers actually want to be. Costa Rica gives fee-simple title almost everywhere except the coastal Maritime Zone. Panama is the most frictionless of the three — fee-simple title, the lowest typical closing costs, and the US dollar as legal tender — with its own narrow 10-kilometer border restriction. The right country depends on whether you're optimizing for proximity, rule of law, or transaction simplicity.

The 2026 snapshot

Mexico, Costa Rica, and Panama are the three most-searched Latin American destinations for American buyers, and for good reason: all three let a US citizen own real property with the same legal rights as a local, none of them requires a visa or residency to close a deal, and all three sit within a few hours' flight of most of the continental United States. But "foreigner-friendly" means something different in each country, and the differences show up exactly where it costs the most to get wrong — title, taxes, and the fine print of residency.

Mexico's catch is geographic: a constitutional restriction on direct foreign title within 50 km of the coast or 100 km of a land border, worked around for decades through the fideicomiso bank-trust structure. Costa Rica's catch is narrower — a public-interest strip along the coastline called the Maritime Zone — but it applies to some of the most desirable beachfront in the country. Panama's catch is the narrowest of the three: a 10-kilometer no-foreign-ownership band along its land borders with Costa Rica and Colombia, plus a long-running problem with untitled "Rights of Possession" land that has nothing to do with nationality and trips up foreign and local buyers alike.

Foreign ownership rules compared

Mexico. Under the Mexican Constitution, foreigners cannot hold direct title to residential land inside the restricted zone — within 50 km of the coastline or 100 km of a land border. Since most of what Americans want (Riviera Maya, Puerto Vallarta, Los Cabos) sits inside that zone, the practical answer is the fideicomiso: a Mexican bank holds legal title as trustee while the foreign buyer, as beneficiary, keeps every meaningful ownership right — to live in it, rent it, renovate it, sell it, and pass it on. Outside the zone (Mexico City, San Miguel de Allende, Mérida), you simply take direct title with no trust at all. See our full fideicomiso guide and guide to buying property in Mexico.

Costa Rica. Away from the coast, foreigners buy with the same fee-simple title as a citizen — no trust, no special structure, no restriction. The one real carve-out is the Maritime Zone, the strip of coastline regulated separately from ordinary private property, where development and foreign participation are restricted and most use runs through government concessions rather than outright ownership. Buyers focused on inland or already-titled coastal properties avoid the issue entirely; see the guide to buying property in Costa Rica.

Panama. Ownership is open nationwide with two narrow exceptions. The 10-kilometer border rule bars foreigners — and even Panamanian companies with any foreign capital — from owning land within 10 km of the Costa Rica or Colombia frontier, affecting parts of Chiriquí, Bocas del Toro, and Darién; there is no corporate workaround. Separately, swaths of rural and island land (notably in Bocas del Toro) are sold as untitled "Rights of Possession" rather than registered title — a trap for any buyer, foreign or Panamanian, not a nationality-based restriction. Elsewhere, including Panama City condos and most resort areas, foreigners buy on identical terms to citizens. See the guide to buying property in Panama.

Closing costs & ongoing property tax

Panama is the cheapest to transact in, Mexico the most expensive — and in Mexico's case, the cost depends entirely on whether the fideicomiso applies. Costa Rica sits in between, with a smaller spread because it has no equivalent trust structure to add a second fee layer.

Residency routes compared

None of the three countries hands out residency for simply owning property, but a purchase can anchor an application in two of them, and the routes differ sharply in speed and structure.

Mexico runs its non-work Temporary Resident visa on an economic-solvency test, in UMA (Unidad de Medida y Actualización) terms: roughly 680 UMA-days of monthly income (about $4,650), 11,400 UMA-days of savings or investments (about $78,000), or Mexican real estate worth 91,710 UMA-days (about $628,000) — any one of the three qualifies, with the property route the rarest in practice. See the full Mexico Temporary Resident Visa guide for the consulate-to-canje process and the path to permanent residency.

Costa Rica offers three non-work categories built for exactly this kind of buyer: Pensionado (a guaranteed US$1,000/month lifetime pension), Rentista (US$2,500/month in verifiable income, or a US$60,000 two-year bank deposit), and Inversionista (a qualifying investment, including real estate — though the live threshold is itself a moving target in 2026, discussed below). All three convert to permanent residency after three years and require mandatory enrollment in the CCSS public health system. See the Costa Rica residency guide.

Panama has two standard non-work routes. Pensionado needs a guaranteed lifetime pension of US$1,000/month (US Social Security qualifies, and the bar drops to US$750/month if paired with titled Panamanian real estate worth more than $100,000) — and uniquely among the three, it grants permanent residency immediately, with no provisional stage. The Friendly Nations Visa, open to US citizens, instead runs on an economic tie: a titled property worth US$200,000 or more, a US$200,000 three-year fixed bank deposit, or a qualifying job offer, with a two-year provisional-to-permanent timeline. See the Panama Pensionado guide and Friendly Nations Visa guide.

Income tax: worldwide vs territorial

Here the three countries split cleanly into two camps. Costa Rica and Panama both run territorial income-tax systems: only income sourced within the country is taxed, so foreign-source income — a US pension, US rental income, US investment gains — generally falls outside the local tax net even once you become a local tax resident. Mexico instead taxes tax residents on worldwide income, with non-residents taxed only on Mexican-source income; under Article 9 of the Código Fiscal de la Federación, you become a Mexican tax resident once you establish your casa habitación (home) there, or — if you keep homes in more than one country — once Mexico becomes your centro de intereses vitales (more than 50% of your income Mexican-sourced, or your main professional activity centered there). Mexican law sets no 183-day threshold, unlike the day-count tests used elsewhere.

For most American buyers in this comparison, the practical difference is smaller than the headline suggests. A vacation-home owner who remains a US tax resident and spends limited time locally is unlikely to trip any of the three countries' residency tests in the first place. The distinction matters most for someone who actually relocates and becomes a local tax resident with substantial US-source income — a retiree living on a pension, for instance, keeps far more of it in Costa Rica or Panama than as a Mexican tax resident. What never changes, in all three countries, is the US side: the IRS taxes its citizens on worldwide income regardless of where they live, so rental income and a future sale remain reportable wherever the property sits, with the foreign tax credit relieving most double taxation.

Healthcare & currency

Costa Rica's CCSS (Caja Costarricense de Seguro Social) is a mandatory, genuinely functional public health system that every Pensionado, Rentista, and Inversionista resident must join, typically supplemented with private insurance for faster specialist access. Panama's public CSS system exists but most American expats and Pensionado-visa holders rely primarily on private insurance and Panama City's well-regarded private hospitals. Mexico offers both a public system (IMSS, generally tied to formal residency and employment) and an extensive, comparatively affordable private-hospital network, especially in cities and major expat hubs.

Currency is where Panama stands apart: the US dollar is legal tender there, circulating alongside the Panamanian balboa, which is pegged 1:1 to the dollar and rarely appears in physical cash form. That removes day-to-day exchange-rate risk entirely — though a US buyer financing or holding other assets there should still watch Panama-specific economic and banking-sector risk, which is separate from currency risk. Mexico and Costa Rica both price and often settle property in their own floating currencies (the peso and the colón, respectively), though US dollars are frequently accepted by private agreement in both, especially in resort markets — carrying ordinary exchange-rate exposure on anything held or earned locally.

The detail most blogs miss

Generic comparisons tend to rank these three purely on sticker price — "Panama is cheapest, Mexico is priciest" — and stop there. What actually matters is that the real distinguishing issue in each country sits in a different place entirely. In Mexico, the fideicomiso isn't really a cost problem; it is, by design, a secure and well-tested 40-year-old legal instrument, and the IRS has confirmed (Revenue Ruling 2013-14) that a standard residential fideicomiso is not a foreign trust for US tax purposes, so the onerous Forms 3520/3520-A generally don't apply — a detail many "move to Mexico" blogs get backwards. In Costa Rica, the headline risk isn't the Maritime Zone at all for most buyers (who simply avoid it); it's the ubiquitous Costa Rican S.A. corporation used to hold title, which can create unexpected US reporting obligations (Form 5471, potential CFC/GILTI exposure) that have nothing to do with the property itself. In Panama, the real trap isn't the 10-kilometer border rule — it's "Rights of Possession" land sold without registered title, concentrated in exactly the beach and island markets foreign buyers find most tempting.

One number in this comparison is genuinely unsettled rather than simply nuanced: Costa Rica's Inversionista investment threshold. Ley 9996 temporarily reduced it to US$150,000, but whether that reduction has reverted to the standard US$200,000 following the law's 14 July 2026 window could not be confirmed against an official source at the time of writing — some DGME-published checklists reportedly still showed the lower figure in early September 2026. Anyone planning to use real estate to qualify for Costa Rican residency should confirm the live figure directly with DGME or an immigration attorney before relying on either number.

How to choose

If proximity to the US, the lowest holding costs, and dollar-friendly pricing matter most, Mexico wins — provided you make peace with the fideicomiso on coastal property, which is routine rather than risky once you understand it. If you want the strongest rule-of-law reputation in the region, a military-free stable democracy, and a clean three-year route to permanent residency through an income or pension test, Costa Rica fits best — just budget for the Maritime Zone and the corporate-structuring nuance if you buy coastal. If you want the lowest transaction friction, the fewest moving legal parts, a dollar-denominated economy, and — if you qualify for Pensionado — the single fastest permanent-residency approval of any country in this comparison, Panama is the strongest fit.

Budget and lifestyle narrow it further. Retirees with a guaranteed pension of $1,000/month or more should look hardest at Panama's Pensionado route, which beats the equivalent programs in both other countries on speed alone. Buyers chasing the lowest all-in cost on a straightforward inland purchase should compare Panama and Costa Rica directly, since Mexico's advantage evaporates once a coastal fideicomiso and its annual fee enter the picture. And anyone for whom US tax reporting simplicity matters most should note that none of the three is simpler than the others on that front — FBAR and Form 8938 follow the money, not the country.

Step-by-step: how to buy

  1. Confirm the zone or structure. In Mexico, check whether the property sits in the restricted zone (fideicomiso required) or not. In Costa Rica, confirm it sits outside the Maritime Zone or understand the concession terms if not. In Panama, confirm it is titled (not Rights of Possession) and outside the 10 km border band.
  2. Engage independent local counsel. Retain an attorney separate from the seller's notary or broker in all three countries, plus a US cross-border CPA before wiring funds.
  3. Get your local tax ID and bank account. Mexico (RFC), Costa Rica (cédula de residente or NITE for non-residents), and Panama (RUC, if using a corporate holding structure) each require their own identifier.
  4. Run title due diligence. Confirm registered title at the relevant public registry in each country — the Registro Público in Mexico and Panama, the Registro Nacional in Costa Rica — and check for liens.
  5. Set up any required structure. Establish the fideicomiso (Mexico, if applicable) or decide whether to hold title personally or through a corporation/foundation (Costa Rica and Panama both commonly use one, each with its own US reporting consequences).
  6. Transfer funds compliantly. Wire through traceable channels and keep documentation proving the source and path of funds for all three jurisdictions' anti-money-laundering rules.
  7. Close and register. Sign before the relevant notary (all three use a civil-law notary system) and confirm registration at the public registry before considering the deal final.
  8. Plan the US tax side before, not after. Decide how the purchase will be reported — FBAR/Form 8938 for any foreign account, and Form 5471 or 3520 exposure if a foreign entity is involved — with your CPA before you close, not at filing season.

Is it worth it in 2026?

All three remain genuinely strong options for the right buyer, and the differences between them are real rather than cosmetic. Mexico's proximity and pricing are hard to beat for a true second-home buyer who can accept the fideicomiso as routine rather than exotic. Costa Rica's stability and straightforward residency math suit someone planning an eventual full relocation more than a purely passive investment. Panama's dollar economy, lighter transaction friction, and the Pensionado program's immediate-permanent-residency feature make it the standout for retirees with a qualifying pension and for anyone who simply wants the fewest moving legal parts.

What all three share is the same underlying lesson: the headline "foreigner-friendly" pitch is true in every case, but the one detail that actually costs people money or years is never the one the marketing leads with. Treat each purchase as a serious, professionally-advised transaction specific to that country's actual legal mechanics — not a generic "buy abroad" checklist — and any of the three can deliver exactly what it promises.

Frequently asked questions

Which country is easiest for an American to buy property in: Mexico, Costa Rica, or Panama?

Panama is the simplest on paper — fee-simple title, no residency needed, and the fewest ownership restrictions outside the 10-kilometer border zone. Costa Rica is nearly as open, with the Maritime Zone as its one real carve-out. Mexico is open everywhere too, but coastal and border property — most of what Americans actually want — requires a fideicomiso bank trust rather than direct title.

Which country has the lowest closing costs?

Panama, typically 2%–5% of the purchase price. Costa Rica runs about 4%–6%. Mexico is the highest and the most situational: roughly 5%–8% outside the restricted zone with direct title, rising to 7%–10% inside it once a fideicomiso and its setup fee are added.

Does buying property give me residency in any of these three countries?

Not automatically in any of them, but it can support a residency application in two. In Panama, a titled property worth US$200,000 or more is one of the two standard Friendly Nations Visa routes. In Costa Rica, a qualifying real-estate purchase can anchor an Inversionista application. Mexico's temporary-resident solvency test can also be met with Mexican real estate worth roughly US$628,000, though most applicants qualify on income or savings instead. None of the three grants residency from ownership alone — you still have to file the separate immigration application.

Which of the three taxes Americans the most lightly?

Costa Rica and Panama both run territorial income-tax systems, so neither taxes foreign-source income even once you become a local tax resident — useful if you have US or other foreign income, though it does nothing for your IRS bill. Mexico taxes tax residents on worldwide income — residency there follows where you keep your casa habitación or centro de intereses vitales (Art. 9, Código Fiscal de la Federación), not a day-count. For a US citizen who remains a US tax resident and only owns a vacation home, the practical difference is smaller than it looks, because the IRS taxes worldwide income in all three cases regardless of the local system.

Which country has the fastest path to permanent residency?

Panama's Pensionado visa is the fastest by far — it grants permanent residency immediately on approval, with no provisional stage, for anyone with a qualifying lifetime pension of US$1,000/month (US Social Security counts). Costa Rica's three non-work categories all convert to permanent residency after three years. Panama's own Friendly Nations Visa and Mexico's temporary-resident routes both start with a provisional or temporary card and convert later.

Can I use US dollars directly in these countries?

Panama is the simplest — the US dollar is legal tender there alongside the balboa, which is pegged 1:1 and barely circulates in cash form, so there is no currency-conversion risk on day-to-day spending. Costa Rica and Mexico both use their own floating currencies, the colón and the peso, so property is usually priced and settled in local currency or sometimes US dollars by private agreement, and either way you carry exchange-rate exposure on anything held locally.

What is the biggest US tax trap across all three?

Foreign financial account reporting. A Mexican fideicomiso bank account, a Costa Rican Rentista deposit, or a Panamanian fixed deposit used for residency are all foreign financial accounts, and once your aggregate foreign accounts cross US$10,000 at any point in the year you must file an FBAR (FinCEN Form 114), with Form 8938 potentially layering on top. None of this is unique to one country — it follows from being a US citizen with money abroad, whichever of the three you choose.

Which country should I actually choose?

It depends on what you are optimizing for. Choose Mexico for proximity to the US and the lowest holding costs once you accept the fideicomiso if you are coastal. Choose Costa Rica for the strongest rule-of-law reputation in the region and a straightforward three-year path to permanent residency. Choose Panama for the lowest transaction friction, the US-dollar economy, and — if you qualify for Pensionado — the fastest permanent-residency approval of the three.
