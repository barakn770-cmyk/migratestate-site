US Taxes on Foreign Property: The Complete Guide for American Owners (2026)

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated September 2026  ·  14 min read
The United States taxes its citizens and green-card holders on everything, everywhere — including a house in Lisbon, a condo in Tulum or a flat in Athens. Buying the property triggers no US tax, but it starts a reporting trail: an FBAR once your foreign accounts top $10,000, possibly Form 8938, Schedule E if you rent it (with a mandatory 30-year depreciation), a foreign tax credit on Form 1116 for income tax paid abroad, and capital gains tax in US dollars when you sell — softened by the Section 121 exclusion if it was your home. The property itself is never reported; the money around it always is. This page maps the whole lifecycle and links to the detailed guide on each step.

The one rule that drives everything

Most countries tax you because you live there. The United States taxes you because you are American. A US citizen or green-card holder is taxed on worldwide income regardless of where they live and where the asset sits — and that single principle explains every form on this page. Portugal will tax the rent from your Algarve apartment because the apartment is in Portugal; the IRS will tax the same rent because you are a US person. The system that stops that becoming double taxation is the foreign tax credit, and the system that makes sure the IRS knows the income exists is a stack of information returns with penalties that are often larger than the tax.

Two consequences follow. First, there is almost never a US tax on buying — the IRS taxes income, gain and transfers, not ownership. Second, the reporting obligations are triggered by accounts and structures, not by the bricks. Directly owned foreign real estate does not appear on any US form until it earns or is sold. The bank account you opened to pay the notary does. Most of the expensive mistakes American owners make are reporting mistakes, not tax mistakes, and they are made in the first year, at closing.

Buying: no tax, but the reporting starts

Wiring $400,000 from your US brokerage to a Spanish notary's account is not a taxable event, and no form goes to the IRS for the transfer itself. What changes is that you now probably have a foreign financial account, and foreign accounts are the single most policed item in the US expat tax system.

The FBAR: the $10,000 line you will cross on closing day

FinCEN Form 114, universally called the FBAR, is due once the combined maximum balances of all your foreign accounts exceed $10,000 at any single moment in the year. Not the year-end balance — the peak. If your purchase funds sat in a Portuguese account for forty-eight hours before completion, you crossed the line. The form is filed electronically with FinCEN, not with your tax return, by April 15 with an automatic extension to October 15. The property is not listed; the accounts are. A 2023 Supreme Court decision capped the non-willful penalty per annual report rather than per account, but willful failures remain severe. Our FBAR guide for property owners walks through which accounts count — including escrow, joint and property-manager accounts — and our foreign bank account guide covers why some banks refuse Americans and what to do about it.

Form 8938: the wrapper, not the house

Form 8938 (FATCA) is filed with your Form 1040 and reports "specified foreign financial assets" once they exceed a threshold that depends on where you live and how you file — from $50,000 at year end for a single filer in the US to $400,000 for a married couple living abroad. Real estate you own in your own name is not a specified asset. But an interest in a foreign company, partnership or trust that owns the real estate is, at fair market value. The same foreign bank account can appear on both the FBAR and Form 8938; filing one never satisfies the other. Details, thresholds and the entity trap are in the Form 8938 guide.

Gifts and family money: Form 3520

If part of the purchase price came as a gift or inheritance from a non-US person — a parent abroad, say — and the total from that person exceeded $100,000 in the year, you file Form 3520. There is no tax, but the penalty for not filing is a percentage of the gift. Also note how moving your own money abroad interacts with anti-money-laundering rules, covered in transferring money abroad for a property purchase.

Closing costs are not lost — they become basis

Transfer tax, notary fees, registration and legal fees are not deductible in the year you pay them. They are capitalised into your cost basis, which reduces the taxable gain when you eventually sell. Keep every receipt and its dollar value on the day paid. The country-by-country numbers are in closing costs when buying abroad.

Holding: property taxes, currency and the mortgage trap

Owning a foreign home that you use yourself produces no US taxable income. Three things still matter.

Foreign property taxes are not an itemized deduction. Since the 2017 tax reform, real property taxes paid to a foreign jurisdiction cannot be deducted on Schedule A. They remain deductible against rental income on Schedule E if the property is let. What the taxes actually cost in each country is in annual property taxes abroad.

Every number lives in dollars. Your basis is the dollar value of what you paid on the day you paid it. Rent is translated at the rate when received; expenses when paid. The IRS publishes yearly average rates, and a consistent method matters more than which rate you choose. Over a decade, exchange-rate movement alone can create — or erase — a six-figure taxable gain.

A foreign-currency mortgage is a second taxable asset. Under IRC Section 988, repaying a euro or peso loan with fewer dollars than you borrowed produces ordinary income in the year of payoff, sale or refinance. No cash changes hands, the Section 121 home-sale exclusion does not cover it, and a loss on the same movement is generally not deductible for a personal residence. The mechanics and a worked example are in foreign mortgage currency gains; the lending side is in foreign mortgages for US citizens.

Renting: Schedule E, depreciation and the credit

The moment the property earns rent, it becomes a US business asset. Four rules govern the return.

Schedule E, in dollars, after expenses

Foreign rent is reported on Schedule E exactly like a US rental: gross rent less mortgage interest, management, repairs, insurance, local property tax and depreciation. The foreign rental income guide covers the full expense list and the personal-use rules that apply if you also stay there.

Depreciation is mandatory and slower abroad

Property used predominantly outside the United States must use the Alternative Depreciation System: 30 years straight-line for a residential rental placed in service after 2017 (40 years before), 40 years for commercial — never the 27.5 years a US rental gets, and never bonus depreciation. Only the building is depreciated, so you need a defensible land/building split. The IRS reduces your basis by the depreciation "allowed or allowable" whether or not you claimed it, and taxes that amount at up to 25% on sale — so skipping it costs you twice. The 30-year ADS guide explains the Form 3115 fix if you have been using 27.5 years.

The foreign tax credit — for income tax only

Income tax you pay the other country on the rental profit is creditable dollar-for-dollar on Form 1116, in the passive category basket. Annual property tax, transfer tax and tourist levies are not income taxes and are not creditable (they are deductible expenses instead). If the foreign rate is higher than your US rate on the same income, the excess carries back one year and forward ten. The foreign tax credit guide covers the basket rules and the de minimis election.

The FEIE trap

Americans living abroad often assume the foreign earned income exclusion shelters everything foreign. It shelters earned income — wages and self-employment — up to $132,900 in 2026. Rent is passive and gets no exclusion at all; the credit is the only relief. Short-term letting adds a second layer of local licensing and, in some countries, outright bans — see short-term rental rules abroad.

Selling: capital gains in dollars, Section 121 and 1031

A sale abroad is a US taxable event even if the country of location has already taxed it. The gain is proceeds in dollars (at the rate on the sale date) minus basis in dollars (at the rate on the purchase date, plus capitalised costs, minus depreciation). Held over a year, the gain is taxed at 0%, 15% or 20% depending on your bracket, plus the 3.8% net investment income tax above the NIIT thresholds. The full computation, with the currency effect, is in capital gains tax on property abroad.

Section 121 works abroad

If the property was your principal residence for at least two of the five years before sale, you can exclude $250,000 of gain — $500,000 married filing jointly — exactly as for a US home. The trap: excluding the gain also strands the foreign tax credit on the excluded portion, so paying foreign tax and then claiming Section 121 can leave you worse off than the arithmetic suggests. The ownership and use tests, and when to elect out, are in the Section 121 foreign home guide.

Which form: 8949 or 4797

A never-rented home is reported on Form 8949 with totals carried to Schedule D. A property that was rented at any point goes on Form 4797 first, where depreciation recapture is calculated, before the remainder flows to Schedule D. Choosing the wrong form is the most common mechanical error on a foreign sale; the Form 8949 guide shows both routes line by line.

The credit rarely covers the whole foreign bill

Foreign income tax on the sale is creditable — but on a long-term gain the credit is limited by a rate-differential adjustment to roughly the US tax at 15–20%. Where the foreign country taxes gains at 28% or more, the excess strands as carryover that a personal owner may never use. Modelling this before you sign is worth more than any other planning step on this page.

No 1031 across the border

IRC Section 1031(h) says US real property and foreign real property are not like-kind. You cannot defer gain by rolling a Texas rental into a Portuguese one, or the reverse. A foreign-for-foreign exchange can qualify if you run it through a qualified intermediary within the 45-day identification and 180-day closing windows — but the foreign country will still tax the sale now, so the deferral is one-sided. Details in 1031 exchanges and foreign property. Country-specific selling guides cover the local side for Mexico, Portugal and Greece.

Passing it on: estate tax and forced heirship

Three separate systems decide what happens to a foreign property at death. US estate tax reaches a citizen's worldwide assets, but the exemption is $15 million per person in 2026, so the federal tax bites very few estates; above it the rate is 40%. Local inheritance or succession tax usually applies where the property sits, often at rates that depend on the heir's relationship to you, and only a handful of countries have an estate-tax treaty with the US that coordinates the two. Forced heirship in civil-law countries — Spain, France, Italy, Portugal and much of Latin America — can dictate that children receive a fixed share regardless of your will; within the EU a US citizen can often elect their home-state law under the EU Succession Regulation, which changes who inherits but not the tax. Heirs receiving a large foreign bequest file Form 3520 (no tax, but a penalty if missed), and inherited property takes a stepped-up basis to fair market value at death for US purposes. The inheritance guide covers the country-by-country rates and the Brussels IV election.

Own name, LLC or trust?

For a single foreign home, most Americans should hold it personally. Every wrapper — a local company, a US LLC, a foreign trust — adds a US information return with a $10,000-per-form, per-year starting penalty: Form 8858 for a foreign disregarded entity, Form 5471 for a foreign corporation, Form 8865 for a foreign partnership, Forms 3520 and 3520-A for a foreign trust. Structures earn their cost for rental portfolios, multi-investor deals, or countries whose law forces a vehicle on you. The LLC vs personal name guide weighs each option. One structure Americans meet constantly is Mexico's fideicomiso, the bank trust required for coastal and border property: the IRS ruled in Revenue Ruling 2013-14 that a standard fideicomiso is not a foreign trust, so Forms 3520 and 3520-A are normally not required — a point most blogs still get wrong, explained in US taxes on Mexican property.

If you have missed filings

Owners who discover, years in, that they never filed an FBAR or reported foreign rent are common — and the IRS has formal routes for non-willful catch-up that substantially reduce or eliminate penalties, provided you come forward before the IRS contacts you. The right route depends on whether you live in or outside the US and on whether tax was actually underpaid. Do not simply start filing correctly from this year and hope; the earlier years are visible once the later ones exist. This is a conversation for a cross-border tax professional, not a form you download.

The annual owner's checklist

  1. January: pull the peak balance of every foreign account for the prior year. Over $10,000 combined? FBAR by April 15 (extension to October 15).
  2. Gather local tax paid: separate income tax (creditable on Form 1116) from property, transfer and tourist taxes (deductible on Schedule E, not creditable).
  3. Translate everything to dollars with a consistent method; keep the rate source with your records.
  4. If rented: Schedule E with 30-year ADS depreciation on the building portion; Form 1116 for the foreign income tax.
  5. If held through an entity or trust: confirm which of Forms 8858, 5471, 8865, 3520 or 3520-A applies — every year, not only the first.
  6. If you refinanced or paid off a foreign-currency mortgage: compute the Section 988 result.
  7. If you sold: Form 8949 or 4797, Section 121 test, foreign tax credit with the rate-differential adjustment, and the currency effect on basis.
  8. If you gave or received large sums cross-border: Form 3520 for gifts from foreign persons over $100,000.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to report property I own abroad to the IRS?

Not the property itself, if you hold it in your own name. There is no IRS form for directly owned foreign real estate, and it is not a specified foreign financial asset for Form 8938. What you report is the money around it: foreign bank accounts on the FBAR once they exceed $10,000 in aggregate, possibly Form 8938, rental income on Schedule E, and the sale on Form 8949 or Form 4797. Property held through a foreign company, partnership or trust is different — the interest in the entity is reportable.

Is rental income from a foreign property taxable in the US?

Yes. US citizens and green-card holders are taxed on worldwide income, so foreign rent is reported on Schedule E in US dollars, after expenses and a mandatory 30-year (ADS) depreciation. Income tax paid to the other country on the same rent is usually recoverable as a foreign tax credit on Form 1116. The foreign earned income exclusion does not apply — rent is passive income.

Do I pay US capital gains tax when I sell a house abroad?

Generally yes. The gain is computed in US dollars and taxed at 0%, 15% or 20% if held over a year, plus the 3.8% net investment income tax for higher earners. A foreign principal residence can still use the Section 121 exclusion of $250,000 ($500,000 married filing jointly) if you owned and lived in it for two of the last five years, and foreign income tax on the sale is creditable — though a rate-differential adjustment often leaves a residual US bill.

Can I do a 1031 exchange with foreign property?

Only foreign for foreign. Under IRC Section 1031(h), real property in the United States and real property outside it are not like-kind, so you cannot exchange a US rental for one abroad or vice versa. Exchanging one overseas investment property for another can qualify if the 45-day and 180-day rules are met through a qualified intermediary.

Does a foreign mortgage create US tax?

It can. A mortgage denominated in a foreign currency is a separate transaction under IRC Section 988. If the dollar strengthens between the day you borrow and the day you repay, the dollars saved in extinguishing the debt are ordinary income in the year of payoff, sale or refinance — even though no cash changes hands. A loss on the same movement is generally not deductible for a personal residence.

Is there US estate tax on property I own abroad?

US estate tax reaches the worldwide assets of a US citizen, but the exemption is $15 million per person in 2026, so most estates owe nothing federally. The country where the property sits usually applies its own inheritance or succession tax, and civil-law countries may impose forced-heirship rules on who inherits, which a US will alone may not override.
