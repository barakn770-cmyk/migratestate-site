Portugal vs Spain for Americans (2026): Buying Property, Residency Visas and Tax Compared

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated October 2026  ·  12 min read

For an American in 2026 the honest summary is that Portugal is the cheaper country to hold property in, and Spain can be the cheaper country to buy into — and that the decision turns on tax, not on price. Portugal now charges a non-resident buyer a flat 7.5% IMT on a home, with no exemptions, unless you become resident within two years. Spain taxes a US owner's rental income at 24% of gross rent with no deduction for expenses, charges wealth tax on Spanish assets above €700,000, and has a real inheritance tax. Portugal taxes rental income at 25% after Category F deductions, has no general wealth tax, and exempts spouses and children from tax on inheritance. The residency routes are closely matched; the citizenship timelines are now identical at ten years.

The 2026 snapshot

Portugal and Spain are the two countries Americans shortlist most often, and they are usually compared on the wrong axis. The property markets are genuinely similar — open to foreign buyers, no nationality restriction, freehold title, a functioning land registry, and prices that still look reasonable against coastal California or the Northeast. The differences that actually determine your outcome sit in the tax code and in the visa rules, and in 2026 several of them moved.

Three changes matter most. Spain abolished its Golden Visa in April 2025, so the investment route into Spanish residency is simply gone. Portugal's Golden Visa survives but has had no real estate route since 2023, so buying a home there earns you no immigration status either. And Portugal's famous five-year path to citizenship was abolished in May 2026, putting it level with Spain at ten years. The result is that neither country now offers residency or a passport in exchange for a property purchase: in both, buying and immigrating are two separate projects.

What it costs to buy

Portugal charges IMT — Imposto Municipal sobre as Transmissões Onerosas de Imóveis — and in 2026 it added a rule that changes the arithmetic for exactly the reader of this article. Under article 17 of the IMT code, as amended by Decreto-Lei n.º 97/2026 with effect from 20 May 2026, the rate "is always 7.5% on the acquisition of an urban building or autonomous fraction intended exclusively for habitation, with no exemption or reduction applying, whenever the acquirer is non-resident". An American buying a Portuguese home while living in the United States therefore pays a flat 7.5% — not the progressive scale, and not the lower bands at the bottom of it.

That flat charge is reversible, and the two escape routes are worth knowing before you sign. It does not stick if you become Portuguese tax resident within two years of the acquisition, or if you let the property as affordable housing within six months and keep it let for at least 36 months during the first five years. In either case the tax authority cancels the difference between the 7.5% paid and the ordinary rates on written request, filed within six months of becoming resident or of signing the lease. If you are buying now with a genuine intention to move within two years, that is real money — on a €500,000 purchase the gap between 7.5% and the ordinary scale runs to five figures — and it is worth structuring the timeline around.

For buyers who will become resident, the ordinary scale in the same article applies: for a property that is not your own permanent home the marginal bands run 1% on the first €106,346, 2% to €145,470, 5% to €198,347, 7% to €330,539 and 8% to €633,931, with a single flat rate of 6% between €633,931 and €1,150,853 and 7.5% above that; the own-and-permanent-home scale starts at 0% instead of 1%. A 10% flat rate with no exemptions applies where the buyer is resident in a jurisdiction on Portugal's privileged-tax-regime list — the United States is not on it. On top of IMT, whichever rate applies, sits stamp duty at 0.8% of the same value, plus notary and registration fees and your lawyer.

Spain works the opposite way round. The transfer tax on a resale home — ITP, Impuesto sobre Transmisiones Patrimoniales — is a competence of the autonomous communities, so the rate is a function of where you buy rather than how much you spend. Where a region has not set its own rate the national default of 6% applies, but most regions have legislated their own, and the spread between the cheapest and the most expensive is wide enough to matter more than any negotiation you will do on price. Buying new-build from a developer takes you out of ITP altogether and into VAT plus stamp duty instead. The practical consequence for a buyer comparing a €400,000 flat in two countries is that the Portuguese bill is predictable from a published national table, while the Spanish bill cannot be known until you have chosen the region — so get the exact figure from your region's tax agency before you make an offer, not after.

You will need a tax number in either country before you can transact — in Portugal that is the NIF (see our guide to getting a Portuguese NIF), in Spain the NIE.

What it costs to hold

Annual costs are where Portugal pulls ahead for most American owners, and the reason is wealth tax.

Both countries levy a municipal property tax on rateable value: IMI in Portugal, set by each município between 0.3% and 0.45% for urban property, and IBI in Spain, set by each ayuntamiento. Those are comparable and neither is the differentiator. The differentiator is what sits above them.

Spain charges non-residents the Impuesto sobre el Patrimonio by obligación real — that is, on assets located in Spain — with an exempt minimum of €700,000 and a state scale starting at 0.2% and rising to 3.5% on the largest estates. An American who owns a €1.2 million villa in Spain and nothing else there is inside that tax; the same American owning a €1.2 million apartment in Lisbon is not, because Portugal has no general wealth tax. What Portugal has instead is AIMI, an additional charge on property only, which applies above an allowance of €600,000 per owner at 0.7% on the first million, 1% from €1 million to €2 million and 1.5% above €2 million. Because the allowance is per owner, a couple holding jointly effectively shelters €1.2 million of Portuguese property value before AIMI begins — and AIMI is charged on the rateable value, which in Portugal is routinely well below the market price.

Spain also imposes an imputed income charge on a second home that sits empty: a non-resident owner who does not rent the property out still files and pays a small annual amount calculated from the cadastral value. It is not large, but it surprises owners who assumed that an unlet property generates no Spanish tax return at all.

Rental income — the 24% trap

If you intend to let the property, this section is the one that changes the answer, and it is the point most Portugal-vs-Spain comparisons get wrong.

Portugal taxes rental income in Category F at an autonomous rate of 25%, charged after the deductions that category permits — the costs of maintaining and running the property, IMI, condominium charges and so on. The effective rate on a real-world letting is therefore meaningfully below 25% of the rent you collect.

Spain taxes non-resident rental income under the IRNR at a rate that depends on where you live, not where the property is. Residents of the EU, Iceland, Norway and Liechtenstein pay 19% and may deduct expenses. Everyone else — explicitly including US citizens — pays 24%, and the taxable base is the full amount received from the tenant with no deduction of any expenses. The 19% figure quoted in most English-language guides to Spanish property simply does not apply to an American.

The gap this opens is much larger than the six-point difference in headline rates suggests. Take a property producing €20,000 of annual rent with €6,000 of deductible running costs — a conservative ratio for an older European apartment once you count IMI or IBI, insurance, management and maintenance:

But the credit is limited to the US tax on that income, so once the foreign rate exceeds the US rate the excess is stranded in a carryforward rather than refunded. Residency routes compared

Neither country gives residency for buying property, so both decisions run through an ordinary visa.

Portugal. The D7 is the long-standing route for people living on their own income — pensions, rents, royalties, dividends — and requires documentary proof of that income and of its availability in Portugal. The D8 digital-nomad visa covers people working remotely for a foreign employer or clients, and requires average monthly income over the previous three months of at least four times the guaranteed monthly minimum wage, which is €920 in 2026 — roughly €3,680 a month. The Golden Visa still exists but, with no property route, means a €500,000 qualifying fund or one of the smaller donation options; our Portugal Golden Visa guide covers the fund route and its US tax complications in detail, and the Greece comparison puts it against the main European alternative.

Spain. The non-lucrative visa requires financial means of 400% of the IPREM for the main applicant plus 100% of the IPREM for each dependant, full private health insurance with no co-payments, and — the binding condition — that you do not work. The digital nomad visa created by the Startup Law is the route for people who do work remotely. The investment route closed in April 2025. Our Spain non-lucrative visa guide walks through the consulate process and the renewal cycle.

In both countries the visa is the start of a residence permit that renews on a fixed cycle, and in both the moment you spend more than 183 days in-country you become a local tax resident on worldwide income — which is a far bigger event for an American than the visa itself. Portugal's IFICI regime (the much narrower successor to NHR, offering 20% on eligible Portuguese-source professional income) and Spain's impatriate "Beckham" regime are the two planning tools for that moment, and both are local-side only: neither touches your US filing obligation.

Citizenship: both now ten years

Until May 2026 this section would have had a clear winner. Portugal required five years of legal residence for naturalisation — the shortest in Western Europe, and the single biggest reason Americans chose it over Spain. Lei Orgânica n.º 1/2026, published in the Diário da República on 18 May 2026 and in force from the following day, ended that. Naturalisation now requires ten years of legal residence for nationals of countries outside the EU and outside the Portuguese-speaking bloc — a category that includes the United States — and seven years for CPLP and EU nationals.

Spain's ordinary naturalisation requirement is also ten years. Spain does offer a two-year fast track for nationals of Ibero-American countries, the Philippines, Equatorial Guinea, Portugal and Sephardic Jews of Spanish origin, but a US citizen without one of those connections does not qualify for it. Spain also, in principle, requires renunciation of the previous nationality for most naturalising foreigners, where Portugal does not — a difference that matters more to many Americans than a year or two of timeline, and one to raise specifically with counsel.

The practical conclusion: if a second passport is the objective, Portugal no longer has the structural advantage it had, and a ten-year commitment to genuine residence in either country is a life decision rather than an investment decision. Decide on the basis of where you want to live.

Selling and dying: the exit taxes

Spain's treatment of a non-resident seller is simple and well documented: the gain is taxed at 19%, and the buyer is obliged to retain 3% of the agreed price and pay it over to the Agencia Tributaria on Form 211 as a payment on account of the seller's bill. If the 3% exceeds the real liability you reclaim the difference; if it falls short you pay the balance. Build the retention into your cash-flow expectations at closing, because it is withheld from the proceeds, not billed afterwards.

Portugal taxes a non-resident's property gain too, but the mechanics for non-residents have been reshaped repeatedly by European Court of Justice litigation over whether non-residents may access the same 50% inclusion that residents get under article 43 of the IRS Code. We are not going to state a single inclusion rate here, because the official sources we could consult do not resolve it cleanly for a non-resident selling in 2026 — get the figure that will apply to your filing year from a Portuguese accountant before you sign, and treat any blog quoting a flat 28% as unverified. Our guide to selling property in Portugal as a US citizen covers the process and the US side.

On death the two countries diverge sharply. Portugal has no inheritance tax; gratuitous transfers fall under stamp duty, and spouses, descendants and ascendants are exempt, so a Portuguese home typically passes to a surviving spouse or children with no Portuguese tax charge. Spain's Impuesto sobre Sucesiones y Donaciones is a genuine tax, is administered regionally, and the relief available to a spouse or child differs enormously from one autonomous community to the next — in some, close-family inheritance is almost fully relieved; in others it is not. Both countries apply forced-heirship rules that can override the terms of a US will, though the EU Succession Regulation allows you to elect the law of your nationality to govern the estate. If a Spanish property is going to form a material part of what you leave, the regional ISD position is worth modelling before you buy, not after.

The US layer on top of everything

Everything above describes the Portuguese or Spanish side. The American side is identical in both countries and does not go away.

You remain taxable by the United States on worldwide income for as long as you hold the passport. Rent from Lisbon or Málaga goes on Schedule E, converted to dollars, and the building depreciates over 30 years under ADS rather than the 27.5 years a US rental would get. A future sale is a US capital gain computed in dollars, so a move in the euro can manufacture a taxable gain on a property that barely rose in euro terms. Local tax paid is creditable on Form 1116 — but only to the extent of the US tax on that income, which is precisely why a low local rate is not the win it looks like: a country that taxes you lightly simply leaves more for the IRS to collect.

The property itself is not a foreign financial account and is not reportable on an FBAR or Form 8938. The Portuguese or Spanish bank account you open to pay the notary, receive the rent and settle the IMI or IBI almost certainly is, once your foreign accounts exceed $10,000 in aggregate at any point in the year. If you hold through a company rather than in your own name — occasionally suggested in Spain for inheritance-planning reasons — you add Form 5471 or Form 8858 exposure on top, with substantial penalties for a missed filing. Raise the structure question with a cross-border CPA before you buy, because unwinding a bad one later triggers tax in both countries.

Frequently asked questions

Is it cheaper to buy property in Portugal or Spain?

They are charged on completely different logic. Since 20 May 2026 Portugal applies a flat 7.5% IMT to any acquisition of residential property by a non-resident buyer, with no exemption or reduction — so an American buying from the US pays 7.5% regardless of price, plus 0.8% stamp duty. That charge is cancelled on request if you become Portuguese tax resident within two years, or let the property as affordable housing within six months. Spain's ITP on a resale home is set by each autonomous community, so the rate depends on where you buy rather than how much you spend; the default national rate where a region has not legislated is 6%. New-build purchases in Spain carry VAT plus stamp duty instead of ITP.

How is rental income taxed for an American who owns property in Portugal or Spain?

Portugal applies a 25% autonomous rate to Category F rental income, after the deductions that category allows. Spain charges non-residents under the IRNR at 19% for residents of the EU, Iceland, Norway and Liechtenstein — but 24% for everyone else, which includes US citizens, and on the gross rent with no deduction of expenses. That no-deductions rule is the single most expensive difference between the two countries for an American landlord, and it is easy to miss because most comparison articles quote the 19% figure.

Does Spain tax American owners on wealth, and does Portugal?

Spain does. Non-residents are liable to the Impuesto sobre el Patrimonio by obligación real — on assets situated in Spain — with a €700,000 exempt minimum and a state scale running from 0.2% to 3.5%. Portugal has no general wealth tax; it has AIMI, an additional property charge that applies only above a €600,000 allowance per owner, at 0.7%, 1% and 1.5% by band. A couple owning one expensive Spanish home can therefore face an annual charge that a couple owning the same-value Portuguese home does not.

Which country gives an American residency more easily in 2026?

Both run passive-income routes and remote-work routes, and neither requires a property purchase. Portugal's D7 is for people living on their own income and its D8 digital-nomad visa requires average monthly income of at least four times the guaranteed monthly minimum wage — €920 in 2026, so about €3,680 a month. Spain's non-lucrative visa requires 400% of the IPREM for the main applicant plus 100% for each dependant, and bars you from working. Spain's Golden Visa was abolished in April 2025; Portugal's survives but has no property route.

Which country leads to an EU passport faster?

Neither is quick any more. Portugal's five-year track — for years the fastest in Western Europe — was abolished by Lei Orgânica n.º 1/2026, published on 18 May 2026 and in force the next day: naturalisation now needs 10 years of legal residence for nationals of countries outside the EU and the Portuguese-speaking bloc, which includes Americans. Spain's ordinary requirement is 10 years as well. The old shortcut arithmetic that made Portugal the obvious answer no longer applies.

Do I still pay US tax if I move to Portugal or Spain?

Yes. The United States taxes citizens on worldwide income wherever they live, so you file a Form 1040 every year regardless of Portuguese or Spanish residency. Local tax paid can usually be credited on Form 1116, but a credit only ever offsets tax you actually paid abroad — so a low-tax local regime does not reduce your total bill, it simply shifts more of it to the IRS. A foreign bank account used to buy or run the property will also usually trigger an FBAR.

What happens to the property when I die — Portugal or Spain?

This is one of the sharpest differences. Portugal has no inheritance tax as such; gratuitous transfers are charged to stamp duty, and spouses, descendants and ascendants are exempt. Spain's Impuesto sobre Sucesiones y Donaciones is a real tax, is set regionally, and the relief available to a surviving spouse or child varies enormously between autonomous communities. Both countries also apply forced-heirship rules that can override a US will, although the EU Succession Regulation lets you elect the law of your nationality.

Is Portugal's IFICI regime the same as the old NHR?

No. IFICI — widely called NHR 2.0 — replaced the old non-habitual resident regime and is much narrower. It targets qualifying scientific, technical and innovation-related activity rather than retirees, and the headline benefit is a 20% rate on eligible Portuguese-source professional income. Spain's comparable regime is the impatriate or Beckham regime. For a US citizen, both are planning tools on the local side only; neither changes the fact that the IRS taxes the same income.
