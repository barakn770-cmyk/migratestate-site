Fideicomiso Explained: Cost, Setup and US Tax for Americans Buying in Mexico (2026)

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated September 2026  ·  11 min read
A fideicomiso is a Mexican bank trust that lets you own restricted-zone property (within 50 km of the coast or 100 km of a border) with every meaningful ownership right. Setup runs roughly $1,600–$2,700 — a fixed government permit fee of about $21,649 MXN (≈$1,150 USD) plus a bank fee of $500–$1,500 — and the annual trustee fee is typically $500–$800. On the US side, IRS Revenue Ruling 2013-14 confirms a standard fideicomiso is not a foreign trust, so Forms 3520/3520-A generally are not required.

If you are buying anywhere near Mexico's coastline or a land border, you will run into one word within your first phone call to a real-estate attorney: fideicomiso. It sounds more exotic — and more precarious — than it is. This guide breaks down exactly what it is, what it actually costs in 2026 (with a calculator you can run on your own numbers), how it works on the US tax side, and when a Mexican corporation is the better tool instead.

What a fideicomiso actually is

A fideicomiso is a bank trust, not a lease and not a workaround with an expiration date on your rights. A Mexican bank holds legal title to the property as trustee; you, the beneficiary, hold every right that matters — you can live in the home, renovate it, rent it out, sell your rights, and pass it to your heirs. The bank has no discretion: it acts only on your written instructions and cannot use, mortgage or sell the property on its own initiative.

It exists because of one constitutional restriction, explained next — and it has been the standard vehicle for foreign ownership of Mexican coastal and border property for over 30 years, used by hundreds of thousands of owners without incident.

The legal basis: Article 27 and the Ley de Inversión Extranjera

Article 27 of the Mexican Constitution bars foreigners from holding direct title ("dominio directo") to land inside the restricted zone — a strip 100 kilometers wide along Mexico's land borders and 50 kilometers wide along its coastlines. That single sentence is why Cancún, Tulum, Playa del Carmen, Puerto Vallarta and Los Cabos all require a fideicomiso for foreign residential buyers, while inland cities like Mexico City, Guadalajara or San Miguel de Allende do not.

The workaround comes from the Ley de Inversión Extranjera (Foreign Investment Law, "LIE"). Article 11 lets a Mexican credit institution (a bank) acquire, as trustee, the rights to use and enjoy restricted-zone real estate on a foreigner's behalf — the bank holds bare legal title; you hold everything else. Article 13 caps the trust term at 50 years, renewable indefinitely on request. That 50-year figure is itself a 1993 upgrade: the prior 1973 foreign-investment law had capped trusts at just 30 years, and the current 50-year cap has stood unchanged since the LIE replaced it.

Setting one up: the SRE permit and the bank

Two separate approvals happen in parallel, both arranged by your closing attorney or notario:

  1. The SRE permit. The Secretaría de Relaciones Exteriores (Mexico's foreign ministry) must authorize the bank to act as trustee for your specific property, under LIE Article 11. This is a government filing, not a negotiation — the fee is fixed by statute (see below), and SRE's own published service standard is a 5-business-day turnaround, though its page notes that timeline can slip due to backlog on its online filing system.
  2. The bank trust deed. You choose a trustee bank (Banamex/Citibanamex, Scotiabank México, BBVA México, Multiva, Actinver and Intercam all act as fiduciaries), which drafts the trust deed naming you as beneficiary and, if you choose, a substitute beneficiary. No Mexican trustee bank publishes a public fee schedule online, so get a written quote before you commit to one — fees are broadly similar but not identical bank to bank.

Both pieces close alongside the rest of the transaction before the notario público, the government-appointed lawyer who validates and records the sale.

Put together, the fideicomiso-specific piece of a purchase usually runs $1,600–$2,700 at setup, which on a typical $250,000–$400,000 home is well under one percentage point of the price — a small slice of the 7%–10% total closing-cost figure quoted for restricted-zone purchases. Most of that 7%–10% is the acquisition tax (ISAI), notary fees and registry costs, which apply to any Mexican purchase, trust or no trust.

Beneficiary, substitute beneficiary and inheritance

Under Mexico's general trust statute (Ley General de Títulos y Operaciones de Crédito), you name your beneficiary — and, if you want, a substitute beneficiary to inherit your rights — either in the original trust deed or in a later written instruction to the trustee bank. No separate government filing is required to add or change a substitute beneficiary; it is a private-contract matter between you and the bank. One statutory limit worth knowing: a chain of successive substitute beneficiaries cannot run on indefinitely — it is capped at people already alive (or conceived) when you, the original settlor, pass away, an anti-perpetuity rule similar in spirit to US trust law.

The practical upshot: naming heirs correctly in the trust deed lets the property pass to them outside Mexican probate. That is a real advantage over direct-title inheritance, but it does not replace your US estate plan — coordinate the trust deed's beneficiary designation with your US will or revocable trust so the two do not conflict.

Selling or transferring your fideicomiso rights

You never owned the property directly, so on a sale you are legally assigning your beneficial rights under the trust — either to a new fideicomiso the buyer sets up (with its own SRE permit) or via an assignment mechanism your notario structures. Either way, the transaction needs fresh SRE clearance naming the new beneficiary, and Mexico's capital gains tax (ISR) applies to your gain on sale, calculated under Mexican rules. On the US side, the same sale is a US-reportable capital gain (Form 8949/Schedule D), with a Foreign Tax Credit generally available for the Mexican tax paid — the mechanics are covered in full in our guide to selling property in Mexico as a US citizen.

Fideicomiso vs. Mexican corporation

A fideicomiso is built for one restricted-zone property, held for personal use. If you plan to hold several rental properties in the restricted zone, a Mexican corporation (sociedad) is usually the better tool — LIE Article 10 lets a Mexican company with the standard foreigners-admission clause take direct ownership of restricted-zone real estate for non-residential (commercial, industrial or rental) use, with only a post-acquisition notice to SRE rather than a per-property permit, no 50-year renewal clock, and no separate trust for each address.

The US side: Rev. Rul. 2013-14, FBAR and Form 8938

Is the fideicomiso itself a foreign trust?

This was the single biggest worry for American buyers for decades, and it has a clear answer. IRS Revenue Ruling 2013-14 examined a standard Mexican land trust — where the bank's only job is to hold bare legal title and follow the beneficiary's instructions, with no independent management discretion — and held that it is not a trust under Treasury Regulation §301.7701-4(a). No subsequent IRS guidance has superseded this ruling. Because it is not a trust for US tax purposes, the foreign-trust information-return regime does not apply, and Forms 3520 and 3520-A are generally not required for a plain, bare-title fideicomiso.

Read the scope carefully: the ruling explicitly does not apply if the bank holds other assets in the same trust, or has any duty or discretion beyond holding and transferring title to that one property. A standard residential fideicomiso from any of the major trustee banks fits the protected pattern; a customized structure holding cash or multiple assets might not. Confirm your specific trust deed with a cross-border CPA.

What still needs reporting

The property itself is never reported to the IRS. What can trigger reporting is the money around it: a Mexican bank account (often opened for closing funds or property-tax payments) that tops $10,000 in aggregate triggers an FBAR (FinCEN Form 114); depending on your total foreign financial assets, you may also cross the Form 8938 (FATCA) threshold. Rental income is ordinary income reported on Schedule E, with a Foreign Tax Credit (Form 1116) for Mexican tax paid on the same income. A sale is a US capital gain, as covered above.

Is a fideicomiso worth the hassle?

For the vast majority of American buyers in Cancún, the Riviera Maya, Puerto Vallarta or Los Cabos, yes — it is a mature, well-understood instrument, its cost is a small fraction of the purchase price, and the US tax treatment is settled and favorable. The two things worth real attention are picking a trustee bank you're comfortable with for the long haul (you'll pay it every year for decades) and naming your beneficiaries correctly the first time, since fixing a trust deed later is more friction than getting it right at signing.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a fideicomiso cost to set up?

Budget roughly $1,600–$2,700 total: the government SRE permit fee (about $21,649 MXN, roughly $1,100–$1,200 USD, fixed by the Ley Federal de Derechos) plus a bank setup fee of about $500–$1,500 USD. That is typically well under 1% of the purchase price.

What is the annual fideicomiso fee?

Roughly $500–$800 USD per year, paid to the trustee bank. No bank publishes a public fee schedule, so get a written quote before choosing one.

Is a fideicomiso a foreign trust for US tax purposes?

No. IRS Revenue Ruling 2013-14 holds that a standard Mexican land trust used only to hold legal title to a residential property is not a trust under Treasury Regulation 301.7701-4(a), so Forms 3520 and 3520-A are not required for a plain, bare-title fideicomiso.

How long does a fideicomiso last, and can it be renewed?

Up to 50 years under Article 13 of the Ley de Inversión Extranjera, renewable indefinitely on request before it expires.

Can I name someone to inherit my fideicomiso property?

Yes. You name a substitute beneficiary in the trust deed or a later written instruction to the trustee bank, and it passes outside Mexican probate — though your US estate plan should still address it.

Should I use a fideicomiso or a Mexican corporation?

A fideicomiso for a single personal-use home; a Mexican corporation once you're holding multiple restricted-zone properties for rental or commercial use, since a corporation can take direct ownership with only an SRE notice and no 50-year renewal cycle.

Can I sell or transfer my fideicomiso rights?

Yes. You assign your beneficial rights under the trust, which needs fresh SRE clearance for the buyer, and Mexican capital gains tax (ISR) applies alongside any US tax on the sale.

Do I need to file FBAR or Form 8938 because of a fideicomiso?

Not because of the trust itself. But a Mexican bank account opened around the purchase can independently trigger FBAR once it tops $10,000 in aggregate, and possibly Form 8938.
