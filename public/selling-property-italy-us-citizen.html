Selling Property in Italy as a US Citizen, 2026

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated November 2026  ·  11 min read
Italy's ordinary rule is a five-year clock. Sell a home more than five years after you bought or built it and TUIR article 67(1)(b) generally takes that gain outside Italian income tax. Building land is taxable whenever you sell. A property whose superbonus works were finished within the last ten years can stay taxable under letter b-bis. Inside five years, the Agenzia delle Entrate lets you ask the notary, at the deed, to apply a 26% substitute tax. When Italy's tax is zero, the US foreign tax credit has nothing to offset, so the US result rides on Section 121.

How Italy taxes your 2026 sale

Most countries tax a real-estate gain the moment you realize it, then soften the bill with allowances and reliefs. Italy asks a threshold question first — how long have you owned it? — and then carves out the cases that do not follow that clock.

Under the consolidated income-tax code (the TUIR, article 67, paragraph 1, letter b), and outside the cases in letter b-bis, a gain from selling real property is a "reddito diverso" only if the property was bought or built no more than five years before the sale. After that line, an ordinary home is outside this rule. Listings that say "posseduto da oltre 5 anni" are describing that rule. They are not describing building land, and they are not describing letter b-bis.

Building land (terreno edificabile) is taxable on sale however long you have held it. The same paragraph of letter b says so with the words "in ogni caso." A property received by inheritance is excluded from letter b and from letter b-bis. A property received by gift takes the donor's purchase date as the start of the five-year count. The Agenzia delle Entrate states that gift rule on its plusvalenze page.

Letter b-bis is the exception that survives the five-year line. It taxes a gain on real property where the seller, or another person entitled to the incentive, completed the subsidized works in article 119 of Decree-Law 34 of 19 May 2020 (converted by Law 77 of 17 July 2020) — the superbonus — no more than ten years before the sale. Inherited property is excluded. So is a home that was the seller's, or a family member's, main home for most of the ten years before the sale, or for most of the holding period if that period is shorter than ten years. Article 68 revalues the purchase price or construction cost of a letter b-bis property that was bought or built more than five years before the sale, using the consumer-price index for workers and employees. An ordinary home with no such works is still outside the tax after five years.

When a sale is inside the five-year window and is taxable under letter b, article 68 measures the gain as the difference between the consideration and the purchase price or construction cost, increased by every other cost inherent in the asset. That is the statutory formula. It is a nominal difference: letter b itself has no inflation indexation. The indexation sentence in article 68 is written onto letter b-bis property held more than five years, not onto an ordinary letter b gain.

The 26% imposta sostitutiva election

If the gain is taxable under letter b, the default is to add it to ordinary income and pay IRPEF. TUIR article 11, in force for 2026, sets three brackets: 23% up to €28,000, 33% from €28,000 to €50,000, and 43% above €50,000. The Agenzia delle Entrate's rate page, updated 13 January 2026, says Law 199/2025, article 1, commi 3 and 4, claws back the benefit of the cut from 35% to 33% for a taxpayer whose overall income exceeds €200,000. Regional and municipal surtaxes sit on top of the national tax. This page does not restate those surtax rates.

The alternative is a flat imposta sostitutiva. Article 1, comma 496, of Law 266 of 23 December 2005 lets the seller ask the notary, at the deed, to apply a substitute tax on the gain instead of the article 67(1)(b) income-tax rules. Article 1, comma 695, of Law 160 of 27 December 2019 replaced the words "del 20 per cento" with "del 26 per cento." The Agenzia delle Entrate states the rate as 26%. Its page covers buildings and agricultural land bought, built, or received by gift no more than five years earlier. It does not list building land, which letter b taxes in every case. The request is made to the notary at the rogito. The statute does not call that request a separate income-tax return.

Worked example: a property bought for €300,000 and sold three years later for €380,000, with €10,000 of documented purchase costs, produces a gain of roughly €70,000. Electing the imposta sostitutiva at 26% costs about €18,200, paid once, at the deed. Running the same €70,000 through ordinary IRPEF on top of other Italian income in a high bracket, plus surtaxes, would typically cost more — which is exactly why most sellers in this position take the flat-rate election.

The election isn't automatically the cheaper choice for everyone. A seller who has little or no other taxable income in Italy that year — for example, someone who was never Italian tax-resident and has no other Italian-source income — might find that running the gain through the lower bands of the ordinary progressive scale costs less than a flat 26%. Ask your notary or commercialista to run both numbers before the rogito; once you elect the substitute tax at the deed, that choice is generally final for that sale.

The abitazione principale exemption

Even inside the 5-year window, Italy carves out one more, broader exemption: a sale is not taxable at all — no 26%, no IRPEF — if the property was used as the seller's (or a family member's) abitazione principale, main home, for most of the period between purchase and sale. The test turns on residenza: whether you (or an immediate family member) were formally registered as resident at that address with the local comune for most of your ownership.

This matters enormously for Americans who relocate to Italy, live in a property as their actual home, and then sell within five years — a scenario the ordinary 5-year rule alone would tax. If the residency test is met, the sale is simply outside Italy's capital-gains rules, the same as a sale after the 5-year mark. A pure holiday home, a short-let investment, or a property bought purely as a Golden-Visa-style asset play — never actually lived in as a main residence — does not qualify, and such a sale falls back to the ordinary 5-year/26% framework above.

Documenting this exemption matters: keep your certificato di residenza history, utility bills in your name at the address, and any Italian tax-return filings that list the property as your residence. A notary will typically ask for proof of the registered residenza period before letting you skip the imposta sostitutiva election at the deed.

How the US taxes your Italian sale

The US result turns on three questions: was the property your principal residence, how did the dollar move against the euro, and did you ever rent it out. Here is the whole picture on one page.

This is where Italy's generosity creates its own trap. When your ownership clears the 5-year mark (or you qualify for the main-home exemption), Italy's tax is zero — which sounds like unambiguous good news. But the US foreign tax credit only offsets tax you actually paid. Zero Italian tax means zero credit, so the entire gain, converted to dollars, is exposed to US tax with nothing to shield it — unless Section 121 applies. A seller who assumes "Italy says it's tax-free, so I'm done" can be unpleasantly surprised the following April if the property was a rental or a second home rather than a genuine primary residence. For the general mechanics of foreign-property sales on US returns, see our guide to Form 8949 for a foreign property sale.

The dollar-basis rule compounds this. The IRS measures your gain in US dollars: your basis is the euro purchase price translated at the exchange rate on the day you bought, and your proceeds are translated at the rate on the day you sold. A property that barely moved in euro terms can still produce a meaningful US-dollar gain if the dollar weakened against the euro over your holding period — or the reverse. We cover the dollar-basis computation, Section 121, and depreciation recapture in detail in US capital gains on a foreign home sale.

The Section 121 exclusion works on an Italian home exactly as it would on a house in Ohio: up to $250,000 of gain excluded for a single filer, $500,000 filing jointly, provided you owned and lived in it as your principal residence for at least 24 of the 60 months before the sale. Usefully, the same residenza facts that support Italy's abitazione principale exemption often also support a Section 121 claim — so a true primary residence, sold after genuinely living in it, is the cleanest case on both sides of the Atlantic. For the detail, see our guide to the Section 121 exclusion on a foreign home sale.

The US–Italy income tax treaty does not change any of this mechanically: like almost every US treaty, it lets Italy (where the property sits) tax the gain first and leaves the US to tax its citizens on the worldwide amount, relieving double taxation through the ordinary foreign tax credit rather than any special treaty rate for real property gains.

The mortgage twist: Section 988

If you financed the Italian purchase with a euro mortgage, the loan has its own separate US tax life, entirely apart from the house. Under Section 988, repaying foreign-currency debt when the dollar is stronger against the euro than it was when the loan originated produces a currency gain on the debt itself, taxed as ordinary income at rates up to 37%. This is computed independently of the sale of the home, and — importantly — Section 121 does not touch it. You can fully exclude the gain on the residence under Section 121 and still owe ordinary-income tax on the mortgage payoff.

This catches sellers who assume Section 121, or Italy's own tax-free treatment after 5 years, cleans up the whole transaction. It does not: the house and the loan are two separate calculations, and the loan is the one people forget. We walk through the mechanics, including how to compute the Section 988 gain, in our guide to the foreign mortgage phantom gain.

A euro mortgage on the property? Don't let the currency gain ambush you.

The Section 988 charge on a mortgage payoff is separate from both Italy's own exemptions and the US home-sale exclusion, and it's easy to miss. Speak to a cross-border tax adviser who computes it before you close.

Selling step by step

  1. Check your 5-year clock and residency history first. Confirm the exact purchase date on your prior rogito and whether you (or a family member) were registered resident there for most of the ownership period — these two facts decide whether any Italian tax applies at all.
  2. Model the US side regardless. Even where the Italian tax is zero, compute the dollar-basis gain and test it against Section 121 — a tax-free Italian sale is not automatically a tax-free US one.
  3. Get the APE certificate ready. Commission the energy-performance certificate early; it's required before the property can be marketed.
  4. Clear your Italian taxes. Confirm IMU is paid current and your codice fiscale is active; the notary will check both before the deed.
  5. Appoint independent counsel. Use a lawyer or commercialista who does not also represent the buyer, and arrange a power of attorney if you're selling from the US.
  6. Decide the tax election, if the sale is inside 5 years. Run the 26% imposta sostitutiva against ordinary IRPEF before the rogito. The statute requires a request to the notary at the deed.
  7. Sign the rogito. The notary executes the deed, collects any imposta sostitutiva election directly from the proceeds, and any outstanding mortgage is discharged at completion.
  8. Handle the currency separately. If a euro mortgage is being repaid, run the Section 988 calculation on its own and report any gain as ordinary income.
  9. Report on the US return. File Form 8949 and Schedule D, claim Section 121 with adjustment code H if it applies, and claim any Form 1116 credit for Italian tax actually paid.

If you're not American: what changes

The Italian side of the sale is identical for any foreign seller. The 5-year rule, the 26% imposta sostitutiva election, the abitazione principale exemption, the buyer-paid registration tax, the agent commission, the codice fiscale and IMU-clearance requirements do not change with your passport. What is purely American is the wrapper: worldwide taxation, Section 121, the dollar-basis measurement, Section 988 on a euro mortgage, and Form 8949.

If you are not a US person, drop the IRS layer and substitute the rules of your own country of tax residence. Check three things. First, whether your home country taxes gains on foreign property at all, and whether it offers its own main-residence relief — the UK's private residence relief, Canada's principal residence exemption and Israel's single-dwelling exemption each have their own tests, distinct from Italy's. Second, whether you have foreign asset or account reporting obligations at home covering the Italian property and the sale proceeds. Third, whether a double-taxation treaty exists between your country and Italy, remembering that immovable property is almost always taxable first where it sits. Because Italy's own tax is frequently zero after 5 years, the practical outcome for most non-US sellers is simply: no Italian tax, then whatever your home country charges on its own terms.

Is it worth selling in 2026?

For most sellers, timing is the whole strategy. If you can wait until the 5-year mark — or you already qualify under the abitazione principale exemption — and the sale is not building land and not a letter b-bis superbonus case, Italy's side of an ordinary home is as simple as this tax gets: no article 67(1)(b) gain to compute. For a US citizen, that simplicity shifts the entire burden of the analysis onto Section 121. A genuine primary residence sold after 24 of the last 60 months of real use can exit clean on both sides of the Atlantic. A rental, a holiday home, or a sale inside the 5-year window without the main-home exemption means either the 26% imposta sostitutiva (creditable, at least in part, against the US bill) or full US exposure on a gain Italy never taxed at all.

The sellers who do well are the ones who check the ownership and residency clock months before listing, run the dollar-basis computation honestly, test Section 121 rather than assume it, and price in any Section 988 currency exposure on a euro mortgage before they sign. Get that sequence right, and an Italian sale can be one of the cleaner cross-border exits available to an American. Skip it, and a transaction Italy treated as a non-event can still produce a real US tax bill the following April.

Frequently asked questions

Do I owe Italian tax when I sell property I've owned for more than 5 years?

Generally no, for an ordinary home. TUIR article 67(1)(b) taxes an individual's gain on real property bought or built no more than five years earlier, outside letter b-bis. After five years that ordinary gain is outside the rule. Building land is taxable however long you have held it. Letter b-bis keeps a gain taxable when superbonus works under article 119 of Decree-Law 34/2020 were finished no more than ten years before the sale, unless the property was inherited or was the seller's or a family member's main home for most of that period. Inherited property is outside both letters. A gift is timed from the donor's purchase.

What is Italy's 26% imposta sostitutiva and when do I pay it?

If the gain is taxable under article 67(1)(b), article 1, comma 496, of Law 266 of 23 December 2005 lets the seller ask the notary, at the deed, to apply a substitute tax instead of ordinary income tax. Article 1, comma 695, of Law 160 of 27 December 2019 replaced the words "del 20 per cento" with "del 26 per cento." The Agenzia delle Entrate states the rate as 26% and limits that page to buildings and agricultural land bought, built, or received by gift no more than five years earlier. It does not list building land. For 2026, TUIR article 11 taxes income at 23% up to €28,000, 33% from there to €50,000, and 43% above €50,000. Law 199/2025, article 1, commi 3 and 4, claws back the benefit of the 33% rate when overall income exceeds €200,000. Run both calculations before the deed.

Does the 5-year rule still apply if the property was my main home?

You often don't even need the 5 years. Italian law exempts a sale within 5 years from capital-gains tax entirely if the property was used as the seller's (or a family member's) 'abitazione principale' — main home — for most of the period between purchase and sale, based on residenza registered with the comune. A pure holiday home, a buy-to-let, or a property you never actually lived in does not qualify for this exemption and falls back on the ordinary 5-year/26% rules.

Does a US citizen still owe US tax on an Italian sale that is tax-free in Italy?

Usually yes, unless Section 121 covers it. The US taxes citizens on worldwide gains regardless of what Italy does. When Italy's 5-year rule zeroes out the Italian tax, there is no foreign tax credit to claim — a credit only exists if you actually paid foreign tax — so the full gain, translated into US dollars, is exposed to US capital-gains tax unless the Section 121 home-sale exclusion applies. This is the opposite problem from a country that taxes the full gain: in Italy, the easier the Italian side gets, the more the US result depends entirely on Section 121.

Can I use the US Section 121 exclusion on a home in Italy?

Yes, if it was genuinely your principal residence. Section 121 has no geographic limit: a US citizen who owned and used an Italian property as their main home for at least 24 of the 60 months before the sale can exclude up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 filing jointly. Interestingly, the same facts that qualify you for Italy's abitazione principale exemption — living there as your main home — tend to also support a Section 121 claim, so a true primary residence can often be sold clean on both sides.

What does it actually cost to sell a home in Italy?

Budget for a notary fee of roughly 1%–2.5% of the price (paid by the buyer in most deals, but the seller's own lawyer is a separate cost), real-estate agent commission of about 2%–4% plus 22% VAT, an Attestato di Prestazione Energetica (APE) energy certificate of around €100–€300, and, if there's an outstanding mortgage, a formal cancellation (cancellazione ipoteca) that can take weeks to register. The registration tax and most transfer taxes fall on the buyer, not the seller, under Italy's standard conveyancing practice.
