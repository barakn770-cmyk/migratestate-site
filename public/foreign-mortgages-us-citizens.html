Foreign Mortgages for US Citizens: Country-by-Country Guide

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated September 2026  ·  10 min read
Yes — US citizens can get mortgages abroad, on more conservative terms than at home. Expect 50%–75% LTV (a 25%–50% deposit) and rates around 2.8%–4% in the eurozone or 5%–9% on USD loans in Mexico. A foreign mortgage is not just a loan — for an American it's a currency position bolted onto a house, wrapped in another country's underwriting and another layer of US tax rules. The good news: lenders across Europe and the Americas do finance non-resident US buyers in 2026, often at rates that shame a US mortgage. The catch is everything around the rate. Here's the country-by-country reality, plus the two US-tax facts that decide how you should borrow.

Can Americans borrow abroad?

Yes — many countries lend to non-resident Americans, just cautiously. Lenders want US tax returns, proof of income, bank statements and a clean credit history, and they offset the cross-border risk with lower loan-to-value and larger deposits than a local borrower faces. Accessibility varies widely: Portugal, Spain and France are among the friendliest to non-residents, while Italy underwrites conservatively and the UAE caps non-resident lending tightly.

Europe: Portugal, Spain, France, Italy, Greece

Portugal is one of the most accessible eurozone markets — no restrictions on foreign borrowers, ~60–75% LTV, competitive ~3–4% rates and terms up to 30 years. Spain lends ~60–70% to non-residents at attractive fixed rates (~2.8–3.5%) but adds the mortgage's own stamp duty (AJD). France is surprisingly open to US buyers for residential property, ~60–75% LTV. Italy is the strictest of the four: non-resident mortgages exist but underwriting is detailed and LTV often 50–70%, so many buyers there pay cash. Greece sits close to Italy on caution: major banks such as Alpha Bank publish up to 70% financing for non-EU residents (80% for EU residents), with a choice between long fixed-rate periods (from around 2.7%) and a floating rate tied to 3-month Euribor plus a spread from about 1.8%, plus a small mandatory state levy on every mortgage. Three years of income documentation is standard across all five markets — none of them pull a US credit score, so the paper trail you bring matters more than your FICO number. See the country guides for Portugal, Spain, Italy and Greece.

Mexico, Costa Rica & Panama

Mexico offers the most US-friendly structure on this list: dedicated cross-border USD lenders at ~5–9% — and, unlike every other country on this page, these lenders routinely do pull US credit history — alongside peso bank loans at 9–14% and developer financing — full detail in our Mexico mortgage guide. Costa Rica is the outlier: local banks are legally free to lend to non-residents but in practice rarely do without a local credit history or Costa Rican-sourced income, so most foreign buyers end up with private "hard-money" lenders instead — no credit score required, but LTV capped around 50% (often closer to 30% in practice) and rates commonly 9%–12% or higher, frequently stepping up after an initial fixed period. Panama is more conventional: local banks lend to foreigners at 50%–70% LTV and ~7%–9%, plus a 1% FECI tax on any property that isn't your primary residence, against two years of apostilled tax returns, bank references and proof of income — and a Panamanian mortgage is not reported to US credit bureaus either way.

Dubai (UAE)

Dubai lends to non-residents but conservatively, typically ~50–60% LTV at ~6–7% (often EIBOR-linked), with property-linked residency upside; the Dubai guide covers it. Because the UAE has no local income or capital-gains tax, a dirham mortgage there carries the same US-only tax exposure described below.

The currency question, with a worked example

Before the rate, decide the currency. Borrow in local currency (euro, peso, dirham) and your debt moves with the exchange rate against your dollar income — great if it weakens, painful if it strengthens. Borrow in US dollars (where available, e.g. Mexican cross-border loans) and your dollar income matches your dollar debt, eliminating FX risk. The rule of thumb: match the loan currency to the income that will repay it. A euro-rental that covers a euro mortgage is naturally hedged; a dollar earner servicing a euro loan from US income is carrying a currency bet whether they realize it or not.

Here is what that bet looks like in numbers. Say you buy a €300,000 property in Portugal with a 60% LTV mortgage: a €180,000 loan and a €120,000 cash down payment. At an illustrative EUR/USD rate of 1.08, that's a $324,000 property, funded with roughly $129,600 in cash and a $194,400-equivalent euro loan. A cash buyer converts once, at closing, and is done — no further currency exposure on the purchase itself. A financed buyer carries that €180,000 debt for the life of the loan. If, ten years later, the euro has weakened 10% against the dollar (EUR/USD around 0.97), paying off or refinancing the remaining euro balance costs roughly 10% fewer dollars than it would have at signing — and under Section 988, the IRS can tax that dollar-denominated "saving" as ordinary income, even though you did nothing but repay a loan on schedule. On the simplified numbers above (ignoring amortization for clarity), a 10% dollar move on a $194,400 loan-equivalent is roughly $19,000–$20,000 of potential phantom gain. Run the reverse: if the euro instead strengthens 10%, the financed buyer's real debt burden in dollar terms rises by a similar amount — but there is no offsetting deduction, because a currency loss on a personal-use mortgage generally isn't deductible. The cash buyer never plays this game at all. This asymmetry — taxable gains, non-deductible losses — is the single biggest reason cross-border CPAs steer dollar-earning clients away from unhedged foreign-currency mortgages on a home they intend to use rather than rent out locally.

Budgeting rule: debt service in a foreign currency

If you do borrow locally, budget the monthly payment in the currency you actually earn, not the currency you're borrowing in. A euro payment that looks comfortable today can absorb a noticeably larger share of a dollar income after a currency swing, even with the rate and balance unchanged — the mortgage itself hasn't gotten more expensive in euros, but it has in dollars. Two practical habits from lenders and cross-border planners who work with US buyers: stress-test the payment against a materially weaker dollar (not just today's rate) before committing to a foreign loan, and keep enough of a dollar cash buffer that a bad currency year doesn't force a forced sale or a missed payment. Neither is a legal requirement anywhere on this page's list — they're risk-management habits, not rules — but they're the difference between a currency move being an inconvenience and being a crisis.

US tax: the deduction and the Section 988 trap

Two US-tax facts shape every foreign-mortgage decision. First, the upside: interest on a foreign home loan can be deductible on your US return under IRS Publication 936's qualified-residence rules, exactly like a US second home. The mechanics: the loan must be secured by the property, the home must be one of your two qualified residences (your main home plus one second home — a foreign vacation home qualifies), and the deduction is capped at interest on $750,000 of acquisition debt ($375,000 if married filing separately) for loans taken out after December 15, 2017, or $1,000,000 ($500,000 married filing separately) for loans that predate that cutoff and haven't been refinanced above the original balance. A foreign home used purely as a rental follows business-property rules instead of the home-mortgage-interest rules — a different (and often more generous) set of deductions, but not this one. Second, the trap that catches almost everyone: IRS Section 988 foreign-currency rules, illustrated above. When you repay or refinance a foreign-currency mortgage after the currency has weakened against the dollar, the IRS can treat your "saving" as a taxable foreign-currency gain — ordinary income — even though you simply paid off a loan. Worse, the rule is asymmetric: a currency loss on a personal mortgage generally isn't deductible. So a euro or peso mortgage can hand you a US tax bill at payoff that no one mentioned at signing. This single rule is the strongest argument for either borrowing in USD or borrowing against your US home instead.

Alternatives: US equity, developer and cross-border loans

A local bank mortgage is not the only way to finance a purchase abroad — three other structures show up constantly in practice, each with a different risk trade-off.

  • Borrow against your US home (HELOC or cash-out refinance). The option most "foreign mortgage" articles never mention, and often the smartest one. You borrow in dollars, against US equity, with familiar underwriting and no foreign lender to satisfy — then buy the property abroad in cash. That sidesteps the FX risk, the Section 988 trap, the larger foreign deposit, and the cross-border paperwork in one move, and it often beats the all-in cost of a non-resident foreign loan. The trade-off: you encumber your US home and forgo a natural local-currency hedge if the property earns local-currency rent.
  • Developer financing. Common in Mexico, Panama, and pre-construction markets generally: the developer itself finances part of the purchase price, typically over 2–5 years, often interest-free or at a modest rate during construction, converting to a market rate afterward. It's fast to arrange and needs no bank underwriting, but the terms are set by the seller, not a regulated lender — read the contract for balloon payments, default clauses and what happens if the project is delayed.
  • Dedicated cross-border lenders. A small set of specialist lenders (concentrated in the Mexico market, with a smaller footprint elsewhere) underwrite non-resident US buyers directly in USD, using US income and — unusually for this list — US credit history and tax returns, rather than requiring a local credit footprint. They sit between a US HELOC and a fully local mortgage: dollar-denominated like the HELOC, but secured by the foreign property itself rather than your US home.

For a vacation home a US dollar buyer will use, the US-equity route frequently wins on total cost and simplicity; for a euro- or peso-income rental, a matched local loan or a cross-border USD lender can be the better structural fit. Compare all three before defaulting to whichever a real-estate agent's preferred bank happens to offer.

Step-by-step

  1. Compare both paths first — a foreign mortgage vs a USD HELOC/cash-out on your US home.
  2. Pick the loan currency to match the income that will repay it.
  3. Get pre-qualified locally with an international broker; prepare US tax returns, income proof and statements.
  4. Budget the deposit and costs — 25%–50% down plus local mortgage taxes/fees (e.g. Spain's AJD).
  5. Engage an independent local lawyer for title and the loan documents.
  6. Model the US side — interest deductibility and Section 988 currency exposure — with a cross-border CPA.
  7. Close, register, and set up FBAR/FATCA reporting for any foreign account.

If you're not American: what changes

The lending mechanics are identical for any foreign buyer. The country-by-country LTV, rates, deposits and currency-matching logic apply the same whatever passport you hold. What's US-specific is the tax layer — the mortgage-interest deduction and the Section 988 currency-gain trap.

If you're not American, ignore the Section 988 discussion and the US-equity-vs-foreign-loan framing built around US rules — though the underlying currency risk is universal. Instead check three things in your country of residence: (1) whether mortgage interest on a foreign property is deductible at home; (2) whether your country taxes foreign-currency gains on debt; and (3) whether borrowing against home equity in your country is cheaper than a non-resident foreign mortgage. A UK, Canadian or European buyer will run different numbers than a US dollar earner. Confirm with a tax adviser in your country of residence before borrowing.

Is a foreign mortgage worth it in 2026?

Sometimes — and sometimes a dollar loan against your US home beats it outright. Eurozone rates are genuinely attractive, and a local mortgage that matches local rental income is a clean, hedged structure. But for a US dollar earner buying a home to use, the foreign loan's larger deposit, FX risk and the Section 988 trap often tip the math toward borrowing in dollars. Decide on the currency first, compare the US-equity alternative, and price the US tax before the rate — then a foreign mortgage becomes a tool, not a tripwire.

Frequently asked questions

Can US citizens get a mortgage abroad?

Yes. Many countries lend to non-resident Americans on conservative terms. Portugal, Spain and France are most accessible (~60–75% LTV); Italy and the UAE are stricter. You'll provide US tax returns, income proof and statements, and accept a larger deposit.

How much can an American borrow on a foreign property?

Typically 50–75% of value (a 25–50% deposit). Portugal and France ~60–75%, Spain 60–70%, Italy 50–70%, Mexico 60–70% cross-border, UAE ~50–60%.

What are foreign mortgage rates for US citizens in 2026?

Eurozone rates are ~2.8–4% (often fixed) in Spain, Portugal, France and Italy. Mexican cross-border USD loans run ~5–9% and peso loans 9–14%. Rates depend on term, deposit and currency.

Is interest on a foreign mortgage tax-deductible in the US?

Often yes — interest on a qualified first or second home is deductible within the US limits, even abroad. But borrowing in a foreign currency exposes you to IRS Section 988 at payoff. Confirm both with a cross-border CPA.

Should an American borrow abroad or against their US home?

It depends. A USD HELOC or cash-out on your US home avoids FX risk and the Section 988 trap, often with simpler underwriting. A local foreign mortgage hedges local-currency rental income and keeps US equity intact. Compare both.

Can Americans get a mortgage in Costa Rica or Panama?

In Panama, yes, fairly conventionally: local banks lend to foreigners at roughly 50%–70% LTV and 7%–9%, plus a 1% FECI tax on non-primary residences, against two years of apostilled tax returns. Costa Rica is harder: local banks rarely lend to non-residents without local credit history, so most foreign buyers use private hard-money lenders instead, at LTVs around 30%–50% and rates commonly 9%–12% or higher.

How much could a weak currency cost me on a foreign mortgage?

On an illustrative €300,000 Portugal purchase financed 60% LTV, a 10% weaker euro by payoff could create roughly $19,000–$20,000 of taxable Section 988 gain on the loan alone, even though you simply repaid what you owed. A cash buyer has none of this exposure. Run this kind of scenario with a cross-border CPA before choosing a foreign-currency loan.

What is developer financing, and is it safer than a bank mortgage?

Developer financing is a payment plan the seller itself offers, common in Mexico and Panama pre-construction sales, typically over 2–5 years and often interest-free during construction. It needs no bank underwriting but the terms are set by the seller, not a regulated lender, so read the contract carefully for balloon payments and default or delay clauses before relying on it.
