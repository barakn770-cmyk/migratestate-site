Buying Property in Tulum as an American (2026): Ejido Land, Pre-Construction Risk and Tax

Tulum sits in Mexico's restricted zone, so an American holds title through a fideicomiso, like any coastal purchase. The real risk in Tulum is upstream of that trust: confirming the land was validly converted out of ejido status before it was ever sold. Pre-construction deposits are not held in a statutory escrow, and a project near a cenote needs its own SEMARNAT environmental authorization.

Tulum gets sold as a single story: jungle, cenotes, a beach road full of boutique hotels, and condos that look like they belong in a design magazine. The legal reality underneath a Tulum purchase is less photogenic, and it is different in degree — not just in the usual restricted-zone way — from a resale condo in, say, Puerto Vallarta or Los Cabos. The mechanics that apply to every coastal purchase in Mexico, including the fideicomiso and the 50-kilometer rule, are in our guide to buying property in Mexico. This page is about what is specific to Tulum: a documented history of land whose ejido status was never properly resolved, a pre-construction market that boomed on units some buyers never received, and an environmental permitting layer that exists because the area sits on one of the world's most studied cave-and-aquifer systems.

Tulum is in the restricted zone like any coastal buy

Article 27 of the Constitution bars a foreigner from holding direct title to residential land within 50 kilometers of the coast, and Tulum's entire developed area is inside that strip. An American buyer holds the property through a fideicomiso, a renewable bank trust, after a permit from the Secretaría de Relaciones Exteriores (SRE). The SRE's own cost and timing page lists $21,650 as the 2026 fee to issue that permit. The national mechanics — the 50-year renewable term, beneficiary rules, and what the bank charges on top of the SRE fee — are in the dedicated fideicomiso guide. None of that is unusual to Tulum. What is unusual is what the fideicomiso sits on top of.

The ejido trap: why title, not the trust, is the risk

Much of the land in and around Tulum was, within living memory, ejido land — communal agrarian land granted to a community under Mexico's post-revolutionary land-reform program, which by law could not simply be sold on the open market in its original status. The Ley Agraria sets out the only lawful path for that land to become ordinary private property that a fideicomiso can hold clean title to:

  • The ejido's general assembly, meeting with the quorum and approval thresholds in articles 24–28 (three-quarters of ejidatarios present on first call, two-thirds of those present voting in favor, with a Procuraduría Agraria representative and a notary attending), resolves under article 81 that ejidatarios may adopt dominio pleno (full private ownership) over their individually delimited parcels.
  • Each ejidatario then individually elects, under article 82, to assume dominio pleno over their own parcel and asks the Registro Agrario Nacional (RAN) to remove it from the ejido registry.
  • The RAN issues a title; that title is then recorded at the local Registro Público de la Propiedad. Only at that point, per article 82, does the land "dejar de ser ejidal" (stop being ejidal) and become subject to ordinary civil law.
  • On the first sale of a dominio pleno parcel to an outsider, article 84 gives family members, long-term workers of the parcel, fellow ejidatarios, and the ejido itself a 30-day right of first refusal (derecho del tanto); skipping that notice is grounds to annul the sale.

Skip any step — an assembly that never actually met the quorum, a RAN filing that was started but never finished, a right-of-first-refusal notice that was never sent — and the "title" a buyer is shown can be defective no matter how official the paperwork looks or how many past resales it has been through. This is not a theoretical risk specific to one bad actor; it is a structural feature of how a tourism boom outran a decades-long federal land-reform program in this exact region. A buyer's protection is procedural, not cosmetic: have an independent notario pull the parcel's history directly from the RAN and the Registro Público de la Propiedad, not just read a seller-supplied certificado de libertad de gravamen (lien-free certificate), and confirm the dominio pleno conversion is actually recorded, not merely "in process."

Quintana Roo's 2% ISABI and closing costs

Tulum is one of six municipalities — along with Bacalar, Felipe Carrillo Puerto, José María Morelos, Lázaro Cárdenas, and Solidaridad (Playa del Carmen) — covered by the Ley del Impuesto Sobre Adquisición de Bienes Inmuebles de los Municipios del Estado de Quintana Roo. Article 9 sets the rate at a flat 2%: "el impuesto se calculará aplicando la tasa del 2% al valor del inmueble." Article 8 defines that value as the highest of three figures, each capped at 180 days old at the time of acquisition: the price actually paid (adjusted by the consumer price index), the cadastral appraisal, or a bank or state-registered appraiser's valuation. There is no separate foreigner surcharge; a US buyer pays the same 2% as a Mexican buyer.

Two things this page will not do: quote a specific notario percentage, because the notarial tariff is set case by case and not by the ISABI statute, and quote a specific predial rate, because Tulum's current cadastral tables and any early-payment discount are set and changed by the municipality, not by state law. The tax is simple. The title is the hard part.

Quintana Roo's 2% ISABI is easy to budget. Confirming the land under it was never ejido — or was properly converted if it was — is the diligence step that actually protects your money.

Pre-construction: escrow myths and the oversupply years

Tulum's condo market grew fast through the 2020s on pre-construction sales, and not every project that took deposits finished on schedule, or finished at all. Mexican law does not create a statutory construction escrow the way some US states require for new-development deposits; a developer is free to spend a buyer's deposit as it comes in unless the purchase contract itself routes the money into a controlled account the buyer and an independent notario can verify. "The deposit is in escrow" is a contract term to confirm, line by line, not a legal default you can assume.

Before signing a pre-construction contract or wiring a deposit, confirm three separate things, each independently of the sales office: that the developer's own title to the land is clean (not an unconverted ejido parcel, see above); that any environmental authorization the specific project needs has actually been issued, not merely "in process" (see the next section); and that the payment schedule releases money against verified, photographed construction milestones rather than a fixed calendar that pays out whether or not the building rises. A buyer's representative — not the developer's notario, and not the developer's in-house "legal team" — should be the one confirming all three.

Cenotes, SEMARNAT, and what gets demolished

Tulum sits on part of the Yucatán Peninsula's cenote-and-cave aquifer system, and construction with a potential environmental impact — including building near a cenote, in mangrove, or inside the federal maritime-terrestrial zone along the coast — generally needs a Manifestación de Impacto Ambiental (MIA) authorized by SEMARNAT (the Secretaría de Medio Ambiente y Recursos Naturales) before construction begins, under the framework of the Ley General del Equilibrio Ecológico y la Protección al Ambiente. This is ordinarily the developer's obligation on a pre-construction project, not something an individual condo buyer files personally, but it is a real obligation with real consequences for skipping it: Mexican authorities have ordered illegally built structures in the Tulum area torn down, and SEMARNAT has blocked or rejected hotel and extraction projects in and around Tulum's protected areas on environmental grounds. A buyer should ask the developer or seller to produce the project's MIA resolution (or confirm, for an existing building, that one was obtained at the time of construction) rather than assume that a building standing today was necessarily built with one.

This page does not quote a MIA processing fee or timeline, because both vary by the modality of the assessment (particular vs. regional) and the project's specific footprint, and inventing a number here would not help a buyer evaluate a real contract.

Short-term lets and the condo reglamento

As in any Mexican condominium, the building's own registered reglamento de condominio can prohibit or restrict short-term rentals even where the municipality and a listing platform would otherwise allow them. Read the reglamento and the most recent assembly minutes before treating nightly income as a feature of the unit rather than a privilege the building's owners can vote to withdraw. State and municipal lodging-tax rules that may apply to short-term rental income are a separate question from the ISABI purchase tax above; this page covers the transfer tax a buyer pays at closing, not the ongoing tax treatment of rental income, which belongs with your accountant once you know how you intend to use the unit.

US tax: Revenue Ruling 2013-14

IRS Revenue Ruling 2013-14 holds that a standard Mexican fideicomiso used to hold a personal residence is not a trust for United States tax purposes, and that holds for a Tulum property exactly as it does anywhere else in the restricted zone. Forms 3520 and 3520-A are not required for that arrangement. If you rent the unit, the income is still reportable in the United States; how foreign residential property is depreciated is covered in the depreciation guide, and Mexico-specific reporting is in US taxes on Mexican property. Selling has its own withholding mechanics, covered in selling property in Mexico as a US citizen. A Mexican bank account used for the purchase or for rent is not the house itself, but it can trigger the FBAR once your foreign accounts together exceed $10,000 at any point in the year, and possibly FATCA reporting on Form 8938 at higher thresholds — see our Form 8938 guide for exactly when a foreign account, as opposed to the directly held real estate itself, crosses that line.

Step-by-step

  1. Hire your own notario and your own abogado — not one recommended by the seller or developer — before you sign anything.
  2. Trace the title back through the RAN and the Registro Público. Confirm, with primary records, that the parcel was never ejido, or completed every step of dominio pleno conversion if it was.
  3. For pre-construction, verify the land title, the project's environmental authorization, and a milestone-based (not calendar-based) payment schedule before any deposit leaves your account.
  4. Open the fideicomiso with a Mexican bank and pay the SRE's 2026 issuance fee of $21,650.
  5. Budget the 2% ISABI on the highest of price, cadastral value, or appraisal, plus notario and appraisal fees you confirm case by case.
  6. Close, register, and keep every document — the closing statement is your US cost basis.
  7. If you intend to live there, start a residency application separately; the deed is not a visa. See the temporary resident visa guide.

If you're not American

Article 27, the SRE fideicomiso permit, the ejido/dominio pleno title rules, Quintana Roo's 2% ISABI, and the SEMARNAT environmental-permitting framework all apply to a foreign buyer regardless of passport; none of them are US-specific. What changes for a non-American is the home-country tax treatment. Revenue Ruling 2013-14 and the FBAR are United States rules. If you are not a US person, ask an adviser in your own country whether a Mexican fideicomiso is treated as a trust there, and whether a Mexican bank account is reportable at home.

Frequently asked questions

Can an American legally own property in Tulum?

Yes, but only through a fideicomiso, a bank trust, because Tulum sits inside the restricted zone under Article 27 of the Mexican Constitution (within 50 kilometers of the coast). The buyer applies through a notario and a bank for a permit from the Secretaría de Relaciones Exteriores, whose 2026 fee for issuing that permit is $21,650. The bigger risk in Tulum specifically is not the fideicomiso mechanism itself, which is routine, but confirming the land under the fideicomiso was validly converted out of ejido status before anyone tried to sell it.

What is ejido land and why does it matter in Tulum?

Ejido land is Mexican communal agrarian land that cannot be sold on the open market in its original status. Under the Ley Agraria, an ejido assembly can vote, by a three-quarters quorum and two-thirds approval (articles 24-28), to let ejidatarios adopt dominio pleno (full private ownership) over their individually assigned parcels (article 81), after which the Registro Agrario Nacional removes the parcel from the ejido registry and issues a title the ejidatario can record at the Registro Público de la Propiedad (article 82). A buyer must confirm this conversion actually happened and was properly registered, rather than relying on a certificate that looks official. Much of the land around Tulum was ejido land within living memory, and some of it has been sold informally without completing this process.

What transfer tax do I pay when buying in Tulum?

Tulum is one of six municipalities covered by Quintana Roo's Ley del Impuesto Sobre Adquisición de Bienes Inmuebles de los Municipios del Estado de Quintana Roo. Article 9 sets the rate at 2% of the property's value. Article 8 defines that value as the highest of the price paid, the cadastral appraisal (no more than 180 days old), or a bank or registered appraiser's valuation (also no more than 180 days old). At an illustrative rate of 20 pesos per US dollar (use the day's actual rate; this page does not publish an official exchange rate), a US$400,000 price is MXN 8,000,000 and the 2% ISABI on that price is MXN 160,000, before notary, registry, and appraisal fees. The tax base can be higher than the price if the cadastral value or the appraisal is higher.

Is pre-construction buying in Tulum safe?

Not automatically. Mexican law does not require a developer to hold pre-construction deposits in a regulated escrow the way some US states do. A buyer's deposit is contractual money unless the contract itself routes it into an account the buyer and an independent notario can verify. Confirm, before signing, that the developer holds clean title to the land (not an unconverted ejido parcel), that any required SEMARNAT environmental authorization for the specific site has actually been issued, and that the payment schedule is tied to verifiable construction milestones rather than the calendar.

Do I need a SEMARNAT permit to build near a cenote in Tulum?

A project with potential environmental impact, including construction near a cenote, mangrove, or the federal maritime-terrestrial zone, generally requires a Manifestación de Impacto Ambiental (MIA) authorized by SEMARNAT before construction, under the Ley General del Equilibrio Ecológico y la Protección al Ambiente framework. This is normally the developer's obligation on a pre-construction project, not an individual condo buyer's, but a buyer should ask to see the project's MIA resolution rather than assume one exists, since unauthorized construction near Tulum's aquifer has been ordered demolished before.

Does Revenue Ruling 2013-14 cover a Tulum fideicomiso?

Yes. IRS Revenue Ruling 2013-14 applies to a standard Mexican fideicomiso used to hold a personal residence anywhere in the restricted zone, Tulum included, so Forms 3520 and 3520-A are not required for that arrangement. Rental income and any later sale are still reportable on your US return, and a Mexican bank account can trigger the FBAR once your foreign accounts together exceed $10,000 at any point in the year.

Does buying a condo in Tulum give me Mexican residency?

No. The deed and the SRE fideicomiso permit are not a visa. Residency is a separate application, most commonly a temporary resident visa, and is unrelated to how the property is titled.
