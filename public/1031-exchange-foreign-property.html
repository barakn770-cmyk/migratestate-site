1031 Exchange on Foreign Property: The Rules for Americans, 2026

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated July 2026  ·  11 min read
You cannot 1031 between US and foreign real estate — ever. Under IRC Section 1031(h), US property and property located abroad are not like-kind, in either direction. What can defer US capital gains tax is a foreign-to-foreign exchange — one overseas investment property for another — if you meet the 45-day and 180-day deadlines through a qualified intermediary. But Section 1031 is a US rule only: the foreign country still taxes your sale now, which can strand your foreign tax credit.

What a 1031 exchange actually does

A Section 1031 "like-kind exchange" lets a US taxpayer sell one investment property, roll the entire proceeds into another, and defer the capital gains tax that would otherwise be due on the sale. The gain does not disappear — it rides along in the basis of the new property until you eventually sell without exchanging again — but the deferral can compound value for decades. It is one of the most powerful tools in US real-estate tax planning, and the question Americans buying abroad ask constantly is whether it reaches across borders.

The short answer is that it reaches abroad, but not across the US border. Since the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, Section 1031 applies to real property only — personal property and business equipment were cut out — and that narrowing survived unchanged through the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act, so the 2026 rules are the same stable framework. Within real property, the geography rule in subsection (h) is the one that catches people out, and it is worth understanding exactly, because it is absolute.

The 1031(h) wall: US and foreign are never like-kind

Congress wrote the answer directly into the statute. IRC Section 1031(h) provides that real property located in the United States and real property located outside the United States are not property of a like kind. There is no ambiguity, no facts-and-circumstances test, and no exception to argue around. If one leg of your exchange sits on US soil and the other sits abroad, the exchange fails and the gain is fully taxable in the year of sale.

The most important thing to grasp is that this bar runs in both directions:

So an American who dreams of selling a US rental and rolling the gain, tax-deferred, into a villa in Italy is out of luck: that specific move is exactly what 1031(h) forbids. The proceeds from a US sale must go into US replacement property to defer, and the proceeds from a foreign sale must go into foreign replacement property. The only recognised gloss on "US property" is a narrow one — under Section 1031(h) certain US territories, historically the US Virgin Islands, Guam and the Northern Mariana Islands, can be treated as within the United States for this purpose when their own tax rules mesh with the Code — but that is a technicality for territory investors, not a doorway to Europe.

When a foreign-to-foreign exchange works

Here is the part that surprises people: a US citizen or green card holder can use Section 1031 entirely outside the United States. Because the US taxes its citizens and residents on worldwide income, the sale of a foreign investment property is a US-taxable event in the first place — and the same worldwide reach means the IRS will honor a like-kind exchange of that foreign property for another foreign one. Sell an investment apartment in Lisbon, roll the proceeds into an investment apartment in Madrid through a proper exchange, and the US capital gains tax on the Lisbon gain is deferred just as it would be on a Phoenix-to-Reno swap.

All the ordinary machinery applies unchanged. Both the relinquished and replacement properties must be held for investment or productive use in a trade or business. A qualified intermediary must hold the proceeds. The identification and closing deadlines must be met. Nothing about the property being overseas relaxes those requirements — if anything, executing them cleanly across a foreign closing process, a foreign notary and a foreign currency is harder, which is exactly why the timing trap in the next-but-one section bites so often.

The rules that don't bend: QI, 45/180, investment use

A 1031 exchange is not a loose "sell one, buy another" concept — it is a rigid choreography, and a single misstep collapses it. Three rules cause almost every failure.

You may never touch the money. The proceeds of the sale must flow to an independent qualified intermediary (QI) engaged before your sale closes, who then uses them to acquire the replacement. If the cash ever lands in your own hands or bank account, the IRS treats it as "constructive receipt," the exchange is dead, and the gain is taxed. This is a particular hazard on a foreign closing, where funds routinely route through a local notary or lawyer's client account — that path can amount to constructive receipt unless the QI structure is set up correctly around it.

The two clocks are absolute. From the day your sale closes you have 45 days to identify replacement property in writing — specific address or legal description, delivered to the QI, no vague "an apartment in Barcelona" — and 180 days to close on it. The clocks run simultaneously, not back-to-back, and they do not extend for weekends, holidays, a slow foreign land registry, or any other reason. Foreign transactions are slower and less predictable than US ones, so the 180-day wall is where cross-border exchanges most often break.

It has to be investment property. Section 1031 only covers property held for investment or business use. A home you live in, or a vacation place you use personally, does not qualify — more on that below.

The numbers to memorise: 45 days to identify, 180 days to close, both starting at the sale closing and running at the same time; 0 days you are ever allowed to hold the proceeds yourself. Miss any of them and the deferral is gone with no appeal.

The foreign-tax timing trap most guides miss

This is the nuance that almost no consumer article explains, and it can turn a "tax-deferred" exchange into a genuine double tax. Section 1031 is a United States rule. The country where your property sits has never heard of it and is not bound by it. When you sell your Lisbon apartment, Portugal taxes that gain in the year of the sale, exactly as if you had simply sold and walked away — the fact that the US lets you defer is irrelevant to the Portuguese tax authority.

Now line the two systems up. In the year of the exchange you pay foreign capital gains tax now, but the US recognises no gain — you deferred it. Ordinarily the foreign tax credit is what stops the same gain being taxed twice. But the credit can only offset US tax on that same income, and this year there is no US tax on the gain to offset, because you deferred it. The foreign tax paid this year can be stranded. Then, years later, when you finally sell the replacement property without a further exchange, the US taxes the built-up gain — and the foreign tax you paid back in the exchange year may be long gone, outside the ten-year foreign-tax-credit carryforward window and in the wrong basket to help. You can end up having paid the foreign country in full and the US in full on overlapping gain.

The bitter irony is that the 1031 deferral, designed to save tax, is what breaks the credit that would otherwise have relieved the double tax. Had you simply sold and paid the US tax in the same year the foreign tax fell due, the two would have met in the same year and the credit could have absorbed most of the foreign tax. This is why a foreign-to-foreign 1031 is often worse than not exchanging at all — unless the foreign country happens not to tax the gain, or offers its own rollover relief that mirrors the US deferral. Modelling this before you sign is the whole ballgame.

Your overseas home is not a 1031 property

A great many Americans buying abroad are buying a home, not an investment — and Section 1031 does nothing for a home. It applies only to property held for investment or productive use in a trade or business, so a personal residence and a purely personal vacation home are both outside it. Renting a place out casually for a few weeks a year rarely converts a personal home into investment property; the IRS looks at your actual use and intent, and mixed personal use is scrutinised hard.

If you are selling a foreign property you genuinely lived in, the right tool is not 1031 at all but the Section 121 exclusion, which can shelter up to $250,000 of gain ($500,000 for a married couple) on a main home you owned and occupied for two of the last five years — and it applies to a qualifying foreign home just as to a US one. We cover the details, and the currency wrinkle that trips owners up, in the guide to the Section 121 exclusion on a foreign home sale. For a property that really is an investment, the related question of how the eventual sale is reported is covered in Form 8949 for a foreign property sale, and the credit mechanics in the foreign tax credit guide.

How a foreign-to-foreign exchange runs, step by step

  1. Confirm both legs are foreign and both are investment property. Relinquished and replacement must each sit outside the US and be held for investment or business. This is the 1031(h) and use test — get it wrong and nothing else matters.
  2. Engage a qualified intermediary before closing. The QI must be in place and documented before you sell. Choose one experienced in foreign exchanges who can handle a non-US closing without you taking constructive receipt.
  3. Sell the relinquished property. Proceeds go to the QI, never to you. Coordinate the foreign notary or lawyer's client account so the money does not pass through your control.
  4. Identify replacements within 45 days. In writing, unambiguous, delivered to the QI, respecting the three-property or 200% identification rules. The clock is calendar days from closing.
  5. Close on the replacement within 180 days. Build in wide margin — foreign registries, financing and translation routinely eat weeks. There is no extension.
  6. Model the foreign tax now, not later. Work out what the source country taxes on the sale this year and whether the foreign tax credit can ever absorb it given the US deferral. This is where the exchange is won or lost.
  7. Report it on Form 8824. The exchange is disclosed on Form 8824 with your US return for the year, carrying the deferred gain into the basis of the replacement, with amounts translated to US dollars.
  8. Track basis and currency. Keep the deferred gain, the dollar basis and the exchange rates documented — the gain resurfaces on the ultimate sale, and any local-currency mortgage can add a separate Section 988 issue.

If you're not American: what changes

Section 1031 is a US-only mechanism. The local rules on your foreign sale — how the country where the property sits taxes the gain, its transfer taxes, and its own conveyancing — are identical whatever passport you hold. What is purely American is the 1031 deferral itself, the 1031(h) US/foreign bar, and the 45/180-day machinery. A non-US owner does not use Section 1031.

If you are not a US person, ignore 1031 entirely and look to whether your country of tax residence offers a comparable "rollover" or "reinvestment relief" that defers gain when you sell one investment property and buy another. Several do, and the conditions vary widely. Check three things. First, whether your home country taxes the gain on a foreign property sale at all, and whether it offers any deferral or rollover for reinvestment. Second, whether you have foreign-asset or account reporting obligations at home covering the property and the proceeds. Third, whether a double-taxation treaty exists between your country of residence and the country where the property sits — immovable property is almost always taxable first where it is located, with your home country relieving the double tax second. A British, Canadian, Australian or Israeli owner runs the same sale through an entirely different domestic rulebook, so confirm the detail with a tax adviser where you are resident.

Is it worth doing in 2026?

For an American, the honest answer is: rarely, and only with eyes open. The dream version — sell a US rental, defer the gain, and reinvest tax-free in a place in Europe — is flatly impossible under 1031(h), and no adviser can structure around it. The version that is legal, a foreign-to-foreign exchange, is technically available but frequently self-defeating, because the deferral desynchronises your US tax from the foreign tax you must pay in the year of sale and can strand the very credit that would have prevented double taxation.

Where it earns its keep is a narrow band of cases: a foreign investment property in a country that does not tax the gain, or that offers its own matching rollover relief, held by an owner who intends to keep investing abroad and can absorb the mechanics. Outside that band, an American is often better off simply selling, paying the US tax in the same year the foreign tax falls due so the foreign tax credit can do its job, and reinvesting freely — including back into the US, which a 1031 replacement could never allow. The tool is real, but for cross-border owners it is a scalpel, not a shortcut. Run the two tax systems side by side before you sell, and let the numbers, not the appeal of the word "deferral," decide.

Frequently asked questions

Can I do a 1031 exchange between US and foreign real estate?

No. IRC Section 1031(h) says real property located in the United States and real property located outside the United States are not property of a like kind. That rule runs both ways: you cannot exchange a US rental for a house in Portugal, and you cannot exchange a Paris apartment for a Florida condo, and defer the gain. It is a hard statutory bar with no exception, workaround, or planning structure that overcomes it.

Can a foreign-to-foreign 1031 exchange defer US tax?

Yes. Because a US citizen or green card holder is taxed on worldwide income, the IRS will honor a like-kind exchange of one foreign investment property for another — say a rental in Lisbon for a rental in Madrid — and defer the US capital gains tax, provided all the normal Section 1031 rules are met: both properties are held for investment or business, a qualified intermediary holds the proceeds, and the 45-day identification and 180-day closing deadlines are respected.

Does a 1031 exchange stop the foreign country from taxing my sale?

No. Section 1031 is a US deferral rule, and the country where the property sits is not bound by it. Most countries tax the gain in the year you sell, whether or not the US lets you defer. That creates a timing mismatch: you pay foreign tax now while the US recognizes no gain this year, so there may be no US tax for the foreign tax credit to offset — the credit can be stranded and the same gain effectively taxed twice.

Can I 1031 exchange my overseas vacation home?

Generally no. Section 1031 applies only to property held for investment or productive use in a trade or business. A personal residence or a purely personal vacation home does not qualify. A US owner selling a foreign home they lived in should look instead to the Section 121 exclusion, not a 1031 exchange. A genuine foreign rental held for investment can qualify, but only for another foreign investment property.

What are the 1031 exchange deadlines in 2026?

Two clocks start the day your sale closes and run at the same time. You have 45 days to identify replacement property in writing, unambiguously, delivered to your qualified intermediary, and 180 days to close on it. Neither deadline can be extended for weekends, holidays, or any other reason. These rules were unchanged for 2026 — the 2025 OBBBA tax law left Section 1031 intact, and since 2017 it applies to real property only.
