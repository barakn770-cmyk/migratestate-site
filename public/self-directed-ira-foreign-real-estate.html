Buying Foreign Real Estate with a Self-Directed IRA (2026)

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated November 2026  ·  14 min read
A self-directed IRA can hold foreign real estate, but the IRS never created a special account for that purpose. Publication 590-A (2025) requires a bank, a federally insured credit union, a savings and loan association, or an IRS-approved entity as trustee or custodian, and it requires contributions other than rollovers to be in cash. You do not take title. Buying the property for personal use, now or later, is a prohibited transaction, and the account stops being an IRA as of 1 January of the year that happens. If the IRA borrows, Publication 598 pulls a slice of the rent into unrelated debt-financed income, and the 2025 Instructions for Form 990-T require a filing once gross unrelated business taxable income is $1,000 or more.

Custodians market "self-directed IRAs" as if they were a separate kind of retirement account that the IRS designed for houses, private notes, and foreign land. They are not. An IRA is the trust described in section 408(a), held by a qualified trustee. "Self-directed" is the industry name for a custodian that will accept assets a brokerage IRA will not, including real estate. The tax rules that follow are the ordinary IRA rules. The foreign deed, the notary, and the land registry are a second problem the IRS publications do not solve.

What "self-directed" actually means

Publication 590-A states the opening rule in one sentence: the trustee or custodian must be a bank, a federally insured credit union, a savings and loan association, or an entity approved by the IRS to act as trustee or custodian. A friend, a local attorney, or you cannot be the trustee because you would like to close faster. The 2025 Instructions for Form 990-T add that a custodian is treated as a trustee under section 408(h), and that each IRA is a separate trust. If that trust will file Form 990-T, it needs its own employer identification number. One owner with three IRAs does not file one combined return for all three.

The same instructions draw a line that marketing pages skip. Individual retirement annuities are not subject to unrelated business income tax. A self-directed real-estate purchase is an account. If a salesperson describes the investment as an annuity, stop and have the custodian say, in writing, which kind of IRA this is.

Cash in, title in the custodian's name

Publication 590-A is equally direct about what you may put into the account. Contributions, except rollover contributions, must be in cash. You cannot deed a condo in Lisbon, a lot in Baja, or a flat you already own into the IRA and call it a contribution. A rollover moves assets from another IRA or eligible plan. It is not a way to contribute property you hold personally.

The publication also says assets in the account cannot be combined with other property, except in a common trust fund or common investment fund, and that money in the account cannot be used to buy a life insurance policy. For a house, the practical reading is that the IRA's asset stays the IRA's asset. You do not put your name on the deed next to the custodian's "so the bank will accept it," and you do not pay the deposit from a personal account and plan to sort it out later. The custodian signs. The custodian wires. If a local seller will only contract with a natural person, that is a reason to walk away or to buy outside the IRA, not a reason to sign personally and "assign" the contract into the account after the fact. See how to hold property abroad in your own name or an entity if the IRA route is the wrong wrapper.

Prohibited transactions

The IRS page "Retirement topics — Prohibited transactions" defines a prohibited transaction in an IRA as any improper use of the account by the owner, the beneficiary, or any disqualified person. Disqualified persons include the owner's fiduciary and members of the family: spouse, ancestor, lineal descendant, and any spouse of a lineal descendant. A child, a parent, and a child's spouse are inside that circle. A cousin is not on that list. Do not invent a wider ban, and do not assume a cousin is safe if the cousin is also acting as a fiduciary of the account.

The same page gives four examples. Borrowing money from the IRA. Selling property to it. Using it as security for a loan. Buying property for personal use, present or future, with IRA funds. The last one is the rule that ends most foreign-house plans. A retirement home you intend to occupy in ten years is personal use in the future. A week in the house "to check on the tenants" is personal use in the present. Renting it to your spouse, or to your adult child, is a use by a disqualified person. The page does not publish a de minimis number of nights.

What a prohibited transaction does to the IRA

The consequence is not a fine you pay and then carry on. The IRS page says that if the owner or a beneficiary engages in a prohibited transaction at any time during the year, the account stops being an IRA as of the first day of that year. The account is treated as distributing all of its assets at their fair market values on that first day. If that total is more than your basis in the IRA, the difference is taxable income. The distribution date is 1 January, not the day you slept in the house. A prohibited use in November reaches back to the start of the year and sweeps in every asset in that IRA, not only the foreign house.

Debt, rent, and Form 990-T

A mortgage is a different problem from a prohibited transaction, and the two official texts do not collapse into one safe answer. Publication 598 explains unrelated debt-financed income. Investment income that would otherwise be excluded from an exempt organization's unrelated business taxable income is included to the extent it comes from debt-financed property. Rental real estate is one of the sources the publication names. For each property, the included income is a percentage, not over 100 percent, of the gross income from the property. That percentage is the average acquisition indebtedness divided by the property's average adjusted basis for the year.

The publication's own example is the one to use, because it is the IRS's arithmetic and not a hypothetical foreign deal. An exempt organization owns a debt-financed office building that produced $10,000 of gross rental income. Average acquisition indebtedness was $50,000 and average adjusted basis was $100,000, so the debt/basis percentage was 50 percent. Unrelated debt-financed income was $5,000. On a sale, the publication uses a different percentage: the highest acquisition indebtedness in the 12 months before the sale, divided by average adjusted basis, applied to the gain or loss. The publication is written for exempt organizations. The Form 990-T instructions are what put an IRA on that form.

The 2025 Instructions for Form 990-T say a traditional IRA under section 408(a), a SEP, a SIMPLE, and a Roth IRA must file if they have gross unrelated business taxable income of $1,000 or more. The return is due the 15th day of the fourth month after the end of the trust's tax year. The $5,000 in the publication's example is already over that filing line. This page does not restate the trust tax-rate brackets. Those are on the form and its instructions for the year you file, and they are not a number this guide should invent.

The prohibited-transaction page, separately, lists "using it as security for a loan" as a possible prohibited transaction. Publication 598 assumes debt-financed property can exist and then taxes a slice of the income. Those are not the same sentence. A loan the IRA takes, secured in a way the custodian believes is not a pledge of the IRA to a disqualified person, is a structure many custodians offer and that this page will not bless. Have the custodian and a tax advisor read both IRS texts against the actual loan documents before the account borrows. A personal guarantee from you is a different and worse fact, because you would be a disqualified person extending credit or furnishing security. Do not sign one to "help the deal close."

What the foreign country will and will not do

Nothing on the IRS pages tells a Spanish notary, a Mexican trustee bank, or a Portuguese land registry to accept a US custodian as buyer. The custodian's willingness to hold the asset and the country's willingness to record it are separate consents. Ask the notary, before any reservation deposit, whether the deed can name the custodian for the benefit of the IRA, and ask the custodian whether that form of title is one it will accept. If either answer is no, the purchase does not happen inside the IRA.

Mexico's restricted zone is the clearest example of a local rule the IRS does not mention. Within 50 kilometres of the coast or 100 kilometres of the border, a foreign buyer uses a fideicomiso, the bank trust described in our fideicomiso guide. The SRE permit pages that guide cites do not say whether a US IRA may be the beneficiary. Treat that as an open question for the trustee bank and the notary, not as a yes. Buying outside the restricted zone, where a foreigner can take title directly, still requires the custodian to be the party on the deed.

Two US rules travel with you regardless of the country. You still cannot use the house. And if the IRA borrows, the debt-financed-income rules above still apply to the rent and to a later sale, even when the local country taxes that rent lightly or not at all. A local tax holiday does not switch off Form 990-T. If you are comparing a cash purchase in your own name with a financed purchase inside an IRA, the foreign-mortgage guide covers the personal-loan side. It is not a guide to an IRA borrowing.

FBAR and the account around the property

Directly held foreign real estate is not itself an FBAR asset. Our FBAR guide for foreign property owners and the Form 8938 guide are about the accounts and the entities around the property. An IRA purchase adds a custodian to that picture. Rent may land in an account the custodian opens in the country, or it may be paid to the custodian in the United States. Ask, in writing, whose name is on any foreign account and who files if the balance crosses the FBAR aggregate. Do not assume "it's a retirement account, so nobody reports it."

When to buy in your own name instead

Buy outside the IRA if any of these are true. You want to stay in the house, even occasionally. A spouse or child will stay in it. The seller or the notary will only contract with you personally. The custodian will not hold foreign real estate, or will not hold it in that country. You would need to guarantee a loan. You are trying to contribute a house you already own. In every one of those cases the IRA route either fails the IRS examples or fails the local closing. Owning in your own name, and paying US tax on the rent and the gain in the ordinary way, is often the cleaner structure. The prohibited-transaction cost of getting this wrong is the end of that IRA, dated 1 January, on the whole account.

Steps before the custodian wires

  1. Confirm with the custodian that the account is an IRA account under section 408(a) or a Roth under section 408A, not an individual retirement annuity, and that the custodian will hold foreign real estate in the country you named.
  2. Fund it with cash or a rollover. Do not contribute the property, and do not sell property you own to the IRA.
  3. Ask the local notary whether the deed can name the custodian. In Mexico's restricted zone, ask the trustee bank whether an IRA can be the fideicomiso beneficiary. Get the answer before a reservation contract.
  4. If the purchase needs a loan, have the custodian and a tax advisor read the prohibited-transaction examples and Publication 598 against the loan documents. Do not sign a personal guarantee.
  5. If there will be debt, budget for Form 990-T once gross unrelated business taxable income is $1,000 or more, and get the IRA its own EIN.
  6. Write down who will manage the property. You can direct the custodian. You cannot use the house, and a disqualified person should not be the tenant.

Frequently asked questions

Can a self-directed IRA buy property in another country?

The IRS rules for an IRA do not forbid foreign real estate as such. Publication 590-A requires a bank, federally insured credit union, savings and loan association, or IRS-approved entity as trustee or custodian, and it requires non-rollover contributions to be in cash. The custodian, not you, holds the investment. Whether a given country's land registry will record a US custodian as owner is a local question the IRS pages do not answer.

Can I live in a house my IRA buys?

No. The IRS lists buying property for personal use, present or future, with IRA funds as a prohibited transaction. The same list covers your spouse, ancestors, lineal descendants, and the spouses of those descendants, because they are disqualified persons. If you or a beneficiary engages in a prohibited transaction, the account stops being an IRA as of the first day of that year and is treated as distributed at fair market value on that day.

Can I move a foreign house I already own into my IRA?

Not as a contribution. Publication 590-A says contributions, other than rollovers, must be in cash. Selling property you already own to the IRA is on the IRS list of prohibited transactions. The IRA has to buy the property with cash already in the account, or with a rollover of existing IRA assets, through the custodian.

What happens if the IRA borrows to buy the property?

Publication 598 treats rental income from debt-financed property as unrelated debt-financed income, in proportion to the debt divided by the property's average adjusted basis. The 2025 Instructions for Form 990-T say a traditional IRA, SEP, SIMPLE, or Roth IRA must file Form 990-T if gross unrelated business taxable income is $1,000 or more, and that each account is a separate trust that needs its own employer identification number. The same IRS prohibited-transaction page lists using the IRA as security for a loan as a possible prohibited transaction. Read both texts with the custodian before the account borrows.

Does an IRA annuity follow the same real-estate rules?

No. The 2025 Instructions for Form 990-T say individual retirement annuities, unlike IRA accounts, are not subject to unrelated business income tax. A self-directed real-estate purchase is an account held by a custodian, not an annuity.

Is the foreign property itself an FBAR asset?

Directly held foreign real estate is not an FBAR asset. A foreign bank account is a different thing. Ask the custodian whether any account opened to pay local taxes or receive rent is in the IRA's name and who reports it, and read the FBAR guide for the US$10,000 aggregate rule.
