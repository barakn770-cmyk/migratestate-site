Depreciating Foreign Rental Property: The 30-Year ADS Rule (2026)

MS By The MigrateState Editorial Team · Reviewed by a licensed international real-estate attorney  ·  Updated September 2026  ·  12 min read
A rental property outside the United States cannot be depreciated over 27.5 years. Because it is "used predominantly outside the United States," IRC §168(g) forces it onto the Alternative Depreciation System (ADS): straight-line over 30 years for a residential rental placed in service after 2017 (40 years if placed in service earlier), and 40 years for foreign commercial property. Only the building is depreciated, never the land; furniture and appliances get longer ADS lives too; bonus depreciation and §179 are unavailable. Depreciation is not optional — the IRS reduces your basis whether or not you claimed it and taxes that amount at up to 25% when you sell. If you have been using 27.5 years, or nothing at all, Form 3115 is the fix.

Ask ten American owners of an apartment in Lisbon, a casita in Tulum or a flat in Athens how they depreciate it, and most will say "27.5 years, same as my rental back home" — if they depreciate it at all. Both answers are wrong, and the second is worse than the first. A rental property located outside the United States is governed by a different depreciation system with a longer recovery period, a rule that sits in the statute itself, not in a footnote. Getting it wrong overstates your deduction every year on a return the IRS can revisit, and skipping it altogether hands you a recapture bill at sale for a deduction you never enjoyed. This guide is the deep dive that our foreign rental income guide only had room to flag: exactly what the Alternative Depreciation System requires, how to set the basis up correctly in year one, and how to repair the filings if you've been doing it the domestic way.

Why foreign property is forced onto ADS

US depreciation for real estate runs under the Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System (MACRS), which has two branches. The General Depreciation System (GDS) is the default and gives residential rental property the familiar 27.5-year straight-line life. The Alternative Depreciation System (ADS) is the slower, mandatory-in-some-cases branch. IRC §168(g)(1)(A) says that for "any tangible property which during the taxable year is used predominantly outside the United States," the depreciation deduction "shall be determined under the alternative depreciation system." IRS Publication 946 restates it plainly in its list of property for which ADS is required: "Any tangible property used predominantly outside the United States during the tax year."

"Predominantly outside" has a bright-line definition in the regulations: property is treated as used predominantly outside the United States if it is "physically located outside the United States during more than 50 percent of the taxable year" (Treas. Reg. §1.48-1(g)(1)(i)). A building cannot travel, so a house in Portugal is outside the US 100% of the year and the test is met automatically. The statute carves out a short list of exceptions in §168(g)(4) — US-registered aircraft, vessels documented under US law, certain satellites, and property of a US person used in a US possession — none of which describe a villa. There is no election out, no treaty override, and no de minimis rule: if the property is abroad and you rent it, ADS is the system.

This is the "CPA-level" mistake for a reason. Most US tax software defaults to GDS when you enter a residential rental, and nothing in the interview asks where the building is. The owner picks 27.5 years, the software applies the standard table, and the error compounds silently for a decade. The fix is not hard — but you have to know to make it.

30 years, 40 years — which one is yours?

ADS uses the straight-line method over the recovery periods set out in §168(g)(2)(C). For real estate the periods are:

Property (used predominantly outside the US)GDS (not available)ADS recovery period
Residential rental property placed in service after 31 December 201727.5 years30 years
Residential rental property placed in service before 1 January 201827.5 years40 years
Nonresidential real property (shop, office, commercial unit)39 years40 years
Additions and improvements to the building—Same period as the building, starting when the improvement is placed in service

The 30-year figure is a Tax Cuts and Jobs Act change (Pub. L. 115-97, §13204), which shortened the ADS period for residential rental property from 40 to 30 years for property placed in service after 31 December 2017. It did not reach back: Publication 946's ADS table carries the footnote "40 years for property placed in service before January 1, 2018." So an American who started renting out a Spanish apartment in 2015 stays on a 40-year schedule for that building, while a neighbor who bought and rented in 2019 is on 30 years. (The one narrow exception in the footnote — a pre-2018 property held by an "electing real property trade or business" that was not already subject to §168(g)(1) — does not help a foreign rental, because §168(g)(1)(A) already applied to it.)

"Placed in service" is the date the property is ready and available for rent, not the closing date. If you bought in March, renovated until September and listed it in October, the recovery period starts in October. And "residential rental property" keeps its US definition abroad: a building where 80% or more of the gross rental income is from dwelling units. A mixed-use building in Italy with a shop on the ground floor may fail that test and land on the 40-year nonresidential period instead.

Fixing the basis: land, building and dollars

Three basis questions decide the annual number, and foreign purchases complicate all three.

Land is never depreciable. Publication 527 is explicit: if your cost includes the land, "you must divide the cost between the land and the buildings," allocating in "the ratio of the FMV of that asset to the FMV of the whole property at the time you buy it," and if you are not sure of those values you may use the split in the assessed values for local property tax. That second route is often the practical one abroad — a Portuguese caderneta predial, a Spanish valor catastral breakdown or a Mexican avalúo typically shows land and construction values separately. An apartment in a large condominium block carries a small land share; a beach villa on a big plot carries a large one. Get the allocation in writing from the appraisal or the cadastral record and keep it with your file, because it is the first thing an examiner asks for.

Basis is cost, in US dollars, at the historical rate. The IRS requires that "you must express the amounts you report on your U.S. tax return in U.S. dollars," using "the exchange rate prevailing when you receive, pay, or accrue the item." Your building's depreciable basis is therefore the purchase price plus capitalized acquisition costs — notary and registration fees, transfer tax, legal fees — each translated at the rate on the date paid, and then frozen. It does not float with the euro or the peso afterwards; a weaker dollar later does not raise your basis, and a stronger one does not lower it. Include every capitalizable closing cost: on a €300,000 purchase in a country with 8–10% in transfer taxes and fees, that is €25,000–€30,000 of extra depreciable basis that owners routinely forget.

A home converted to a rental uses the lower of two numbers. If you lived in the foreign property first and later rented it, Publication 946 sets your depreciable basis at the lesser of fair market value on the date of conversion and your adjusted cost basis. In a market that fell after you bought — or where the dollar strengthened sharply against the local currency between purchase and conversion — the FMV figure can be the binding one, and it is measured in dollars at conversion.

The math: 27.5 vs 30 years on a real purchase

Take an American who buys a furnished apartment in Valencia for the dollar equivalent of $400,000 including closing costs, places it in service on 15 March, and whose cadastral record allocates 20% to land. The depreciable building basis is $320,000.

ItemGDS 27.5 years (wrong for foreign property)ADS 30 years (required)
Annual straight-line depreciation (full year)$11,636$10,667
First year (placed in service in March, mid-month convention = 9.5 months)$9,212$8,444
Annual rate3.636%3.333%
Overstatement if GDS is usedAbout $970 per year of excess deduction — roughly $9,700 over a decade, all of it reversed on audit or at sale

Both systems use the mid-month convention for real property: Publication 946 states that under it you "treat all property placed in service or disposed of during a month as placed in service or disposed of at the midpoint of the month," so a March start earns 9.5 months of depreciation in year one, and the year you sell also gets a half month for the month of sale. The difference between 27.5 and 30 years is not dramatic in any single year — which is exactly why it goes unnoticed — but it is a systematic overstatement, and an impermissible method used for two consecutive years locks you into an accounting method that can only be corrected through Form 3115 (see below). Note also that depreciation rarely eliminates tax on a well-let property: on a $320,000 basis, $10,667 a year shelters roughly $890 a month of rent.

Furniture, appliances and improvements

The ADS rule is not limited to the building. Everything in the property that is "tangible property used predominantly outside the United States" is on ADS too, and Publication 527's recovery-period table (Table 2-1) gives the lives side by side:

Asset in a foreign rentalGDS life (not available abroad)ADS life (required)
Appliances (stoves, refrigerators), carpets, furniture used in rental property5 years9 years
Computers and peripheral equipment5 years5 years
Office furniture and equipment7 years10 years
Property with no class life (most other contents)7 years12 years
Fences, roads, shrubbery (land improvements)15 years20 years
Building, structural components and improvements (new roof, kitchen, boiler)27.5 years30 years

Two practical consequences. First, a furnished let should be inventoried at purchase: a €15,000 furniture package bundled into the price can be carved out and written off over 9 years instead of 30, which is worth doing. Second, improvements versus repairs follows the ordinary US rule — repairs and maintenance are deducted on Schedule E in the year paid, while an improvement (a new roof, a full kitchen, a replacement boiler) is capitalized as "separate depreciable property" with, per Publication 946, "the same" property class and recovery period as the original building, starting when the improvement is placed in service. A 2026 renovation of a 2015-vintage foreign rental therefore runs 30 years from 2026 even though the building itself is on the older 40-year schedule. Personal property under ADS uses the half-year (or mid-quarter) convention rather than mid-month.

No bonus depreciation, no §179

Owners who read about 100% bonus depreciation being restored for property acquired after 19 January 2025 sometimes hope to expense the furniture or a renovation abroad in one year. They cannot. Publication 946 is direct: "If you are required to use ADS to depreciate your property, you cannot claim any special depreciation allowance," and the statute itself excludes from "qualified property" for bonus purposes "any property to which the alternative depreciation system under subsection (g) applies" (§168(k)(2)(D)). The same publication lists "property used predominantly outside the United States" among the property for which the section 179 deduction cannot be elected. In short, a foreign rental gets straight-line ADS on every asset class, with no front-loading of any kind. The upside is that a straight-line schedule is simple and predictable; the downside is that owners comparing after-tax yields between a US rental and a foreign one should not assume the same first-year write-offs.

Recapture: why skipping it costs more

Here is the rule that makes depreciation mandatory in substance, not just in form. Publication 946: "You must reduce the basis of property by the depreciation allowed or allowable, whichever is greater," and "if you do not claim depreciation you are entitled to deduct, you must still reduce the basis of the property by the full amount of depreciation allowable." When you sell, that reduced basis increases your gain — and the portion of the gain attributable to depreciation is unrecaptured section 1250 gain, which IRS Topic 409 confirms "is taxed at a maximum 25% rate," above the 0/15/20% long-term capital-gains brackets that apply to the rest.

Run the Valencia example forward. After ten years of allowable ADS depreciation the building basis has fallen by about $104,000 whether or not the owner ever deducted a cent. Sell for a $150,000 nominal dollar gain over the original $400,000 cost, and roughly $104,000 of the total gain is taxed at up to 25% as recapture, with the remainder at capital-gains rates. An owner who "kept it simple" and never depreciated paid full ordinary-rate tax on the rent for ten years and now pays 25% recapture on deductions never taken. That is the worst of both worlds, and it is entirely avoidable. Two further points sharpen it: the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax can stack on top for higher earners, and if the property later became your main home, the Section 121 exclusion does not shelter gain attributable to depreciation claimed after 6 May 1997 (IRS Publication 523). Been filing it wrong? The Form 3115 fix

There are two ways to have got this wrong — 27.5 years instead of 30 (or 40), or no depreciation at all — and the repair route depends on how long it has been going on. Publication 946 explains that you "adopt a method of accounting for depreciation by using a permissible method … when you file your first tax return, or by using the same impermissible method of determining depreciation in two or more consecutively filed tax returns." If the error appears on only one filed return, an amended return (Form 1040-X) can correct it. Once it has appeared on two or more consecutive returns, it is an adopted method, and the publication lists "a change from an impermissible method of determining depreciation" and "a change in the depreciation method, period of recovery, or convention" as changes that require Form 3115, Application for Change in Accounting Method.

The good news is that this particular change is an automatic one under the IRS's list of automatic changes (Revenue Procedure 2025-23 and its successors, referenced in Publication 946), so it does not need advance IRS consent or a user fee: Form 3115 is attached to the current-year return and a copy is sent to the IRS. The correction is made through a section 481(a) adjustment — the cumulative difference between the depreciation you claimed and the depreciation allowable under the correct method. For an owner who never depreciated, that adjustment is a one-time deduction of all the missed years on the current return, which can be a substantial negative adjustment taken in full in the year of change. For an owner who used 27.5 years, the adjustment goes the other way (income), because too much was claimed. Either way, the result is a clean 30-year schedule going forward and a basis that matches what the IRS will assume at sale. This is a job for a cross-border CPA, but it is routine, and it is far cheaper than discovering the problem during a sale-year audit.

How depreciation interacts with the foreign tax credit

US depreciation is a purely US concept, and the country where the property sits will compute your local rental tax its own way — some allow a building allowance on their own schedule, some allow a flat percentage deduction, and some tax gross rent with no depreciation at all. The result is that your US net rental income (after 30-year ADS depreciation) is frequently lower than the income the foreign country taxed. That matters for the foreign tax credit: Form 1116 limits the credit to the US tax on the foreign-source income as computed under US rules, so when depreciation shrinks the US figure, the foreign tax you paid can exceed the creditable amount and generate excess credits in the passive basket, which carry back one year and forward ten. Slower ADS depreciation actually softens this mismatch slightly compared with GDS — a smaller deduction means more US income to absorb the credit — but the planning point stands: model both countries' computations together rather than assuming the credit will wash everything. Depreciation also never reduces the foreign tax itself, and it does not change your FBAR or Form 8938 obligations on the accounts the rent flows through, which are covered in our guide to tax reporting for property abroad.

Step-by-step on Form 4562

  1. Fix the placed-in-service date — the day the property was ready and available to rent, not the closing date. This decides 30 vs 40 years and the first-year mid-month fraction.
  2. Build the dollar basis — purchase price plus capitalized acquisition costs, each converted at the exchange rate on the date paid, then frozen.
  3. Split land from building — by FMV ratio or the local assessed-value ratio; keep the appraisal or cadastral document.
  4. Carve out contents — furniture, appliances and carpets go on 9-year ADS; other personal property with no class life on 12-year ADS.
  5. Enter the building in Form 4562, Part III, Section C — the ADS section: line 20c for the 30-year period (40-year property goes on line 20d), straight-line, mid-month convention. In later years the property is reported with the other prior-year assets on line 17.
  6. Carry the total to Schedule E — depreciation is a rental expense on Schedule E, line 18, against the rent for that property.
  7. Treat improvements separately — each capitalized improvement starts its own 30-year (or 40-year) schedule when placed in service.
  8. Track the accumulated total — keep a running depreciation schedule; you will need it for recapture and for Form 8949 when you sell.
  9. Repair the past if needed — one wrong year: amend; two or more: Form 3115 with a section 481(a) adjustment.

If you're not American: what changes

The local tax is identical for any foreign owner. The country where the property sits taxes the rent under its own rules — with or without a building allowance — regardless of your passport. What is purely American is the home-country layer: MACRS, the ADS rule for property outside the US, the 30-year and 40-year periods, "allowed or allowable" recapture at 25%, and Form 3115.

If you're not a US person, none of this applies to you unless you become one — but the same type of question does. Check three things in your own country of tax residence: (1) whether it taxes foreign rental income at all and, if so, whether it allows a capital allowance or depreciation on an overseas building (the UK, for example, generally allows none on residential property; Germany and Canada have their own schedules); (2) whether any depreciation you claim at home is clawed back on sale, as it is in the US; and (3) how a treaty with the property's country allocates taxing rights on the rent and the eventual gain. And if you are a green-card holder or become a US tax resident while already owning a rental abroad, note that the US clock starts on your US placed-in-service date, with basis set under the conversion rules above — a point worth raising with an advisor before you move.

Is it worth getting this right in 2026?

Yes — and the cost of getting it right is small. The ADS rule is one of the few foreign-property rules with no judgment call in it: the building is abroad, so it is on 30 years (or 40), straight-line, mid-month, building only, no bonus, no §179. The owners who get hurt are the ones who never ask the question — either claiming a 27.5-year deduction the IRS will unwind, or claiming nothing and paying 25% recapture on deductions they never took. Set the basis and the land split up correctly in year one, claim the ADS amount every year, keep the schedule, and file a Form 3115 now if the past returns are wrong. Done that way, depreciation is what it should be: a reliable, predictable shelter for a meaningful slice of the rent, and no surprise at the closing table when you sell.

Frequently asked questions

How many years do you depreciate a foreign rental property?

A residential rental located outside the United States must be depreciated under ADS, straight-line over 30 years if placed in service after 31 December 2017, or 40 years if placed in service before 2018. Foreign nonresidential (commercial) property uses 40 years. The 27.5-year and 39-year GDS periods apply only to property used predominantly inside the US.

Why can't I use 27.5 years for my apartment abroad?

Because IRC §168(g)(1)(A) requires ADS for any tangible property used predominantly outside the United States, and the regulations treat property as such when it is physically located abroad for more than 50% of the year. A building abroad is there 100% of the year, so ADS is mandatory, not elective, and 27.5 years is an impermissible method that overstates the deduction every year.

Is depreciation on a foreign rental optional?

No. Under the allowed-or-allowable rule your basis is reduced by the depreciation you were entitled to claim even if you never claimed it, and that amount is taxed at sale as unrecaptured §1250 gain at up to 25%. Skipping it means no deduction now and a recapture bill later. If you missed it for two or more years, Form 3115 lets you claim the accumulated amount in one catch-up adjustment.

Do I depreciate the land under a foreign property?

No. Land is never depreciable. Split your cost between land and building by the ratio of their fair market values at purchase or, if unclear, the ratio in the local property-tax assessment. Only the building portion, converted to US dollars at the exchange rate on the purchase date, goes on the 30-year ADS schedule.

What about furniture and appliances in a furnished foreign rental?

They are also used predominantly outside the US and also fall under ADS. Publication 527 lists appliances, carpets and furniture used in rental property at 9 years under ADS (5 under GDS), office furniture at 10 years, and property with no class life at 12 years. Because ADS applies, bonus depreciation and §179 are not available.

What happens to the depreciation when I sell the foreign property?

Your adjusted basis is reduced by all depreciation allowed or allowable, which increases the gain. The part attributable to depreciation is unrecaptured §1250 gain taxed at a maximum 25% federal rate; the rest is long-term capital gain at 0%, 15% or 20%. Even if the property later became your home, the §121 exclusion does not cover gain attributable to depreciation claimed after 6 May 1997.
